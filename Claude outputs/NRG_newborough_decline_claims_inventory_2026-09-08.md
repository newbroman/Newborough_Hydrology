## Opposite / stable / rising claims found

- **Davy et al. 2010** (#5): explicitly reports a *recovery* — "Since 2000, annual rainfall has tended to be greater than the long-term average and flooding of some slacks has recurred" — i.e. the water table is described as having risen back after the late-1980s/early-1990s dry spell, attributed to rainfall, not forest removal (no felling of consequence had occurred by 2010).
- **NRW Forest Resource Plan respondent** (#7): "There has never been proof that the forest has had a detrimental effect on the water table…" — direct denial of the forest-decline hypothesis.
- **Save Newborough Forest / Hesketh 2009** (#9): frames climate, not the forest, as driver — implicitly arguing the water table would still be falling even without the pines, so felling is not an effective remedy.
- No source located that directly documents water-table **rise following felling** with data (the NRW 2016 hydrological trial that would test this is described by NRW itself, in the 2025 Forest Resource Plan engagement document, as still ongoing / not concluded).

## Citation-chain analysis

The available material does **not** show a clean, traceable chain running back to a single
long-term instrumental decline record. What actually exists:

1. **Ranwell (1959)** — genuinely primary: fortnightly dipwell readings at 17 sites, 1951–53.
   As the task brief anticipates, I found no evidence in any secondary source that this paper
   itself asserts a long-term *decline* — it is consistently used (where cited at all, e.g. by
   Stratford et al. 2013) for **descriptive baseline seasonal range**, not trend. I could not
   fetch the paper's own text directly (JSTOR/Wiley paywalled, not reachable) to confirm the
   70–100 cm range wording verbatim; this is quoted from the task brief and echoed in a
   secondary description ("water table fluctuations…typically around 70 cm") in the Stratford
   summary — these are not identical figures (70–100 cm range vs "typically around 70 cm"),
   which itself suggests imprecise downstream paraphrasing rather than a stable, careful chain.
2. **A distinct, later, independently-sourced strand** — Davy et al. (2010) and the two
   Wikipedia articles — describes a **1970s–80s (Davy: "1970s"; Wikipedia: "late seventies and
   eighties"/"mid-1990s") dry/low-water-table episode**. Davy et al. attribute this explicitly
   and only to **below-average rainfall**, citing it as "anecdotal evidence" and drawing a
   direct parallel to the same phenomenon at Braunton Burrows (i.e., a regional rainfall
   signal, not a Newborough-specific afforestation signal). The two Wikipedia articles describe
   what appears to be the same episode but disagree with each other on attribution: one gives no
   cause, the other names **afforestation-driven evapotranspiration** as the cause while also
   conceding regional dune systems generally were low due to rainfall. Neither Wikipedia article
   cites a source for its causal claim, so it is impossible from what I could access to tell
   whether the "afforestation" framing traces to Jennings (1990) (unreachable — see below),
   to Hill & Wallace, to CCW management-plan text, or is simply editorial synthesis.
3. **Jennings (1990)** — the MSc thesis the task names as a likely key source for an
   afforestation-attributed hydrological change claim — **I could not locate or access this
   thesis at all** (no Bangor repository record, no citing source gave a quotable extract).
   Given the task brief's framing ("changing hydrology ... afforestation"), it is plausible this
   thesis is the actual origin of the afforestation-attribution strand that Wikipedia echoes
   without citation, but I found **no direct evidence connecting them** — this is a gap, not a
   confirmed link.
4. **Hill & Wallace (1989)** — confirmed primary (1986 field survey vs pre-planting air
   photographs) but, from the abstract alone, concerns soil chemistry and vegetation, not water
   table. Cited by the NRW Abermenai–Aberffraw management plan, but for vegetation effects.
5. **Modelling/projection literature** (Clarke & Sanitwong Na Ayutthaya 2010, cited by both
   Stratford et al. 2013 and the Sands of LIFE report) is a **third, separate strand**: future
   climate-driven projections (1–3 m fall by 2080-ish horizon) for dune systems generally,
   explicitly ranked ahead of afforestation and sea-level rise as a driver. This is not a
   historical-decline claim and not Newborough-specific, but it is the most quantitatively
   explicit "decline" figure in the whole set — and it is a projection, not a measurement.
6. **Present-day institutional position (NRW, 2024–2025 Forest Resource Plan)**: NRW's own text
   states that a hydrological trial begun in 2016 to test forest impact on Warren hydrology is
   still running with data still being collected — i.e., the responsible statutory body has
   **not** published a resolved finding on whether the forest lowers the water table, despite
   decades of the claim circulating. This is the single clearest, most citable fact bearing on
   "is there actually a resolved primary evidence base" — and the answer, per NRW's own words, is no.

**Bottom line on the chain:** there does not appear to be one dominant, well-cited primary
measurement series that all later claims trace back to. Instead there are (a) Ranwell's early
1950s baseline (no decline claim), (b) an untraceable 1970s–90s "dry phase" narrative that
splits between a rainfall attribution (Davy 2010, sourced as "anecdotal") and an afforestation
attribution (Wikipedia, uncited), (c) forward-looking climate-change modelling unrelated to a
historical Newborough record, and (d) an NRW-run 2016 hydrological trial whose results, as of
the most recent document found (the 2025 Forest Resource Plan), have not been published. Jennings
(1990) — the thesis most likely to be the missing link for the afforestation-attribution strand —
could not be located or accessed in this research pass.
