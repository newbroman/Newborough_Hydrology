### The 1951–53 Record: Fifty-Four Years Before Calibration

The only observation of this aquifer older than the 1989–96 record is Ranwell's. Between February 1951 and August 1953 he read seventeen galvanised pipes across the open slacks of the Warren at least fortnightly, levelled each to Ordnance Datum in 1951 and re-surveyed the mobile-dune sites in 1953, and published the result as a seasonal range of 70–100 cm and a set of figures (Ranwell, 1959). He printed no table. Three of the series survive as plotted points (his Sites 1, 4 and 8) and seven more as monthly ranges, and these were recovered from the published figures to about a centimetre; the two figures are independent digitisations of the same readings and agree at the sites they share. The pipes stood in the Penlon and Clwt Gwlyb slacks and in the coastal slack south of them; none stood under the plantation, which was then being planted, and none stood in the slack of his Sites 2–15 for which he printed no series. Where the pipes stood is known to the slack and, on sloping ground, to a few metres: his site sketch is not metric, but his levelled heights agree with the modern terrain model to within the scatter of the placement (median offset 0.16 m, spread 0.14 m), and every site falls in the terrain basin the sketch draws it in.

Two tests follow, and they are different in kind. The first is the 1989–96 test taken thirty-eight years further back. Each of Ranwell's plotted sites is paired with the modern wells that share its terrain basin and lie within 300 m of it, and each paired well's own coefficients from the modern fit are driven by the RAF Valley record from 1930, with no refitting. Because the pipe and the well are not at the same point, the comparison is made after removing the mean level difference between them, and because a flooded slack reads at the ground, the modelled level is capped at the surface. What remains is a test of the dynamics alone: at the nearest paired well the correlation between Ranwell's monthly means and the hindcast is 0.74, 0.75 and 0.91 at the three sites, the seasonal range is reproduced to within a tenth of a metre at each, and the month of the 1951 minimum is the same in the observations and the model at all three. Across every same-basin pairing the correlation runs from 0.70 to 0.84 and the bias-removed efficiency from 0.32 to 0.80, the lower values belonging to the Penlon site, where the modelled 1951–52 winter rise overshoots readings that the pipe could not follow above the ground. The forcing can be checked over exactly these years, because Ranwell also published the rainfall at the Parc Mawr nursery: over his reading span it totals 0.97 of the RAF Valley figure, with a monthly correlation of 0.92. Coefficients fitted to 2005–2026 therefore reproduce the seasonal filling and draining of these slacks fifty-four years before the calibration window opens, in the last years before the forest closed.

The second test asks whether the level has moved, and it is the more delicate of the two. Ranwell's mean level at each of the eight printed sites, in metres above Ordnance Datum through his own levelling, is set against the modern mean water-table surface interpolated to that point from the wells around it, less the difference the climate of the two periods would produce on its own — the hindcast's own 1951–53 mean against its modern mean at the paired well, which is within 0.05 m of zero at every site. The comparison is not point to point, and its error budget says so: the leave-one-out error of the interpolated surface, the surface gradient times the positional uncertainty of the pipe, the difference between a reading mean and a mid-range mean, and the spread of Ranwell's datum against the terrain model, combined in quadrature, give per-site uncertainties of 0.23 to 0.37 m in the Penlon and Clwt Gwlyb slacks and about 0.7 m at the two coastal-slack sites, where the modern surface rests on a single well. Six of the eight sites stand within 0.3 m of their 1951–53 level; the two Clwt Gwlyb sites furthest from a modern well stand about 0.3 and 0.4 m lower, at 1.3 and 1.9 standard errors; and Site 8, at the seaward end of the coastal slack that Ranwell himself recorded as accreting, stands 1.3 m lower against one well 118 m away and is reported rather than explained. No site differs at two standard errors. Weighted by their uncertainties the eight sites give a change of −0.09 ± 0.10 m over the sixty-six years between the midpoints of the two records, with a scatter about that mean of 1.4 times what the error budget predicts; the six sites whose modern surface is well constrained give −0.07 ± 0.11 m.

The record therefore supports the reading the introduction gives it. At the open slacks Ranwell measured, the water table today stands within about a decimetre of where it stood in 1951–53, once climate is allowed for; a fall larger than about 0.3 m over the interval is excluded, and so is a rise larger than about 0.1 m. That bound is what "near-stationary" means here, and it is a bound, not a rate: two windows sixty-six years apart cannot say what happened between them, and the 1989–96 record shows that the intervening decades contained at least one excursion of several decimetres that has since reversed. Nor does the comparison reach the forest. No pipe stood under the canopy, and at the Clwt Gwlyb sites, which lie 120–200 m from the nearest forest wells, the drawdown the interception model of Section 4.9 predicts is of order 60–80 mm — inside this comparison's error, and so neither confirmed nor excluded by it. What the century does not contain, on the only measurements that exist, is the progressive regional decline the site's literature has repeated since the 1970s without a measurement to rest on.
