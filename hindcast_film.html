Newborough Warren under a century of weather

How wet the dune slacks would have been, month by month, from 1930 to 2026 — if the reserve as it stands today had lived through the weather of each of those years.

Only the warren is modelled, not the forest. Everything here is about the open dune slacks of Newborough Warren — the study area outlined on the map. The planted forest beside them is not part of the model and is not assessed; the absence of yellow and blue cells does not imply an absence of flooding, only that it has not been modelled. The map covers the warren study area only (307 hectares of slack floor); blank ground outside the outline means not assessed, not dry.

How to read the film

  • Yellow is wet floor: saturated ground, or a thin sheet of water hidden in the grass.
  • Blue is open water: pools you could see from the air.
  • The title gives the total of each across the study area, in hectares.
  • The line at the bottom is the water table — how far below or above the ground the groundwater sits, averaged over the reserve's monitoring wells. The red marker is where the film has got to; the clock runs at about a year a second.

How it was made

Three things joined together:

  • A weather-driven groundwater model. Since 2005 the reserve's dipwells have been read monthly, and from them a simple rule was learned for how the water table rises with rain and falls with evaporation and drainage. RAF Valley has kept weather records since 1930, so the rule can be run through the whole century, steered by nothing but the weather. Where the wells exist, the model's water table is corrected to match them.
  • Satellite pictures. Sentinel-2 has photographed the warren every few days since 2016 in 10 m squares. In the near-infrared, water is dark and grass is bright, so a wet slack floor is a dark patch. Counting the dark squares in 43 clear winter scenes and comparing with the water table gives two smooth curves — one for open water, one for wet floor.
  • Where the water goes. Each 10 m square has its own history in those pictures, so we know the level at which it usually turns dark. The film lights each square when the model reaches that level: where the colours appear was learned from the satellite, when is the model.

Please read this before watching

It is today's warren, not the warren of the time. The rules were learned from 2005–2026 and the ground is the 2023 survey, with Newborough Forest at its present size. The forest was only planted between 1947 and 1965, and a forest lowers the water table around it — in 1939 there was no forest. The film answers a what-if: what the reserve as it is now would have done under that weather, not what actually happened. It is an illustration of the area–level relationship, not a flood map.

The biggest floods go beyond anything measured

The wettest spring on record was 2021. In some months — 1939, 1959, 1961–62 and 2000–01 — the model pushes the water table higher than that, above the level the curves were fitted to. No satellite picture has seen the warren that wet, so those months are marked beyond the record: the totals are the curves run past the end of the data, and the rest of the slack floor turns pale orange — wetter than anything on record, where exactly we cannot say. The honest claim for those winters is that they were bigger than 2021, nothing finer.
