# <span id="anchor"></span>Limitations

## <span id="anchor-1"></span>Temporal resolution and conservative volatility estimates

The reliance on monthly-averaged groundwater levels mathematically dampens the event-scale fluctuations inherent to highly conductive unconfined aquifers. Because daily peaks and troughs of individual storm events are smoothed over a 30-day window, the derived water balance parameters are optimized for seasonal trend analysis rather than event-based flood routing. This is a standard constraint in historic groundwater modelling (Taylor and Alley, 2001), and means the model captures the overall volumetric water balance while inherently underestimating daily transient spikes. The assessment of seasonal amplitude presented here is therefore conservative: the actual event-scale fluctuation — and any periodic severing of the capillary fringe from the root zone — is likely more pronounced than the monthly averages depict.

## <span id="anchor-2"></span>**Thornthwaite PET and sensitivity of absolute thresholds**

The Thornthwaite method was selected for its compatibility with the low-cost replication objective (Section 3.9), and its systematic biases cancel in all cluster-to-cluster and before-versus-after comparisons because the same PET series is applied uniformly across all clusters and time periods (Section 3.1.2). However, the absolute values produced by the prediction equations and P\_flood thresholds are sensitive to the PET input. Thornthwaite PET overestimates evaporative demand by approximately 10–15% relative to Penman-Monteith in humid maritime climates (Donohue et al., 2010). Because the SSM's atmospheric draw term is β₂ × PET, a 15% reduction in PET would reduce the fitted β₂ coefficients proportionally, with the effect concentrated in the summer months when PET is largest. The P\_flood threshold — which depends on the winter recharge demand required to offset the preceding summer's atmospheric losses — would decrease, meaning the critical rainfall target would be easier to achieve than the current equations suggest. The scenario framework Δh estimates would be similarly affected: the absolute magnitude of forest management responses would be smaller if PET were lower, because the canopy interception and atmospheric draw perturbations are scaled against the PET baseline. The relative ranking of scenarios, the spatial pattern of responses, and the finding that forest management perturbations do not propagate to C1/C2 are all independent of the PET method because they depend on coefficient ratios and spatial structure rather than absolute magnitudes. Users of the P\_flood equations and scenario framework should therefore treat the absolute thresholds as conservative upper bounds on rainfall demand, consistent with the upper-bound interpretation applied to the WTF-derived specific yield estimates (Section 4.2.4).

## <span id="anchor-3"></span>The NW10 broadleaf comparison — a single uncontrolled well

The comparison of NW10 (broadleaf block, established 1993–1996) with adjacent Corsican pine wells represents the only direct observational evidence of species conversion effects within the monitoring network, but it is subject to several limitations that preclude a fully controlled comparison. NW10 is a single well in a single block; replication across multiple broadleaf wells and blocks would be required to establish a statistically robust species effect independent of local variation. The comparison wells (CEH2, CEH34) differ from NW10 in ground elevation, distance from the ridge, and drainage rate (NW10 β₃ = 0.043 vs C4 median 0.019), and whilst the direction of the summer minimum contrast is consistent across all comparators the absolute magnitude is sensitive to these positional differences. Most significantly, the broadleaf stand was characterized by a dense bramble understorey throughout most of the monitoring period, which maintained high ground-level evapotranspiration demand and confounded the tree canopy signal. The stand has only begun to approach a closed canopy configuration from approximately 2022, leaving fewer than four years of post-closure record. The conclusions drawn from NW10 should therefore be treated as indicative observations from a natural experiment rather than confirmed causal attributions, and will require validation against a longer post-closure record and, ideally, against additional broadleaf wells installed within the block.

## <span id="anchor-4"></span>Spatial analysis — absence of calibrated continuous-flow modelling

The spatial framework used in this study (Sections 3.8, 4.9) is a per-well SSM scenario aggregator interpolated by inverse-distance weighting, not a calibrated continuous-flow model. It is well-suited to the questions the paper addresses — how the per-well β parameterization aggregates spatially under scenarios, and whether the BACI-observed forest response propagates beyond the forest zone — but cannot represent cluster-to-cluster hydraulic coupling or the diffusive redistribution of localized recharge perturbations. Hydraulic conductivity is constrained by a single tracer test (K = 6.0 m/day; Betson et al., 2002) and aquifer thickness in the western interior is inferred from four borehole logs across 700 ha (Bristow, 2003). These are the same parameter-sparsity constraints that produced calibration difficulties in the Betson et al. (2002) MODFLOW model at this site. The priority field measurements required to enable a calibrated continuous-flow model are identified in Section 5.9.

## <span id="anchor-5"></span>BACI experiment — network design limitations

The clearfell zone and scraping sites were selected on different grounds: scraping was a targeted conservation intervention, while the clearfell was an experimental felling recommended by previous researchers and implemented as an operational forestry action. Neither was randomly assigned, which introduces the possibility that pre-existing site characteristics could confound the intervention signal. The monitoring network was not designed as a purpose-built propagation transect. The transect analysis in Section 4.6.7 (Figure 36) uses the available surveillance wells to test for an inland gradient in the post-felling signal and finds none, with post-felling step changes distributed uniformly from the plantation interior to the felling core. All 17 wells in the five-tier network have a minimum of eight years' pre-felling baseline (Section 3.5.4). Wells with shorter records (FE1–4, LIS1, NW8B) were excluded from the analysis. The network permits a test of whether clearfell-driven effects propagate into the adjacent warren, but does not provide fine-grained spatial resolution of any residual propagation signal at distances under 100 m from the clearfell edge, nor a purpose-built warren transect perpendicular to the clearfell boundary.

## <span id="anchor-6"></span>Coastal-erosion confound — first-order spatial control rather than time-lagged propagation

Both intervention analyses treat the coastal-erosion confound through covariates and control selection rather than through explicit time-lagged modelling. The clearfell ANCOVA includes an Easting × time interaction that captures average differential deterioration with proximity to the coast across the observation window, and the scraping analysis tracks coastal-driven drift through CEH4's CUSUM trajectory and through the synthetic-control and SSM-forward-residual robustness checks. Neither approach explicitly represents the multi-year to multi-decadal propagation timescale (L²Sy/K, Section 5.4.3) over which coastal erosion signals travel inland through the aquifer. The result is that both intervention analyses estimate the felling-attributable and scraping-attributable steps cleanly within the available analysis windows, but cannot fully separate the contemporaneous management signal from the time-integrated coastal-erosion signal arriving from earlier historical retreat.

A direct test of the spatial component of this confound is now provided by the network-scale gradient regression of Section 4.8.1, which fits the strip-aquifer reach and coast-edge deepening rate directly to the 21-year monthly panel and corroborates the clearfell ANCOVA's easting × time covariate against an independently fitted physics-based model (Section 4.6.3, Table 8). At the headline Forest Impact comparison the BACI's easting × time absorption matches the gradient model's prediction within sampling error, indicating that the first-order spatial control is doing what coastal-retreat physics says it should. The Edge and Climate-tier comparisons absorb additional non-coastal-retreat drift (Section 5.5), so the spatial covariate's role in those comparisons is broader than coastal-retreat correction alone. The remaining limitation is the absence of an explicit transient boundary-condition flow model, which would refine the time-lagged propagation estimates and resolve the residual covariate absorption beyond what the steady-state gradient model captures. Direct measurement of present-day coastal retreat rates at the south-western dune front remains a monitoring priority

## <span id="anchor-7"></span>Water balance residuals — modelling artefact versus physical flux

****The cluster-level water balance closes to within 2.5% of total losses at all five clusters under the displacement formulation (Table 4a). However, the water balance residual field (Section 4.9.7, Figure 57) shows that this near-closure is not spatially uniform: positive residuals concentrate along the northern forest margin and ridge flank, declining to near zero across the open dune and becoming weakly negative at some south-eastern margin wells. This spatial structure is real and reproducible but its physical interpretation is ambiguous. The residual could reflect a genuine unmodelled lateral flux (with the ridge aquifer as the leading geometric candidate), inadequacies of the linear lumped-parameter SSM in representing nonlinear dynamics, minor β coefficient sensitivity, or some combination. Direct stem-flow measurement at the site (Freeman, 2008) amounts to approximately 1% of incident rainfall — an order of magnitude too small to account for the residual — which excludes localized stem-flow concentration as a contributor. Two independent diagnostic tests on the SSM residuals (Supplementary Notes S5 and S6) returned null results for specific mechanistic attributions — the lag-distance test did not corroborate ridge transport, and the sunshine-hours test did not identify unmodelled evaporative demand. The depth-dependent PET diagnostic (Section 5.2) confirms that a real depth-coupling effect exists but is adequately absorbed by the standard SSM's fixed β₂ for operational purposes. Until the residual attribution is resolved through either a nonlinear or spatially explicit model, or through direct field measurement of the candidate source locations, any management strategy whose rationale depends on a specific mechanistic interpretation of the residual should be evaluated against this uncertainty.****

****One specific structural form of the candidate lateral flux warrants consideration, although it is a field-derived hypothesis that the present data cannot test directly. The bedrock ridge forming the northern boundary is not a simple monolith: field observation indicates folds and faults running along its length, crossed by step faults, such that parts of the ridge terminate in a stepped fashion — discernible in plan view in the DEM — with one termination immediately north-north-east of CEH14. This structure could support an along-ridge sand aquifer, bounded below by the ridge-valley floor, conveying water along the length of the ridge and discharging into the dune system at the structural terminations near the forest margin. Because such a conduit would deliver continuous lateral inflow rather than a transient pulse, it is consistent with the persistent, winter-phased character of the positive residual (Section 5.2.1), and it would not be expected to produce the distance-dependent transport lag whose absence is recorded by Supplementary Note S5; the S5 null therefore does not bear on it.****

****The per-well coefficient field is consistent with this reading, though not uniquely so. Apparent drainage β₃ is most strongly suppressed at the wells nearest the inferred discharge — negative at CEH14 and near-zero at CEH13 — recovering to open-forest values south-westward along the corridor toward Caernarfon Bay (Section 4.9.2, Figure 48; Section ****4.9.5). A head-correlated lateral inflow, fitted within a model that contains no inflow term, would be absorbed into β₃ with exactly this signature: drainage apparently weakest, or inverted, where the inflow is greatest. CEH14, the sole well at which the displacement formulation fails to capture the boundary condition at the ridge flank (Section 4.9.2), is on this reading the well where a lateral-inflow term absent from the SSM would be expected to dominate. The same β₃ gradient is, however, already accounted for in Section 4.9.5 by elevation and the substrate inferred to accompany it, and along this single south-westward transect distance from the ridge and ground elevation are strongly collinear; the conduit interpretation and the substrate interpretation make the same spatial prediction and cannot be separated by the hydrograph data. The hypothesis is therefore consistent with the observed coefficient structure without being isolable from it. Resolving it would require targeted investigation of the ridge — shallow boreholes or geophysical profiling along its length, with direct head-gradient measurement at the candidate discharge points near CEH14 — and is identified here as a priority for future work.****

## <span id="anchor-8"></span>**Topographic proxies, aeolian deflation, and bedrock causation**

****The strong spatial correlation between DEM-derived drainage pathways and the hydrogeological cluster boundaries confirms that surface topography acts as a reliable operational proxy for subsurface flow divides. However, this correlation does not imply direct causation: surface sand deposition patterns do not dictate groundwater divides. Both are co-determined by the underlying architecture of glacial till and bedrock ridges, where present. In coastal dune systems this structural relationship is mediated by aeolian deflation — wind erosion is physically limited by the capillary fringe, as cohesive damp sand resists aeolian transport (Bristow and Bailey, 2001; Hesp and Thom, 1990), and because the regional water table is governed by subsurface geology, that geology indirectly controls the baseline limits of surface erosion (Haitjema and Mitchell-Bruker, 2005). Surface topography at Newborough Warren is therefore a physical expression of subsurface hydrological structure, supporting the use of the DEM as a proxy for regional groundwater routing in the absence of high-resolution bedrock mapping.****

## <span id="anchor-9"></span>**MSL5 metric: application beyond its intended domain and t\_R-dependent window sensitivity**

The five-year mean spring water level (MSL5; van Willegen et al. 2025) was validated as an ecohydrological descriptor for dune slack vegetation communities at C1 and C2 open-dune wells. Its use in Section 4.9.8 as a network-wide change detection tool across all clusters reflects a deliberate methodological choice: shorter comparison windows — one to three years either side of each intervention — proved too unstable to yield interpretable results because the expected management signal is smaller than the interannual variability of spring water levels at this site. Following involvement in the van Willegen et al. (2025) research on the same network, MSL5 was recognized as the most stable available metric for a longer-window empirical comparison. The spring season was selected because it is the only season in which readings are reliably obtainable across the full network: summer levels frequently fall below dipwell reach at the deeper wells, and winter readings are subject to surface ponding that truncates the measurable maximum. Because this extends MSL5 beyond its original ecohydrological design, three validation checks were conducted to confirm the chosen window reflects the genuine secular signal rather than a window-specific artefact (Section 5.7.5), and a property of the metric that is relevant to other researchers applying it at sites with heterogeneous drainage architecture was identified.

The implicit assumption of the five-year average is that spring readings within the window are approximately independent — that interannual variability is genuinely suppressed by averaging. The degree to which this holds is set by the head-recession e-folding time t\_R = 1/β₃ (Section 4.9.3), which governs how long a spring anomaly persists. Persistence is graded across the network rather than binary: it is lowest at the fast-draining open-dune clusters C1 and C2 (t\_R ≈ 10–14 months, retaining 28–41% of a head anomaly after one year), rises through C3 and C5 (≈ 20–22 months, 51–57%), and is highest at C4 Main Forest (mean t\_R ≈ 57 months, up to 127 months at the slowest wells). At C4 the five spring readings within an MSL5 window are heavily autocorrelated through drainage memory — a single anomalous spring retains 76% of its amplitude one year later, and over 85% at the slowest wells, contaminating subsequent readings throughout the window. The five-year mean is then dominated by the position of any anomalous spring within the window rather than representing a genuine multi-year average. C4 and C5 do not host the dune slack communities for which MSL5 was designed, so this limitation does not affect the metric's ecohydrological validity at those clusters. However, even within the intended open-dune application, the slower C3 wells (CEH9, CEH39, CEH21; t\_R 24–32 months, retaining 60–68% after one year) are borderline: MSL5 windows spanning a major wet or dry spring will carry a residual anomaly into subsequent readings at these wells that would not affect faster C2 wells in the same window.

The practical consequence at this site is demonstrated by the anomalously wet 2024 spring, which produces an artefactual +107 mm apparent gain at C4 Forest Control wells in the window ending 2025 that disappears entirely when the window is shifted to exclude 2024 (Section 5.7.5). Researchers applying MSL5 at sites with heterogeneous drainage architecture (t\_R = 1/β₃) are advised to apply three checks before attributing spatial MSL5 patterns to management or structural change. First, compute the head-recession e-folding time t\_R = 1/β₃ per well from the SSM fit (Section 4.9.3): a well retains ≈ exp(−12/t\_R) of a spring anomaly one year later, so persistence rises smoothly with t\_R rather than switching on at a single threshold. Wells retaining more than about one-third after a year (t\_R ≳ 12 months) should be treated as window-sensitive, and those retaining more than ~70% (t\_R ≳ 35 months, as at C4 here) as severely so.

The storage–drainage index τ = Sy/β₃ provides a single integrated descriptor that combines the drainage timescale t\_R with the storage capacity Sy, and therefore characterizes not only the degree of window sensitivity but also the physical mechanism behind it. A high-τ well is window-sensitive primarily because its large Sy relative to β₃ means that a spring anomaly — once imposed — is released only slowly; the same large Sy also means that the well integrates climate forcing over a longer period, making it a better recorder of multi-year trends but a poorer discriminator of individual-year management signals. The practical recommendation is therefore to weight MSL5 comparisons by t\_R rather than treating all wells equally: wells with t\_R &lt; 12 months can be treated as approximately independent across years within the window, those with 12 ≤ t\_R &lt; 35 months should be flagged as window-sensitive, and those with t\_R ≥ 35 months should be excluded from cross-cluster MSL5 comparisons or presented with an explicit caveat that their window-mean reflects drainage memory as much as secular trend.

Second, repeat the MSL5 comparison with an alternative window that excludes any anomalous spring — identified as a spring with network-mean depth more than one standard deviation from the long-run mean — and test whether the spatial pattern is stable; if the pattern changes materially between windows, the τ amplification effect is active. Third, test whether the chosen window's spatial pattern correlates with an independent long-run trend metric such as the per-well summer minimum slope: convergence confirms that the window reflects the genuine secular signal rather than a window-specific artefact. At this site, the 2017→2023 window passes all three checks (Section 5.7.5).
