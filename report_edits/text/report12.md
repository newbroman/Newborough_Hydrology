# <span id="anchor"></span>Conclusions

This study provides the first network-wide, multi-method quantitative characterization of groundwater behaviour at Newborough Warren, integrating 88 wells, 21 years of monitoring and an analytical pipeline of 49 steps across 17 phases. The principal conclusions are:

1.  **Five hydrogeological clusters govern site hydrology.** The eastern open dune (C1 Lake Edge, C2 Dune) responds rapidly to rainfall with high recharge sensitivity (β₁ = 4.58, 3.98); drainage is fastest at the lake-coupled C1 (β₃ = 0.090) and slower at the larger, archetypal C2 Dune (β₃ = 0.066, longer turnover). The Western Residual (C3) acts as a deep buffer (LCSC = 28.0%). The two Forest clusters (C4 Main Forest, C5 Coastal Forest) share elevated LCSC values (39.7%, 41.5%) reflecting canopy interception, but differ in setting: C4 occupies the elevated ridge flank (high β₂ = 2.55, weak β₃ = 0.020) and C5 the lower coastal ground (lower β₂ = 1.32, faster β₃ = 0.045). Three parameters estimated by distinct methods — drainage decay half-life (t½ = ln(2)/β₃), SSM forecasting gain (ΔNSE) and specific yield — place the five cluster centroids in distinct regions of diagnostic space (Figure 51), supporting the cluster structure independently of the Ward's linkage that defined it, though individual wells overlap appreciably within those regions. Within the forest zone, elevation alone explains 96% of the variance in β₂, identifying topographic position — and the substrate inferred to accompany it — as the major control on the atmospheric draw coefficient, with depth-coupling and other factors also acting. The plantation subsurface is not cored, so the C4/C5 substrate contrast is an interpretation of the elevation and coefficient evidence rather than a direct observation. The co-temporal amplification field (Section 4.9.8; Figure 65) independently corroborates this substrate reading: C4 amplifies the shared dry-to-wet spring climate swing to 1.72 times the network mean (range 1.38–2.35×), while the lake-edge cluster damps it to 0.61×. This over-response is the opposite of what a canopy-shielding mechanism would predict and is more consistently explained by two positional factors: thin saturated thickness over irregular bedrock, and the forest's occupation of the hydraulic high of the aquifer, furthest from any lateral discharge boundary — lake, coast or estuary — and therefore without the constant-head damping that buffers the lake-edge and coastal clusters in both wet and dry years.

2.  **Substrate, not canopy, controls the forest atmospheric-draw coefficient. **Of the two forested clusters only C5 (β₂ = 1.32) falls below the open-dune range (C2: 1.77; C3: 1.85); C4's β₂ (2.55) is genuinely elevated. An identifiability diagnostic finds the C4 drainage coefficient cleanly resolved, on the least collinear drainage predictor in the network, so the elevated β₂ is not an artefact of a degenerate β₂–β₃ pair. It is attributed instead to position and substrate: β₂ measures head response per unit atmospheric demand, and therefore rises where drainable storage is small. Whether the canopy suppresses summer atmospheric draw cannot be stated as a finding on the present evidence — the amplification result, the coastal-retreat confound at C5, and the imprecision of the seasonal clearfell comparison all leave it open.

3.  **Summer minimum depth, not winter rainfall, is the dominant control on dune slack ecological viability.** Antecedent summer minimum is the primary predictor of winter flooding potential in the Western Residual and Forest clusters, with winter rainfall non-significant (Table 12). The entire ecological gradient from SD15b wet slack to SD16 dry slack spans only 37 cm of summer minimum depth (Curreli et al., 2013). Any management intervention must be evaluated on its summer minimum effect.

4.  **State-space modelling transforms forecast skill.** The median per-well iterative NSE improvement of +0.53 over the traditional linear model — with only 1 of 66 SSM wells producing a negative iterative NSE compared with 22 of 66 for the TLM — indicates that explicit parameterization of head-dependent drainage memory is essential for long-range prediction in this aquifer.

5.  **The spring-level metric is externally validated against vegetation, and the state-space coefficients encode ecohydrological state. **MSL5 reproduces the published spring levels of van Willegen et al. (2025) to within ~11 mm and predicts their co-located Ellenberg-F between wells (r = 0.83, n = 18). An equilibrium wetness index obtained by inverting the SSM drainage term is statistically indistinguishable from MSL5 as an Ellenberg-F predictor (Williams p = 0.81) and, calibrated onto the MSL5 scale, reconstructs open-dune spring levels to 119 mm RMSE, generalizing beyond the vegetation-calibrated set — demonstrating that dynamics-only coefficients carry ecohydrological signal. Its prediction of vegetation moisture is independent of MSL5 — computed from the coefficients and climate alone — and so provides external ecological validation of the state-space model; its reconstruction of MSL5, being calibrated on that metric, is a usability diagnostic rather than an independent result. It is a supporting diagnostic scoped to the open dune (unreliable in the forest), advantageous mainly in needing a shorter record, so its value is transferability to other sites rather than local gap-filling.

6.  **Dune scraping delivers a measurable but spatially and temporally limited benefit whose placement within the drainage system is critical.** Three independent methods identify a scraping benefit at CEH36 in the same direction (raw BACI +0.129 m, synthetic control +0.137 m, SSM forward residual +0.081 m), with a paired summer minimum shift of +195 mm (p = 0.004) — more than half the SD15b–SD16 ecological gradient. The benefit is geometric (permanent surface lowering) rather than a change in aquifer dynamics, retaining approximately 68% of the scrape depth as a long-term benefit. Scraping operates through the same physical mechanism as coastal erosion — lowering ground elevation to increase the displacement head and drainage gradient — and its spatial placement determines whether it complements or exacerbates the existing coastal drainage regime. CEH21's limited response to the October 2023 intervention indicates that position within the coastal drainage pathway is a significant determinant of scraping efficacy. Priority targets are the C1/C2/C3 transitional wells where the aquifer base is stable and P\_flood thresholds remain achievable (rainfall multiplier λ &lt; 1.5); the per-well achievability categorization is mapped in Figure 73 (Section 5.9)

7.  **The clearfell experiment produced a statistically significant mean water table recovery against the Forest control.** The three-counterfactual ANCOVA-BACI found a significant clearfell step of +120 mm at the Impact well (WMC3; p &lt; 0.001). At the Edge tier the step was +33 mm and not statistically distinguishable from zero (p = 0.19); the measurable clearfell recovery is localized at the felled well rather than expressed across the surrounding edge of the compartment. The recovery is concentrated in the mean monthly water level rather than in summer minima: the summer minima analysis returned no significant effect at the felled wells, whose summer minima moved in parallel with the unfelled Forest controls. Synthetic extension of the FE wells within the felled compartment returns a clearfell step of +85 mm (p &lt; 0.001) against the Forest control, consistent in direction and magnitude with the Impact-well result. The corrected scenario projections predict small positive net annual responses to clearfell and thinning at the forest clusters (clearfell: C4 +8.1 mm w.e./month, C5 +10.4 mm w.e./month) and a small net annual effect under broadleaf conversion (C4 −0.7 mm, C5 +1.6 mm) — all substantially smaller than climate-driven changes over the same horizon. The +120 mm Impact-well clearfell step is independently corroborated by the network-scale coastal-retreat gradient model (Section 4.6.3, Table 8), which shows that the ANCOVA's easting × time covariate absorbs approximately the differential coastal-retreat signal that physics predicts (z = −1.16 between BACI absorption and model prediction for the Forest Impact comparison). An empirical MSL5 comparison (window end 2017 vs window end 2023) shows no spatially distinct clearfell signal above the ±25 mm noise threshold in five-year spring means, consistent with the BACI finding that the recovery is concentrated in the monthly-mean water level rather than in spring peaks. The monthly-mean ANCOVA result (+120 mm, p &lt; 0.001) is the robust clearfell estimate; the MSL5 comparison reflects the secular deepening trend rather than the management response.

8.  **Recharge sensitivity (β₁) is declining site-wide, independently of any management intervention.** The network-mean β₁ fell from 2.81 to 2.65 between the pre-fell and post-fell eras (Section 4.6.6; Table 11), a reduction of 5.6%, with the unfelled Climate Control tier — five wells distant from both the clearfell footprint and the coastal margin — declining by 3.8% (four of five wells declining) and supplying the decisive evidence for management-independence. This decline is the strongest candidate mechanism for the observed summer minimum deterioration across the network, and it affects felled and unfelled wells equally. The network-wide decline in the SSM water balance residual α between eras, visible even at open-dune Climate Control wells distant from the forest and coast, indicates that changed antecedent storage state between the two eras is a confound in the era-split coefficient analysis; the post-felling β₃ decline at Forest Control wells should be treated as indicative pending direct hydraulic corroboration. The monitoring evidence therefore characterizes canopy removal as a winter-recharge intervention: it raised mean monthly water tables at the felled wells, but its effect on summer minimum depth is set by this site-wide β₁ decline that operates independently of canopy cover.

9.  Forest management perturbations do not propagate to the open dune clusters where ecological need is greatest. The drawdown propagation analysis (Figure 50) shows that the steady-state head perturbation from the existing 75-year-old plantation is negligible (≤1 mm) at all C1 and eastern C2 wells. The BACI transect analysis shows no felling-specific spatial gradient. The operational domain for direct hydrological intervention is the C1/C2 zone where P\_flood thresholds remain achievable.

10. WTF-derived specific yields are broadly convergent once canopy interception is corrected. C4's corrected estimate (0.248) falls within the open dune range (0.210–0.325) and the interpolated yield surface shows no discontinuity at the cluster margins; C5's (0.321) sits at the upper edge but is constrained by the plausibility clip and is only weakly corroborative. The plantation's distinctive β-coefficient signature therefore derives largely from canopy boundary conditions at the surface rather than from differences in subsurface storage architecture.

11. **The water balance closes to within 2.5% under the displacement formulation, and the per-well residual field shows no coherent spatial structure.** Residuals are balanced about zero, uncorrelated with position on either axis, and show no forest-margin or ridge-flank concentration; three isolated wells exceed +0.02 m/month, none of them in the forest. Two independent diagnostic tests for a ridge-derived lateral input — a rainfall–residual lag-distance test and a sunshine-hours test for unmodelled evaporative demand — returned null results, consistent with the residual field itself.

12. **C5 Coastal Forest shows the steepest decline in the network in both summer (−0.038 m yr⁻¹, p = 0.003) and winter.** At 3.9 times the summer decline rate of C1 — the only other cluster with a statistically significant summer trend — the magnitude of C5's divergence argues against a purely climate-driven explanation, since all clusters share the same climate forcing. Three candidate explanations — amplified head response per unit climate forcing due to thin saturated thickness at the coastal margin, coastal boundary retreat, and progressive canopy maturation — are not mutually exclusive, and the scale of the divergence is reinforced by a parallel post-2018 collapse in seasonal amplitude (Section 4.2.1) indicating that recharge peaks are no longer reaching their pre-2018 heights. A network-scale non-linear panel regression on perpendicular distance to the eroding Caernarfon Bay shoreline ****(Section 4.8.2; Table 16 and Figure 4****1****)**** attributes approximately 41% ****(n = 1 in the forest-free panel; the direction of the result is robust but the magnitude is fragile)**** of C5's exceptional summer-minimum decline to a coastal-retreat gradient (coast-edge anomaly −29.0 mm yr⁻¹ decaying linearly to climate background by 894 m inland; ΔAIC favouring the linear-capped over the exponential decay form). Progressive coastal boundary retreat is therefore quantified as a substantial but partial contributor to the C5 anomaly; the remaining −16 mm yr⁻¹ residual at C5 is consistent with the other two candidate mechanisms operating in parallel. Direct measurement of present-day coastal retreat rates and continued monitoring of the CUSUM trajectory remain a priority. Monthly decomposition of the RAF Valley PET record shows significant increases in May (+0.066 mm month⁻¹ yr⁻¹, p = 0.002), June (+0.047, p = 0.030) and July (+0.062, p = 0.006), providing an independent physical mechanism for the site-wide secular deepening operating through intensified early-summer atmospheric demand.

13. **The intervention window is finite.** A bootstrap of the C1 summer-minimum linear trend places the median crossing year of the SD16 dry-slack viability threshold at 2022, with a 90% confidence interval of 2017–2042 (Section 4.10.1; Figure 70). The cluster is no longer approaching SD16 but oscillating across it: seven of the twenty observed summer minima have exceeded the SD16 depth, including four of the eight most recent years. The wide confidence interval reflects substantial year-to-year variability in summer minima against a shallow trend slope, not a precise threshold; the operational reading is that the intervention window is no longer measured from a future crossing event but from the present, with each successive dry summer landing further past SD16. The analytical framework, prediction equations and interactive tools developed in this study provide the operational basis for evidence-based management decisions within that window.

14. **Progressive coastal boundary retreat is an independent and potentially accelerating threat whose full consequences are lagged.** The convergent deterioration of two spatially separated control wells (CEH4 and CEH22), independently of any management action, indicates that the aquifer is losing volume at its seaward edge. The aquifer's hydraulic diffusivity means that the coastal erosion signal visible in the current CUSUM trajectories represents erosion that occurred years earlier; if the rate of retreat is accelerating, as CEH22's trajectory from 2021 suggests, the full hydrological consequences have not yet propagated to interior wells. The network-scale gradient regression of Section 4.8.1 quantifies the steady-state coastal-retreat signal directly from 21 years of monitoring data: the coast-edge water-table anomaly above climate is approximately −29.0 mm yr⁻¹, decaying to zero over an inland reach of 894 m. C5 Coastal Forest sits within the inner third of this gradient zone; C4, C1 and C2 lie beyond the fitted reach. Direct measurement of present-day coastal retreat rates and continued monitoring of inland propagation through the CEH4 and CEH22 CUSUM trajectories remain a priority.
