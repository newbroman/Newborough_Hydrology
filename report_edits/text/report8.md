## <span id="anchor-1"></span>Data Sources and Preparation

All analyses were implemented as a numbered Python pipeline orchestrated by run\_analysis.py. The pipeline comprises 49 registered steps across 17 phases. Those steps are classified two independent ways. By tier: 39 analytical, four display/utility (Script 26c, MSL5 report-format figures; Script 09f, the management-effects synthesis figure; Script 09g, the mechanism-diagram figure suite; and Script 27, the greyscale figure-conversion utility), and six diagnostic. By execution: 46 run in a default pass, and three (Scripts 24b, 31 and 31b) only behind the --with-supplementary switch. The two classifications each account for the same 49 steps and are not additive with one another; current values are written to outputs/pipeline\_manifest.json on every run.

-   **Phases 1–11** produce the analytical chain presented in this report — clustering, state-space modelling, intervention analysis, forecasting, water balance, scenario evaluation and the network-scale coastal-retreat gradient regression.
-   **Phase 12** contains supplementary residual diagnostics (Scripts 22–24).
-   **Phase 13** runs the van Willegen et al. (2025) mean-spring-water-level analyses — an observational five-year aggregation (Script 26), a companion UKCP18 climate projection (Section 3.7.5, Script 26b), and the report-format MSL5 figures for §4.8.4 and §4.10.1 (Script 26c).
-   **Phase 14** contains the cluster framework diagnostics: the C3 detrend check (Script 28, validating the aquifer-architecture framing of §5.1), the within-C3 variance attribution (Script 29, characterizing the spatial structure within C3), and the C4 constrained-β₃ triangulation sensitivity (Script 30, step 35, which assesses the identifiability of the C4 Main Forest drainage coefficient and is discussed in Section 5.2.4).
-   **Phase 15** produces the observed differential-change, climate-envelope and driver-validation analyses: the secular differential water-table drift map (Script 32, Figure 63), the climate-swing amplification and dry-year floor maps (Script 33, Figures 65 and 66), the per-well climate-sensitivity coefficient (Script 35; Section 5.7.5), the absolute climate-removed secular-trend map (Script 36, Figure 64), the per-driver scale-factor validation of the modelled driver fields against that trend (Script 37; Section 3.8.5, Figure 67), and the comparative driver-footing analysis (Script 37b; Section 3.8.5, Figure 68).
-   **Phase 16** runs the MSL5 two-window sensitivity demonstration (Script 34, Section 5.7.5) and the coast-to-inland transect estimate of the coastal-retreat rate (Script 38, Section 4.8.3) by default, alongside a supplementary opt-in remainder — the cluster-stratified residual climatology (Script 24b), an independent k = 5 partition validation (Script 31), and the cluster separation-versus-recoverability check (Script 31b).
-   **Phase 17** is a publication-ready greyscale figure-conversion utility (Script 27) — a post-processing step rather than an analytical one.

Two further post-review scripts were inserted into earlier phases: Script 11c (P\_flood achievability map, Phase 3) and Script 14b (bootstrap year-of-crossing, Phase 4). Steps 9 and 10 each invoke modular sub-script suites — the scraping intervention analysis (sub-scripts 09a–09e) and the clearfell BACI analysis (sub-scripts 10a–10l). Each script reads from and writes to a shared outputs/ directory; the public repository at <https://github.com/newbroman/Newborough_Hydrology> contains the full source tree and pipeline documentation, and all outputs are reproducible by running the pipeline in sequence.

### **Groundwater Records**

Groundwater level data were obtained from the Newborough Warren dipwell network. After quality control and exclusions (described below), 88 wells entered the classified network — 66 in the reference network and 22 in the extended network. Field measurements record depth to water below the top of each standpipe as a positive number in metres; water standing above the standpipe top is recorded as a negative value. Records span April 2005 to February 2026. Raw records were quality-controlled by removing values exceeding 4.0 m below standpipe top as physically implausible for this dune system, and assigned to the appropriate calendar month by field convention: readings taken on or before the 15th of a month are assigned to the previous month (reflecting the field practice of measuring at the start of the month to characterize the preceding month's conditions), while readings on the 16th or later are assigned to the current month. In general most readings were taken one or two days from the month's end. Where multiple readings fall within the same month after assignment, they are averaged to produce a single monthly mean; this applies to a small number of readings in 2005–06. Gaps of up to three consecutive months were filled by time-based linear interpolation; longer gaps were retained as missing values. All monthly data are indexed by year and month (e.g. July 2007); where date stamps appear in tabular outputs with a day suffix (e.g. 2007-07-01), the -01 day component is a pandas formatting convention and does not denote the first of the month — the record represents the complete calendar month, with the water-level reading taken at the end of that month. Monthly rainfall and Thornthwaite PET totals from RAF Valley were paired to the same calendar month as the water level they accompany. The monthly change in water level (Δh) is thus explained by the climate forcing that acted during that month, with drainage driven by the water table position at the end of the preceding month.

A reference network of 66 wells, each with more than 100 months of valid record extending to February 2026, formed the basis of the clustering and state-space modelling analyses. Eight wells meeting the record-length criterion were excluded from the reference network prior to analysis. Wells within the clearfell management footprint (FE1, FE2, FE3, FE4 and LIS1; Section 4.6) were held out on the grounds that removal of canopy interception following the 2017 felling might have introduced a non-stationary upward drift in their water tables that the time-invariant state-space formulation is not designed to represent. These wells were subsequently excluded from the clearfell BACI analysis (Section 4.6) on the grounds of insufficient pre-felling baseline (Section 3.5.4) and contribute to the extended-network Pearson affinity classification (Section 4.3). One lake-surface well (Llyn Rhos-Ddu) was excluded because it reads a near-fixed head body rather than a water table and therefore violates the storage-decay assumptions of the state-space model. Two further wells (CEH3, CEH22) were excluded on clustering grounds described in Section 3.2.3 as they showed drift attributable to coastal erosion. Seven of these eight reference-network exclusions remain in the extended network for per-well analysis; Llyn Rhos-Ddu is excluded from both networks, as its lake-stage signal is not interpretable as a groundwater response.

One additional well, PDFS, was excluded from both networks. Its hydrograph carries a tidal-influence signature that makes it unrepresentative of water-table behaviour — the same exclusion principle applied to CEH3 and CEH22 — and its record is too short to meet the minimum-length criterion for extended-network inclusion, so it could not be retained for per-well analysis in the way the tidally-affected reference-network exclusions are. The classified network therefore comprises 88 wells: 66 in the reference network and 22 in the extended network.

Each row is a single well: Figure 3a shows the 66 reference wells in cluster colour, and Figure 3b the 22 extended-network wells together with the excluded study-area dipwells and the Llyn Rhos-Ddu lake gauge as a presence-only band. CEH36, which received scraping management at a single point during the monitoring record (Section 4.5), was retained as a reference-network member on the grounds that scraping produced a one-time baseline shift rather than ongoing trend, and its post-intervention response remains consistent with its cluster mates. Wells not meeting the reference-network criteria for reasons of insufficient record length were assigned to the extended network used in the Pearson affinity analysis to extend the hydrogeological classification beyond the reference network boundary. Dipwells are positioned across a range of geomorphological contexts including dune slack floors, slack margins, and inter-slack hollows; most but not all wells are sited within slacks. Notable exceptions include L1, which is positioned adjacent to a fenced grazing experiment on slightly elevated ground; NW1, where the immediate surroundings have since been excavated; NW10 which is in an interslack area, that floods when the adjacent slack overtops; CEH31 and FE3 which sit in low dune ridges to the south of the clearfell area.

Ground surface elevations for all dipwells were derived from the Natural Resources Wales LiDAR Digital Terrain Model (2 m resolution, captured March 2023; Natural Resources Wales, 2023), obtained from the DataMapWales repository ([*https://datamap.gov.wales/maps/lidar-viewer/*](https://datamap.gov.wales/maps/lidar-viewer/)). The accuracy of the DEM-derived elevations was assessed by comparison against independently surveyed DGPS ground elevations at 61 wells (R² = 0.976, RMSE = 0.477 m, mean bias = +0.264 m). The bulk of this RMSE is attributable to six outlier wells where discrepancies exceeded 0.5 m; five are located within the Corsican pine plantation, where satellite signal attenuation under dense evergreen canopy is a known source of DGPS positional error, and the sixth is an open-ground well whose DGPS-recorded elevation matches that of a plantation well 245 m distant, strongly suggesting a recording error. Excluding these six wells, the RMSE falls to 0.231 m with a mean bias of +0.203 m, consistent with the NRW stated DTM accuracy of ±0.15 m RMSE on open ground plus a modest systematic offset attributable to residual vegetation returns. The DEM is therefore adopted as the primary elevation source throughout this study.

Field readings are dips from the pipe top, but the master workbook applies each well's surveyed upstand on export, so the modelled series is a signed height relative to the ground surface (level = upstand − dip): negative below ground, and positive where a slack is ponded. Water table elevation in metres above Ordnance Datum was therefore derived as maOD = ground elevation + level, which is the pipe-top conversion with the upstand already folded in, since maOD = pipe top − dip = (ground + upstand) − dip. Upstand heights range from −0.14 m (CEH28, a sunken casing) to +0.71 m (CEH2); they are retained for audit and reporting, but no further upstand term is applied anywhere in the analysis. The maOD head surfaces are used in the spatial groundwater analysis (Section 3.8) and the broadleaf comparison (Section 5.6.3); the temporal clustering, SSM parameterization, and BACI analyses use the ground-referenced records throughout.

For the cluster average hydrograph construction (Section 3.4.1), member-well series are averaged directly: they already share a common ground-surface datum (Section 3.1.1), so no correction is applied and between-well differences in pipe-top elevation cannot bias the cluster mean. The clustering and Pearson affinity analyses are in any case invariant to absolute depth, z-score standardization removing it. The datum convention is not, however, inert for the state-space model. A constant offset cancels in the first-difference term Δh but not in the level term h\_disp,prev, and because the model is fitted without an intercept there is nothing to absorb it. The effect falls almost entirely on β₃: across the 66 reference wells the coefficient shifts scale with each well's upstand (Pearson r = −0.846 for Δβ₃), while β₁ is near-invariant. Ratios of β₃ between clusters are unaffected, so comparative results do not depend on the choice; quantities computed from β₃ alone, such as the recession half-life, are conditional on it. This is why the ground-surface frame is applied once, at source, and never re-derived downstream. Network summary statistics and well characterization figures are presented in Section 4.1.2 and Figure 6.

### <span id="anchor-2"></span>Climate Data 

Monthly total precipitation and potential evapotranspiration were obtained from the RAF Valley Met Office climate station (SH 30691 75549), approximately 16 km north of the site. The station record extends from December 1930 to February 2026, with a mean annual rainfall of 890 mm over the well-record period (calculated from complete calendar years 2006–2025; the partial years at the start and end of the monitoring record are excluded from this summary statistic). A single missing monthly rainfall value in the historic record (June 1941) was retained as missing rather than substituted; it falls outside the well-record period and has no influence on any analysis.

Potential evapotranspiration was calculated using the Thornthwaite and Mather (1957) method with a day-length and month-length correction, applied to mean monthly air temperature derived as the mean of daily maximum and minimum. The method proceeds in three steps. First, the annual heat index I is computed as the sum of twelve monthly contributions:

**
$$I = {\sum\limits\_{m = 1}^{12}\left( \frac{T\_{m}}{5} \right)^{1.514}}$$
**

where Tₘ is mean monthly temperature clipped to zero for months at or below 0°C. Second, unadjusted monthly PET is calculated as:

**
$${\mathit{PET}\_{\mathit{unadj}} = 16}\times\left( \frac{10T}{I} \right)^{\alpha}$$
**

** **where: **
*α* = 6.75 × 10<sup>−7</sup>*I*<sup>3</sup> − 7.71 × 10<sup>−5</sup>*I*<sup>2</sup> + 1.792 × 10<sup>−2</sup>*I* + 0.49239
**

For months where mean temperature exceeds 26.5°C a high-temperature correction is applied
*P**E**T*<sub>*u**n**a**d**j*</sub> = −415.85 + 32.24*T* − 0.43*T*<sup>2</sup>;
this threshold is not reached at RAF Valley under the historic record but is retained for formal completeness. Months with mean temperature at or below zero are assigned PET = 0. Third, the unadjusted value is scaled by a day-length and month-length correction factor:

$${\mathit{PET} = \mathit{PET}\_{\mathit{unadj}}}\times K,\text{where}{K = \left( \frac{N}{12} \right)}\times\left( \frac{d}{30} \right)$$

where N is the maximum possible sunshine duration for the month (hours), calculated from site latitude (53.25°N, RAF Valley) using standard solar geometry, and d is the number of days in the month. This correction accounts for the fact that the original Thornthwaite equation was calibrated for a standard 30-day month and 12-hour photoperiod.

Thornthwaite PET is known to overestimate evaporative demand in humid maritime climates relative to physically-based methods such as Penman-Monteith (Donohue et al., 2010), and it was selected here because it requires only mean air temperature, consistent with the low-cost replication objective described in Section 3.9. Although forest recharge at Newborough is estimated to be 100–200 mm yr⁻¹ lower than open dune recharge (Stratford, 2006), these absolute differences do not affect the comparative conclusions of this study. Because the same PET series is applied uniformly across all clusters and time periods, systematic biases cancel in cluster-to-cluster and before-versus-after contrasts.

A centred cumulative water balance series (CWB; alternatively termed the centred cumulative water balance anomaly elsewhere in this report) was computed for the well-record period by summing monthly net balance (P − PET), with a mean-centring correction applied to remove the systematic long-term offset between precipitation and Thornthwaite PET inherent to this maritime climate; the corrected series oscillates around zero and directly reflects periods of cumulative surplus and deficit relative to the long-term mean. CWB is referenced as a covariate in the BACI ANCOVA models in Section 3.5.4 and in the climate sensitivity analysis in Section 4.6.3. The long-run detrending mean was 19.46 mm month⁻¹. The correspondence between this cumulative balance series and the unsmoothed network mean water level was assessed by linear regression at zero lag (R² = 0.515), confirming that interannual water table variability is substantially explained by antecedent climate forcing and providing the empirical motivation for the state-space modelling framework described in Section 3.4.

Alongside the precipitation and evapotranspiration series used in the modelling, the long-term temperature record is summarized as a warming diagnostic. A mean summer maximum temperature is computed for each year of the full RAF Valley record as the mean of the June, July and August monthly maximum temperatures, retaining only years in which all three summer months are recorded, and expressed as an anomaly relative to the pre-2013 record mean. An ordinary-least-squares linear trend is fitted to the annual anomaly series to characterize the direction and rate of change across the record. The anomaly series and fitted trend are presented in Section 4.1.1.

*Baseline climate figures and well network characterization statistics (record length, mean water level, seasonal amplitude, and monthly box plots) were produced by 00\_climate\_summary.py, with data preparation, quality control, gap-filling and the canonical geometry derivation implemented in 01\_data\_prep.py.*

## <span id="anchor-3"></span>Hierarchical Clustering 

To characterize the spatial structure of the aquifer and identify distinct zones of hydrological behaviour, agglomerative hierarchical clustering was applied to the continuous dipwell monitoring network.

### <span id="anchor-4"></span>Data Pre-processing and the Pearson Correlation Distance

Because the absolute elevation of the water table varies significantly across the site due to topography, analysing raw head data would artificially separate functionally identical slacks. To isolate the temporal dynamics—the "shape" of the hydrograph—each well's monthly time series was used directly in the correlation matrix; the correlation-based distance metric inherently removes mean and variance differences between series, making explicit z-score standardization unnecessary. (Note that explicit z-score standardization is applied at the cluster average construction stage of the subsequent Pearson affinity analysis, where it ensures that wells with different absolute ranges contribute equally to the cluster average time series.)

Pairwise dissimilarity between the time series was then computed using the Pearson correlation-based distance:
*d*<sub>*i*, *j*</sub>
:

*d*<sub>*i*, *j*</sub> = 1 − *r*<sub>*i*, *j*</sub>

where
*r*<sub>*i*, *j*</sub>
represents the Pearson correlation coefficient calculated over contemporaneous, overlapping temporal records for wells i and j. This specific metric translates the statistical correlation directly into the physical behaviour of the aquifer:

-   Synchronized Slacks (r=1): Where two dipwells rise and fall in perfect harmony, the distance metric becomes zero (d=0). The algorithm recognizes identical hydrogeological drivers and groups them tightly.
-   Unrelated Slacks (r≈0): Where one well responds to flashy winter storms and another is heavily buffered, the correlation approaches zero, yielding a distance of one (d=1), effectively pushing them apart in the clustering space.
-   Opposing Slacks (r=−1): Where wells exhibit inverse temporal behaviour, the distance maximizes at two (d=2).

By utilizing the Pearson correlation distance, the absolute topographic elevation of the sand dunes is stripped from the analysis. The algorithm is forced to group wells purely on the timing and shape of their temporal hydrographs, allowing deep aquifer wells to cluster together even if they are situated at vastly different physical elevations.

### <span id="anchor-5"></span>Agglomeration Strategy 

The dissimilarity matrix was processed using agglomerative hierarchical clustering, employing Ward’s minimum variance linkage **(Ward, 1963)**. At the initiation of the algorithm, each dipwell is treated as an individual cluster. The algorithm then iteratively merges the two clusters that result in the smallest possible increase in the total within-cluster variance, quantified mathematically as the Error Sum of Squares (ESS).

For any given cluster C containing n wells, the ESS is defined as the sum of the squared distances between each well's standardized time series (Xi) and the mean temporal signature (centroid,
$$\overline{x\_{C}}$$
) of that cluster:

$$\mathit{ESS}\_{C} = {\sum\limits\_{i\in C}\left\\ {x\_{i}-\overline{x\_{C}}} \right\\^{2}}$$

When evaluating the potential merge of two clusters, A and B, the algorithm calculates the resulting increase in total variance (ΔESS). This merge cost is computed algebraically as:

*Δ**E**S**S*(*A*,*B*) = *E**S**S*<sub>*A* ∪ *B*</sub> − (*E**S**S*<sub>*A*</sub>+*E**S**S*<sub>*B*</sub>)

Which simplifies to the objective function:

$$\Delta\mathit{ESS}{\left( {A,B} \right) = \frac{n\_{A}\cdot n\_{B}}{n\_{A} + n\_{B}}}\cdot\left| {{\overline{x}}\_{A}-{\overline{x}}\_{B}} \right|^{2}$$

where nA​ and nB​ represent the number of wells in clusters A and B, respectively.

This specific algebraic formulation is hydrogeologically essential for mapping a continuous dune aquifer. Because the spatial groundwater gradient transitions smoothly from the bedrock ridge to the foreshore, single-linkage (nearest-neighbour) algorithms frequently suffer from "chaining"—sequentially merging adjacent wells until the entire site collapses into a single, uninterpretable cluster.

Ward’s mathematical objective function actively resists this chaining effect. The
$$\frac{n\_{A}n\_{B}}{n\_{A} + n\_{B}}$$
term acts as a strict mathematical penalty against merging disproportionately large clusters or appending single outliers to established groups. Instead, it forces the algorithm to prioritize merging smaller, tightly correlated groups first, ensuring that the hydrographic signatures within any newly formed group remain highly synchronized. By penalizing variance, Ward's linkage imposes discrete analytical boundaries onto a continuous physical system, delineating the continuous sand matrix into compact, functionally distinct hydrogeological catchment zones (e.g., the rapid-draining Western Block and the near-perennial Eastern Block).

### <span id="anchor-6"></span>Cluster Determination and Robustness Assessment

The optimal number of clusters (k) was evaluated iteratively for k = 2 to k = 10 using three complementary metrics: the elbow method applied to Ward's merge distances, the mean silhouette coefficient (Yuan and Yang, 2019), and the Calinski–Harabasz index (Caliński and Harabasz, 1974). Silhouette and Calinski–Harabasz both peaked at k = 2, reflecting a fundamental binary split between the shallow western and deeper eastern aquifer responses. However, selecting k = 2 provides insufficient resolution to distinguish the geomorphological units relevant to conservation management. Rather than selecting k on a single metric, the partition was evaluated across a range of candidate values using bootstrap stability analysis (Hennig, 2007) to quantify how sensitive cluster assignments are to the particular set of wells observed.

For each candidate k in {4, 5, 6, 7}, the 66-well reference network was resampled with replacement 1000 times. At each resample, Ward's linkage was re-applied and the resulting assignments compared with the reference (full-sample) partition. For every pair of wells, the fraction of resamples in which both wells were assigned to the same cluster was recorded as a pairwise co-assignment probability; the median of these probabilities across a well's reference-cluster partners provides a per-well stability score. A well scoring 1.0 co-assigns with the same neighbours in every resample; lower scores indicate sensitivity to the composition of the network.

At k = 5, four of the five clusters exhibited median per-well stability exceeding 0.93: the Main Forest cluster (stability 1.00), the Coastal Forest cluster (0.99), the Lake Edge cluster (0.98), and the Dune cluster (0.93). The fifth cluster, Western Residual, exhibited moderate stability (0.50), reflecting the landscape heterogeneity of this group rather than a failure of the partition — at higher k this group fragments into sub-clusters with individually poor stability, confirming that k = 5 represents the finest resolution at which Ward's linkage produces behaviourally coherent groups. The final solution of k = 5 was therefore selected as the finest partition at which the majority of clusters are demonstrably robust under resampling, while providing sufficient resolution to distinguish the principal geomorphological units.

The silhouette coefficient at k = 5 is 0.40. This moderate value is consistent with the physical reality of a contiguous, highly permeable sand aquifer where cluster boundaries are inherently gradational. The bootstrap stability analysis provides a more informative assessment of partition quality than the silhouette score alone, because it directly quantifies whether individual well assignments are reproducible rather than measuring only average cluster separation.

Two wells — CEH3 and CEH22 — were excluded from the reference network prior to clustering because Ward's linkage consistently identified them as singleton outliers at every k from 4 to 9, resistant to grouping with any behavioural cluster. Both are low-elevation coastal wells (ground elevation 3.3–4.5 m) whose correlation structure is consistent with tidal-signal contamination superimposed on the climate-driven response that the clustering and subsequent state-space modelling are designed to characterize. Their exclusion follows the same principle applied to the FE/LIS clearfell wells and Llyn Rhos-Ddu (Section 3.1.1): wells whose behaviour violates the stationarity or single-mechanism assumptions of the analytical framework are retained in the extended network for per-well analysis but excluded from the reference partition. Including either well distorts the Ward's tree at lower k values — CEH3 suppresses the Lake–Dune separation at k = 4, and CEH22 appears as a persistent singleton from k = 5 onward — so their exclusion is both physically motivated and algorithmically necessary.

A converse case is CEH11, which is assigned to C1 (Lake Edge) on the basis of hydrograph similarity but is geographically isolated from the other six C1 members. The remaining C1 wells occupy ground at 8.6–10.9 m AOD around Llyn Rhos-Ddu in the northern part of the site; CEH11 sits at 3.6 m AOD on the south-eastern coast near the Menai Strait, approximately 1.6 km from the nearest C1 well (CEH27). Its damped, low-amplitude seasonal hydrograph — the signature that drives its C1 assignment — likely reflects proximity to the Menai Strait tidal boundary rather than to Llyn Rhos-Ddu: a different constant-head source producing a similar buffered water-table response. The cluster assignment is retained because the statistical grouping is hydrograph-based and CEH11's behaviour is genuinely C1-like, but the different boundary condition means its mechanistic coefficients should not be interpreted alongside the lake-edge wells without qualification. This distinction is relevant to the β₂–elevation analysis in Section 4.3, where CEH11's inclusion extends the C1 elevation range from 8.6–10.9 m down to 3.6 m without contributing to the lake-proximity gradient that governs the other members.

### <span id="anchor-7"></span>Seasonal Amplitude as a Secondary Cluster Descriptor

The Pearson correlation distance described in Section 3.2.1 is scale-invariant: because Pearson correlation is unchanged by linear rescaling, two wells whose hydrographs rise and fall in synchrony will cluster together regardless of whether one exhibits a seasonal range of 0.5 m and the other 2.0 m. This property is desirable for identifying shared drainage mechanisms, but it means that wells within a single cluster may differ substantially in response magnitude — reflecting local differences in specific yield, proximity to hydrogeological boundaries, or depth to an impermeable layer. To characterize each cluster on this second, orthogonal axis, a suite of seasonal amplitude descriptors was computed as a supplementary characterization alongside the pattern-coherence statistics reported in Section 4.2.

The primary amplitude metric is the difference between the 90th and 10th percentiles of each well's monthly water-level series (p90 − p10), expressed in metres. This inter-decile range captures a representative seasonal swing while remaining robust to single-month outliers. It was computed for each well over three windows: the full record, the pre-2018 period (prior to the December 2017 clearfell event in the FE compartment), and the post-2018 period. Wells with fewer than 24 monthly observations in a given window were excluded from that window's calculation. Cluster-level statistics are reported as the median of per-well amplitudes within each cluster, preserving the visibility of within-cluster spread that would be suppressed by computing a single amplitude on the cluster-mean hydrograph.

Because the post-2018 window contains a higher proportion of drought summers than the pre-2018 window, a climate-normalized variant was also computed. Monthly observations from empirically identified drought summers (Jun–Sep rainfall total at least one standard deviation below the long-term mean of the RAF Valley climatology against the pre-monitoring baseline) were removed from both windows before recomputing p90 − p10. This normalization is reported alongside the raw values as a sensitivity check; where the two agree in direction and approximate magnitude, the raw value is taken as primary. The normalization predominantly affects the post-2018 window, since only wells whose records extend back to 2005 are modified in the pre-2018 window.

Amplitude results are presented in Section 4.2 as a secondary descriptor alongside pattern-coherence and bootstrap stability. Where the ratio of maximum to minimum per-well amplitude within a cluster approaches or exceeds 2×, the cluster is noted as amplitude-heterogeneous; this is relevant to the interpretation of cluster-mean β coefficients from the state-space model (Section 3.4), which represent an average response magnitude across wells whose individual magnitudes may differ meaningfully.

## <span id="anchor-8"></span>Checking Cluster Allocation with Pearson Affinity Analysis

While hierarchical clustering imposes discrete boundaries, the physical reality of a highly transmissive sand aquifer is one of continuous hydraulic gradients. To evaluate the rigidity of the Ward’s linkage assignments and quantify the gradational nature of the aquifer, a secondary Pearson Affinity Analysis was conducted to measure the probabilistic membership of each well to all identified clusters (Rao and Srinivas, 2006).

### <span id="anchor-9"></span>Cluster Membership and Affinity Scoring

First, a cluster average hydrograph time series,
$$\overline{X\_{k}}{{(t)} = \frac{1}{n\_{k}}}{\sum\limits\_{i \in C\_{k}}x\_{i}}{(t)}$$
, was generated for each of the five identified clusters (k=1,…,5; shown in Figure 9) by calculating the mean standardized response of all wells definitively assigned to that cluster by the Ward’s algorithm.

The temporal affinity of every individual well i to each cluster centroid k was then quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient (ri,k​) over contemporaneous overlapping records of length N:

$$r\_{i,k} = \frac{{\sum\limits\_{t = 1}^{N}\left( {x\_{i}(t)-\overline{x\_{i}}} \right)}\cdot\left( {\overline{X\_{k}}(t)-\overline{\overline{X\_{k}}}} \right)}{\sqrt{{\sum\limits\_{t = 1}^{N}\left( {x\_{i}(t)-\overline{x\_{i}}} \right)^{2}}\cdot{\sum\limits\_{t = 1}^{N}\left( {\overline{X\_{k}}(t)-\overline{\overline{X\_{k}}}} \right)^{2}}}}$$

*Cluster centroid construction, per-well affinity scoring, and the four-category classification described in Section 3.3.2 are implemented in 05\_pearson\_affinity.py.*

### <span id="anchor-10"></span>**Classification Criteria** 

To categorize the spatial transition of the groundwater dynamics, let r\_{1st} represent a well's highest correlation with any cluster centroid, and r\_{2nd} represent its second-highest correlation. The affinity margin, Δr = r\_{1st} − r\_{2nd}, serves as a mathematical index of well hydrograph behaviour. Based on these metrics, each well in the 66-well reference network was classified into one of four structural categories. The same affinity procedure was subsequently applied to the extended network of wells with shorter or earlier records, allowing the hydrogeological classification to be extended beyond the reference network boundary (Section 3.3.3).

-   **Core (Δr ≥ 0.05):** The well exhibits a dominant, unambiguous affinity to a single cluster, and its highest correlation matches its spatial assignment from the Ward's linkage. These represent the definitive hydrological signature of their respective geomorphological zones.

-   **Fuzzy (Δr &lt; 0.05):** The well exhibits highly competing affinities between its primary and secondary clusters. This mathematical ambivalence identifies physical ecotones — transitional zones where the groundwater regime shifts smoothly between two distinct structural blocks (e.g. the boundary between the Western Residual and the Forest margin).

-   **Spy:** The well's highest temporal affinity (r\_{1st}) belongs to a different cluster than the one assigned by Ward's hydrograph linkage. This divergence highlights localized hydrogeological anomalies where sub-surface stratigraphy overrides broad spatial proximity (Hannah et al., 2000).

-   **Unclassified:** The temporal record is of insufficient length or overlap to generate statistically significant correlation coefficients.

The Δr ≥ 0.05 threshold distinguishing Core from Fuzzy membership is an empirical choice; lowering it to 0.03 or raising it to 0.10 reclassifies 21 and 13 wells respectively between the Core and Fuzzy confidence tiers, reflecting a concentration of affinity margins near the cut-off. This sensitivity affects only the confidence labelling within each cluster, not the partition itself: cluster assignment derives from the Ward's-linkage solution (Section 3.2), and every reference well retains its assigned cluster regardless of the Δr threshold.

A fifth diagnostic flag — Multi-Cluster Affinity (MCA) — is raised in addition to the primary classification for any well where three or more distinct cluster centroids achieved a correlation of r &gt; 0.90. An MCA flag mathematically identifies highly generic, highly responsive boundary wells that lack a specialized local signature, acting instead as structural nodes governed by system-wide climatic forcing rather than local topography.

### <span id="anchor-11"></span>**Extended Network Classification**

To extend the hydrogeological classification beyond the 66-well reference network, the Pearson affinity procedure was applied to all wells with a minimum of 24 months of record that did not meet the reference network criteria. The reference network cluster averages derived in Section 3.3.2 were used as fixed classification templates; these were not recalculated from the extended wells. Prior to correlation, all wells — both reference and extended — were z-score standardized jointly as a single combined dataset, ensuring that extended wells with shorter or earlier records were standardized relative to the same temporal distribution as the reference network rather than independently.

Extended wells were classified using the same affinity margin threshold (Δr = 0.05) and MCA criteria (r &gt; 0.90 with three or more centroids) as the reference network audit. Because extended wells carry no prior Ward's cluster assignment, the Spy classification is not applicable; extended wells are assigned as either Core or Fuzzy based solely on the strength of their best-match affinity.

*Extension of the affinity classification to the full monitoring array is implemented in 06\_pearson\_extended.py.*

## <span id="anchor-12"></span>Using Models to derive the Aquifer Architecture

Predicting how a dune aquifer responds to rainfall and evaporation over time requires a model that captures not just the immediate effect of each month's weather, but also the aquifer's memory of its own state. A simple approach — the Traditional Linear Model (TLM) used as a benchmark in Section 3.4.2 — treats the aquifer as a bucket that fills with rainfall and empties at a fixed background rate, regardless of how full it is (von Asmuth et al., 2002). This works reasonably well when the model is continuously corrected with observed dipwell readings, but fails when run autonomously over longer periods because it has no mechanism to slow drainage as the water table drops or accelerate it when the water table is high (Hypolite et al., 2021; Young, 2011). The state-space model (SSM) captures this physical reality by adding a head-dependent drainage term that scales drainage rate to the displacement of the current water table above a reference drainage base, consistent with the functional relationship between groundwater head and lateral discharge demonstrated in shallow sandy aquifers (Gumuła-Kawęcka et al., 2021; Manis et al., 2022). This single addition transforms the model from a statistical curve-fit into a physically constrained representation of aquifer behaviour, allowing it to simulate water table dynamics autonomously over multi-year periods without accumulating systematic error (Knotters and van Walsum, 1997).

The baseline formulation, working in terms of monthly water table change Δh = h(t) − h(t−1), is:

*Δ**h*<sub>*t*</sub> = *β*<sub>1</sub> ⋅ *P*<sub>*t*</sub> − *β*<sub>2</sub> ⋅ *P**E**T*<sub>*t*</sub> − *β*<sub>3</sub> ⋅ *h*<sub>*d**i**s**p*, *t* − 1</sub>

where Δh(t) is the change in water table depth during month *t*; P(t) and PET(t) are monthly rainfall and potential evapotranspiration totals for month *t*; h\_disp(t−1) is the displacement of the water table above the drainage datum at the end of month *t*−1; and β₁, β₂, β₃ are the recharge, atmospheric draw, and drainage coefficients respectively. The water table position is expressed in the negative-below-ground convention, so h(t−1) is negative for sub-surface levels. Displacement is defined as:

*h*<sub>*d**i**s**p*, *t* − 1</sub> = *D* + *h*<sub>*t* − 1</sub>

where D is the drainage datum depth (metres below ground surface). Working in displacement rather than raw depth ensures that h\_disp is positive when the water table sits above the drainage base, so β₃ is positive (Darcy-consistent) for all clusters. Fitting on raw depth h(t−1) produces negative β₃ for three of five clusters, indicating that the depth-below-surface coordinate does not correctly represent the hydraulic gradient driving lateral drainage. β₁, β₂ and β₃ are all defined and reported as positive quantities; the negative signs in the model equation indicate that PET and drainage act as loss terms on Δh. The complete sign convention is set out in Section 3.4.1.

The drainage datum D was selected by sweeping reference depths from 0.5 to 8.0 m in 0.1 m steps, refitting the cluster-centroid SSM at each depth and recording β₃, its p-value, R² and AIC (Burnham and Anderson, 2002) for all five clusters. The selection criterion was the minimum depth at which all five clusters simultaneously produce positive and statistically significant (p &lt; 0.05) β₃. The C4 Main Forest cluster — which has the deepest mean water table in the network and is therefore closest to the drainage base — is the binding constraint: C1, C2, C3 and C5 reach significance at shallower datums, but C4 requires the deepest. The corresponding canonical value D = 3.7 m is adopted as the network-wide datum to ensure consistent positive displacement across all clusters. As more monitoring data have accumulated since the original calibration, the empirical minimum required to satisfy the criterion has shifted shallower; the canonical value is retained to maintain comparability with all results derived under the displacement formulation. R² and AIC are insensitive to the datum across the viable range, confirming that the choice does not distort model fit. The displacement formulation does not affect β₁ or β₂, which are invariant to the datum constant, nor Δh, in which the constant cancels. The per-well spatial distribution of optimal datum depths and the R² cost of the uniform assumption are presented in Section 4.9.1 (Figure 47a).

The SSM formulation adopted here sits within an established lineage of transfer-function-noise (TFN) and linear-reservoir models for groundwater head series, in which monthly or daily head is expressed as a linear response to climatic forcing with an autoregressive memory term (von Asmuth et al., 2002; Knotters and Bierkens, 2000; Peterson and Western, 2014; Peters et al., 2003). Recent open-source implementations — notably Pastas (Collenteur et al., 2019) and HydroSight (Peterson and Western, 2014) — provide impulse-response calibration at individual wells and have been applied to shallow unconfined aquifers across a range of climatic settings (Obergfell et al., 2019; Bakker and Schaars, 2019). The formulation used in this study departs from these implementations in three ways developed in Section 3.4.1: it is fitted at cluster-average scale rather than individual-well scale; it retains the storage decay coefficient explicitly rather than absorbing it into a convolution kernel; and it constrains the intercept to zero. These choices enable the Lumped Catchment Storage Coefficient (LCSC) to be derived directly from the fitted parameters and inverted algebraically into the P\_flood threshold equations reported in Section 3.6.3.

### <span id="anchor-13"></span> Modelling the Cluster water levels

The state-space model defined in Section 3.4 was applied at the cluster level to derive mechanistic coefficients representative of each hydrogeological zone. For each of the five clusters, a mean monthly time series was computed by averaging the ground-referenced records of all member wells (Section 3.1.1), producing a single representative hydrograph of seasonal water table behaviour. The SSM was fitted to this average hydrograph using the full available record rather than the 100-month window applied to individual wells (Section 3.4.2), yielding a single set of coefficients (β₁, β₂, β₃) representative of the cluster as a whole. Cluster-scale fitting reduces the influence of individual well idiosyncrasies and local topographic noise, producing a parsimonious mechanistic characterization of each hydrogeological zone.

****Cluster-scale versus per-well fitting.**** The framework fits the state-space model at two scales, and does so deliberately, because the two answer different questions. The mechanistic characterization (Table 3, and the water-balance and BACI analyses that draw on it) is fitted to the cluster-mean hydrograph. Averaging the member records before fitting suppresses the measurement noise, short gaps and local topographic idiosyncrasies carried by any single dipwell, and returns one internally consistent coefficient set per hydrogeological zone — a signal-to-noise gain that is the point of clustering in the first place, since the zones were defined precisely so that their members share a dynamic signature. The spatial products (the coefficient surfaces of Section 3.4.3, the water-balance residual field, and the coastal-retreat regression) are instead built from the model fitted independently at each reference well, because their purpose is to resolve within-zone structure — the gradient of recharge sensitivity across the open dune, the ridge-margin concentration of the residual, the distance-decay of the coastal signal — which a cluster mean would erase by construction.

The trade-off runs in both directions and is stated plainly here. Cluster-scale fitting buys robustness at the cost of within-zone detail, and that detail is not negligible: recharge sensitivity varies roughly two-fold within the Western Residual alone (β₁ ranging 2.3–4.8 across its member wells; Table 6), and the forest interior spans drainage half-lives from about 16 to 88 months around a cluster median near 30. A single cluster coefficient set represents this spread by its centroid and should not be read as a property shared uniformly by every member. Cluster-scale fitting also inherits any error in the partition itself: a well assigned to the wrong zone, or a zone that is genuinely gradational rather than discrete, will bias the mean it contributes to — a dependence the per-well fits do not carry. The two scales are therefore treated as complementary rather than interchangeable throughout: cluster coefficients for mechanism and cross-zone comparison, per-well coefficients wherever the spatial distribution within a zone is the object of interest. Where they are combined — for instance in reading a cluster's mean behaviour against the per-well range of Table 6 — the range is reported alongside the mean rather than subsumed into it.

**Sign convention.** β₃ is defined and reported as a positive quantity throughout this study. The negative sign appears explicitly in the model equation as
 − *β*₃ ⋅ *h*<sub>*d**i**s**p*, *t* − 1</sub>
to indicate that storage decay acts as a loss term: a higher antecedent displacement above the drainage datum generates greater drainage, tending to restore equilibrium. Where the notation −β₃ appears in subsequent figures, tables and analytical sections (including the BACI, scraping and cluster mechanistic analyses), it refers to this loss term and should be read as the magnitude of the drainage decay coefficient. An increase in −β₃ therefore indicates accelerated drainage; a decrease indicates retarded drainage or greater storage retention.

**Intercept constraint.** The cluster regression was fitted with the intercept strictly forced to zero. This enforces a strict physical mass balance: in the absence of external climatic forcing (P = 0, PET = 0), the aquifer cannot spontaneously generate or lose water beyond the passive drainage decay dictated by its own antecedent storage state (
 − *β*₃ ⋅ *h*<sub>*d**i**s**p*, *t* − 1</sub>
). The constraint also enables direct algebraic inversion into the P\_flood threshold equations (Section 3.6.3).

**Physical interpretation of β₃.** The storage decay coefficient provides a parsimonious empirical representation of head-dependent lateral drainage that does not require explicit knowledge of hydraulic conductivity, aquifer geometry or hydraulic gradient — parameters that are either unmeasured or spatially variable across a 700 ha dune system where borehole logs are absent across the majority of the study area. β₃ is functionally equivalent to a linearized Darcy flux under the assumption that the hydraulic gradient scales proportionally with water table height above the drainage base, which is a reasonable approximation for a shallow unconfined dune aquifer draining to a fixed boundary (Knotters and van Walsum, 1997).

**Lumped Catchment Storage Coefficient.** The LCSC is defined as the reciprocal of the recharge sensitivity, expressed as a percentage (LCSC = 100/β₁). Expressing it as LCSC% converts the recharge sensitivity into physically intuitive units — the millimetres of rainfall required to raise the water table by 10 cm — and scales in the same direction as storage capacity, making it directly comparable to the empirical specific yield concept used by site managers. A higher LCSC therefore indicates a more buffered, less responsive aquifer: a shallow aquifer over impermeable till produces a large water table rise from a small rainfall event, a high β₁, and a low LCSC, whilst a deep sand aquifer absorbs rain across a thick unsaturated zone before the water table rises appreciably, producing a low β₁ and a high LCSC.

**Cluster-level SSM fits are implemented in **03\_state\_space\_model.py**. The five cluster-mean hydrographs are constructed and exported by the same script and become the input to all downstream cluster-scale analyses.**

### **State-Space vs. Traditional Linear Model Benchmark**

****Model Configurations.**** Both models were independently fitted to the most recent 100 months of data to predict the monthly change in water table elevation (Δh\_t) using the same climatic forcing data, differing only in their structural representation of the aquifer:**

1.  **Traditional Linear Model (TLM)** treats the aquifer as a passive linear store driven purely by atmospheric inputs, with a fitted intercept term capturing background drift and unmeasured forcing, but no mechanism representing the aquifer's memory of its own state (von Asmuth et al., 2002):

*Δ**h*<sub>*t*</sub> = *b**e**t**a*<sub>1</sub> ⋅ *P*<sub>*t*</sub> − *b**e**t**a*<sub>2</sub> ⋅ *P**E**T*<sub>*t*</sub> + *c*

1.  **State-Space Model (SSM):** the physically constrained mass-balance framework defined in Section 3.4, fitted through the origin (c = 0), with antecedent displacement h\_disp,t−1 included as a feedback term that causes drainage to accelerate when the water table is high and slow when it is low:

*Δ**h*<sub>*t*</sub> = *b**e**t**a*<sub>1</sub> ⋅ *P*<sub>*t*</sub> − *b**e**t**a*<sub>2</sub> ⋅ *P**E**T*<sub>*t*</sub> − *b**e**t**a*<sub>3</sub> ⋅ *h*<sub>*d**i**s**p*</sub>, *t* − 1

**Forecasting modes: diagnostic vs iterative.** Model performance was evaluated under two distinct operational modes to separate baseline statistical fit from true forecasting stability:

-   **One-Step Diagnostic Fit:** The model predicts the change in head for month **t** using the *observed* field measurement of h(t−1)​ from the prior month. This tests the fundamental validity of the regression coefficients without compounding error.

-   **Iterative Forecasting Stability:** The model predicts the change in head for month **t** using its own simulated head from the previous timestep (h\_sim), relying entirely on its own simulated state over the 100-month evaluation window without periodic data assimilation; this tests resistance to cumulative drift over multi-year autonomous simulation.

**1) Performance Metrics. **Diagnostic fit (one-step) was evaluated using the Coefficient of Determination (R<sup>2</sup>). Iterative forecasting stability was evaluated using R<sup>2</sup>, the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and the Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) (Nash and Sutcliffe, 1970; ****Krause et al., 2005****).

The RMSE is mathematically defined as:

$$R^{2} = \left( \frac{{\sum\left( {h\_{\mathit{obs}}-\overline{h\_{\mathit{obs}}}} \right)}\left( {h\_{❑}-\overline{h\_{❑}}} \right)}{\sqrt{{\sum\left( {h\_{\mathit{obs}}-\overline{h\_{\mathit{obs}}}} \right)^{2}}{\sum\left( {h\_{❑}-\overline{h\_{❑}}} \right)^{2}}}} \right)^{2}$$

The NSE is mathematically defined as:

$${\mathit{NSE} = 1}-\frac{\sum\left( {h\_{\mathit{obs},t}-h\_{,t}} \right)^{2}}{\sum\left( {h\_{\mathit{obs},t}-\overline{h\_{\mathit{obs}}}} \right)^{2}}$$

The NSE is the most informative metric for continuous hydrological forecasting. An NSE=1.0 indicates perfect predictive agreement, an NSE=0 indicates the model is only as accurate as using the long-term observed mean, and an NSE&lt;0 indicates that the model is actively worse than using the observed mean, typically due to unchecked cumulative mass-balance drift.

**2) Spatial Diagnostics of Subsurface Architecture using SSM and TLM comparison.** Plotting the comparative performance metrics, defined as the improvement in variance explanation:

*Δ**R*<sup>2</sup> = *R*<sub>*S**S**M*</sub><sup>2</sup> − *R*<sub>*T**L**M*</sub><sup>2</sup>

and the improvement in iterative forecasting stability:

*Δ**N**S**E* = *N**S**E*<sub>*S**S**M*</sub> − *N**S**E*<sub>*T**L**M*</sub>

serves as more than a statistical validation; it acts as a spatial diagnostic tool for the underlying geological architecture (Beven, 2012; Gupta et al., 2008). Similar multi-methodological approaches have been employed to constrain recharge estimates and reduce uncertainty in shallow sandy systems by evaluating the sensitivity of model boundaries to observed head variations.** **Because the fundamental mathematical difference between the two models is the inclusion of the head-dependent internal storage decay coefficient (-β<sub>3​</sub>·h(t−1)​), the spatial distribution of model improvement physically delineates the subsurface structural boundaries of the aquifer:

-   **High ΔR² and ΔNSE zones:** Regions where the SSM most substantially outperforms the TLM are those where drainage is most strongly head-dependent at the monthly timescale (Fetter, 2001; Robins, Jones and Farr, 2013). In these zones the proportional drainage rate varies considerably between high and low water table states, making the -β₃ term essential for accurate autonomous simulation. The Western Block's deep sand buffer moderates this dynamic, and canopy interception in the Forest cluster smooths it further, producing a gradient of decreasing SSM gain from east to west that mirrors the structural gradient identified by clustering.

<!-- -->

-   **Low ΔR² and ΔNSE zones: **Conversely, areas exhibiting small or marginally negative improvement between the two models reflect zones where drainage is least strongly head-dependent. A small or zero SSM gain in these zones does not indicate model failure but confirms that the TLM's linear approximation is adequate where drainage dynamics are sufficiently buffered or smoothed (Gumuła-Kawęcka et al., 2021). The two C4 ridge-flank wells where the SSM underperforms TLM are CEH14 (strongly) and CEH13 (mildly); CEH14 also carries the second most negative water balance residual in the network (Section 4.9.7, Figure 58). Both findings point to a hydrological setting at the bedrock-ridge interface where the standard rainfall–PET–drainage framework characterizes the system less completely than at typical aquifer locations.

By mapping the success or failure of the storage decay coefficient -****β₃**** , this comparative methodology physically draws the structural boundary lines between deep sand reserves, perched slacks, and complex discharge zones without requiring extensive invasive coring.

### Drawdown-Field visualization

Six spatial fields visualize how localized perturbations to the water table propagate through the dune aquifer: the drawdown imposed by canopy interception within the forest block, the drawdown imposed by shoreline retreat at the western dune margin, the head gain produced by sea-level rise at the coastal boundary, and the cell-by-cell difference of the latter two. All six are rendered as filled or line contours over the 2 m LiDAR digital elevation model hillshade and clipped to the study-area outline, so that the forest, erosion, sea-level-rise and net maps are mutually comparable. They are single-mechanism illustrative constructions: each isolates one process to show its spatial reach, and is not a closed water-balance solution.

The forest drawdown is propagated outward from the forest boundary as an exponential decay, Δh(d) = H₀·exp(−d/λ), with edge magnitude H₀ = 150 mm taken from the canopy-interception literature (Freeman 2008) and characteristic decay length λ = √(D/β₃) ≈ 220 m. The hydraulic diffusivity D = Kb/Sy and the SSM drainage coefficient β₃ are sourced from the open-dune medium *through which* the drawdown propagates (the C3 Western Residual cluster) rather than from the forest interior. Distance from the forest edge is computed as a flow-direction-weighted cost-distance over the DEM, so that the drawdown follows hydraulically plausible paths rather than straight-line Euclidean distance.

The coastal-erosion field visualizes the drawdown produced by a single discrete shoreline-retreat event of 6 m, a Storm-Brendan-scale exemplar of the episodic, storm-driven retreat process at the western Newborough margin (Pye & Blott 2024). The edge drawdown magnitude is set by the ratio of the retreat event to the long-term storm-inclusive retreat rate of 8.3 m yr⁻¹ (measurements reported by Forgrave 2020) multiplied by the network-scale coast-edge anomaly fitted in Section 3.7.4. The storm-inclusive rate, rather than the chronic background rate (~1.5–1.8 m yr⁻¹ at the southern frontage, Pye & Blott 2024), is the appropriate normalizer because the construction visualizes a single Brendan-class event, not the long-term mean process; using a chronic rate would over-attribute the network-scale coast-edge anomaly to non-event background drift. The field decays inland from the dune-toe front following the forest-free linear-capped coastal-retreat gradient of that fit, Δh(d) = h₀·max(1 − d/L, 0), with δ₀ and L read live from it. The dune-toe front is derived by extracting the DEM contour at the waterline and offsetting it 100 m inland to where the aquifer outcrops; the seaward side is set to zero. The field references the eroding Caernarfon Bay (south-western) shore only.

The sea-level-rise field models the head gain at the coast as a finite-window boundary perturbation diffusing inland, Δh(d) = ΔSLR·erfc(d/(2√(Dt))), with ΔSLR = 0.02 m accumulated over a 5-year window (≈ 4 mm yr⁻¹, the north-Wales near-term UKCP18 central estimate). The diffusivity D = Kb/Sy uses the same C3 specific yield as the forest and erosion fields; the diffusive length-scale 2√(Dt) ≈ 840 m sets the inland reach. The boundary is referenced to mean sea level (0 m AOD), distinct from the dune-toe front of the erosion field — the water table is pinned to mean sea level at the coast, not at the dune toe.

The net coastal field is the cell-by-cell difference between the sea-level-rise head gain and the erosion drawdown over a matched 5-year window. It reuses the two component fields and is subject to the same single-shore, single-mechanism assumptions; it is a difference of two illustrative fields, not a mass-balance solution.

The dune-scrape drawdown field renders the April 2015 CEH36 scrape as a steady-state topographic drain, the same leaky-aquifer construction as the forest field. The edge magnitude H₀ is set not to an assumed excavation depth but to the directly measured CEH36 response (the paired-BACI pure-scraping step, Section 4.5), read live; the unsurveyed excavation depth is recovered as the inferred quantity D = H₀ / Sy through the open-dune specific yield. The field decays from the excavation along a flow-weighted cost-distance with the same characteristic length λ ≈ 220 m as the forest field, sourced from the C3 open-dune medium through which it propagates. The excavation interior is masked: the scraped slack itself rises (Section 5.4.1), so the rendered field is the surrounding drawdown — the dipole counterpart of the slack rise — not a drawdown at the scraped site.

The clearfell pre-fell baseline field combines the two non-felling drawdown drivers bearing on the December 2017 clearfell compartment — the 2015 CEH36 scrape and a single Storm-Brendan-scale shoreline-retreat event — as the cell-by-cell sum of the scrape-drain and coastal-erosion fields. Both are head losses, so the combined field is rendered on the same sequential drawdown scale as its components rather than on a diverging scale; sea-level rise is excluded by construction, the field being the footprint of the two confounders the clearfell BACI must separate from the felling signal (Section 4.6) rather than a coastal mass balance. It is a first-order superposition of two illustrative fields, not a closed water-balance solution.

The drawdown-field visualizations are implemented in 20\_spatial\_figures.py.

## <span id="anchor-16"></span>Anthropogenic Interventions

### <span id="anchor-17"></span>The Before-After Control-Impact (BACI) Design

To evaluate the hydrological outcomes of anthropogenic interventions at the study site, a Before-After Control-Impact (BACI) experimental design was used (Stewart-Oaten et al., 1986). This framework is designed to isolate the effect of a specific localized disturbance from broader natural variability, such as seasonal weather patterns or regional climatic drift.

The fundamental mechanism of the BACI approach involves contrasting an impact zone (where the intervention occurred) with a reference control zone (which remained undisturbed) across designated pre-intervention and post-intervention periods. Rather than simply measuring absolute changes in water levels, the model calculates the difference (Δ) between the impact and control sites. The actual effect of the intervention is subsequently quantified by measuring the shift in this difference after the event.

For highly complex environments where multiple interventions overlap, the standard BACI method was developed into a Hierarchical Nested Control design to ensure robust baseline validation. By using multiple control sites to defend against localized hydrogeological anomalies, this study employs a "Beyond BACI" approach (Underwood, 1992).

**General Before-After Control-Impact (BACI) Calculation. **The standard BACI effect size isolates the intervention signal from natural background variation. The fundamental equation calculates the difference in means (μ) between the Impact (I) and Control (C) sites across the Before (B) and After (A) periods:

*B**A**C**I*<sub>effect</sub> = (*μ*<sub>*I*, *A*</sub>−*μ*<sub>*C*, *A*</sub>) − (*μ*<sub>*I*, *B*</sub>−*μ*<sub>*C*, *B*</sub>)

Where:

-   μ<sub>I,A</sub>​ is the mean water level at the impact site post-intervention.

-   μ<sub>C,A</sub>​ is the mean water level at the control site post-intervention.

-   μ<sub>I,B​</sub> and μ<sub>C,B​</sub> represent the respective means prior to the intervention.

**Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) Statistical Verification. **To validate the precise temporal onset and permanence of hydrological shifts identified by the BACI framework, a Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) sequential analysis was integrated into the analytical framework (Page, 1954). While the BACI contrast quantifies the magnitude of an intervention's effect, the CUSUM statistic is used to identify the exact temporal structural break — or regime shift — within the time series data. Era-specific estimation of the storage decay coefficient −β₃ is additionally reported across treatment and control wells; changes that are confined to the intervention footprint would be consistent with a genuine drainage response, whilst changes extending uniformly to unmanipulated control wells indicate a network-wide signal attributable to climate or SSM fitting artefacts rather than the intervention itself.

**Mathematical Framework. **The CUSUM method operates by calculating a running total of the deviations of individual data points from an established baseline mean. In the context of this study, the input variables (x\_i) are the sequential differences between the impact and control wells (Δ), and the baseline (μ\_baseline) is the mean of these differences during the pre-intervention period. The cumulative sum at time t is formalized as:

$$C\_{t} = {\sum\limits\_{i = 1}^{t}\left( {x\_{i}-\mu\_{\text{baseline}}} \right)}$$

**Interpretation of the CUSUM Trajectory. **The resulting CUSUM trajectory provides a clear visualization of system stability:

-   **Baseline Stability:** A stable relationship between the impact and control sites yields random fluctuations around the baseline mean, producing a roughly horizontal CUSUM trajectory.

-   **Structural Break Detection:** A sustained shift in the hydrological relationship — such as that caused by canopy removal or slack scraping — results in directional deviations that accumulate rapidly, manifesting as a distinct change in the gradient of the CUSUM line.

-   **Temporal Verification:** The exact point of inflection, where the trajectory breaks from its horizontal trend, marks the initiation of the intervention's physical effect.

By aligning these inflection points with the documented dates of the December 2017 clearfell and the 2015 and 2023 scraping interventions, the analysis confirms that the observed divergence is a direct consequence of the site works rather than gradual environmental drift. To isolate the localized effects of topographical and silvicultural interventions from background climatic drift, all CUSUM trajectories were calculated on the BACI delta (impact minus control) rather than raw water table depth. This ensures that any identified structural breaks represent a true divergence from the undisturbed regional baseline.

Where an impact site has experienced more than one intervention over the monitoring period, or where a secondary disturbance confounds the primary signal, the record is subdivided into discrete management eras defined by the dates of known interventions. Each era is treated as a distinct analytical period with its own mean water level, inter-era BACI delta and −β₃ estimate. This allows the analysis to separate the signal of a specific intervention from preceding baseline conditions and from subsequent confounding events — for example, isolating the effect of dune scraping before the onset of a plantation clearance pulse that would otherwise inflate or mask the scraping response. Era boundaries are defined a priori from documented intervention dates rather than from the data, preventing circular reasoning in the identification of structural breaks.

### <span id="anchor-18"></span>Hierarchical Nested Control Application

This framework operates across two distinct analytical tiers:

-   **Tier 1 (Background Drift Validation):** Local control wells are first evaluated against a broader regional mean. This step verifies that the designated control sites are behaving consistently with macro-environmental trends and are not subject to anomalous localized influences, such as unmapped coastal drawdown. This tier verifies that the local control (C\_local​) accurately tracks the broader regional mean (C\_region​). The temporal difference (Δ\_drift​) at any time t is calculated as:

*Δ*<sub>drift, *t*</sub> = *C*<sub>local, *t*</sub> − *C*<sub>region, *t*</sub>

> (where C\_local,t is the water-table depth at the local control well at time t and C\_region,t is the regional mean across the C3 climate control wells at the same time. A near-zero, stable Δ\_drift,t across the pre-intervention baseline confirms that the local control is tracking regional conditions and is not itself subject to localized disturbance.)

> A structurally stable Δ\_drift,t​ over the baseline timeline validates the local control for use in Tier 2.

-   ****Tier 2 (Intervention Signal Isolation):**** Once validated, these local controls are paired directly with specific impact wells. This pairing subtracts the regional noise and secondary intervention pulses, isolating the pure signal of the targeted experiment. The Tier 2 BACI effect calculates the net shift in the impact-minus-control gap between any two consecutive management eras:

> Net Benefit<sub>*E*1 → *E*2</sub> = (*μ*<sub>*I*, *E*2</sub>−*μ*<sub>*C*<sub>local</sub>, *E*2</sub>) − (*μ*<sub>*I*, *E*1</sub>−*μ*<sub>*C*<sub>local</sub>, *E*1</sub>)

> **(where I is the impact well; C\_local is the validated local control well; E1 is the earlier era (e.g. pre-intervention baseline) and E2 is the later era (e.g. post-intervention); and μ\_{I,E} denotes the mean water-table depth at well I during era E. The outer subtraction removes whatever gap existed between impact and control before the intervention, leaving only the change in that gap — the net hydrological effect of moving from era E1 to era E2. Because C\_local has been validated in Tier 1 **to track the regional mean, this difference is free of regional climatic noise and of any secondary intervention pulses that affect both wells equally.)**

### <span id="anchor-19"></span>Dune Scraping Intervention Analysis

A two-tier hierarchical BACI framework was applied across five focal wells. Three wells served as scraping treatment sites — CEH36 (central Warren, scraped April 2015), CEH18 (western boundary, scraped October 2023) and CEH21 (coastal margin, scraped October 2023) — and two as local controls: CEH4 (central Warren, unmanipulated) and CEH22 (coastal margin, unmanipulated). A regional mean was computed as the arithmetic mean of five C3 climate control wells (CEH9, NW7, NW6, NW5, WMC2) to characterize background system-wide trends. NW8 and NW8B were excluded on the grounds of data quality.

The scraping programme extended beyond these three monitored sites. Two further slacks in the western dunes (Scrape A and Scrape B) were scraped in the same 2015 campaign as CEH36 but carry no monitoring dipwells, and three sites in the eastern dunes (CEH40, CEH41 and CEH42) were scraped earlier, in February 2013, with their dipwells first recording in July 2014 — seventeen months after the works. None of these five provides a pre-scraping baseline, so the intervention analysis is necessarily restricted to CEH36, CEH18 and CEH21, the sites at which a before-and-after comparison is possible. The unmonitored and late-instrumented scrapes are represented in the combined spatial driver reconstruction (Section 3.8.5) but cannot be evaluated for benefit; where that reconstruction requires a per-scrape response for them, the measured CEH36 response is assumed, and this assumption is stated in the relevant figure captions.

Because the three treatment wells span different management histories, each was decomposed into site-specific eras defined by intervention dates:

-   **CEH36 and its paired control CEH4** were decomposed into three eras: Baseline (pre-April 2015); Pure Scraping (April 2015 to November 2017); Felling Pulse (December 2017 onwards), capturing the confounding influence of the plantation clearance.

-   **CEH18 and CEH21** were decomposed into three eras: Baseline (pre-December 2017); Felling Pulse (December 2017 to October 2023); After Scraping (October 2023 onwards).

Five limitations of the control wells and treatment records bear directly on interpretation and are stated here to avoid repetition in the results.

First, CEH4 is subject to two independent sources of instability: progressive ground surface lowering from coastal erosion, and sustained water table deterioration attributable to the post-2017 felling pulse confirmed by its Tier 1 CUSUM trajectory. Net benefit estimates at CEH36 during the Pure Scraping era should be treated as upper bounds; an additional upward bias may arise from contemporaneous foredune build-up at the site contributing to a rising water table independently of the scraping intervention. Net benefit estimates at CEH18 over the After Scraping era are similarly subject to caution given CEH4's post-felling deterioration. The robustness of the CEH36 scraping signal is tested independently in Section 3.5.3 using a synthetic control constructed from donor wells calibrated on the pre-scraping period and by SSM forward residual analysis.

Second, CEH22 experienced accelerating deterioration throughout the monitoring period that, as the network-wide −β₃ analysis demonstrates, reflects a system-wide signal rather than a process localized to the coastal margin. CEH22 does not constitute a stable Tier 1 baseline; net benefits are therefore not reported for CEH21, and raw Tier 2 BACI shifts are reported for completeness only.

Third, CEH18 and CEH21 were scraped in October 2023, providing only two full post-scraping summers by the end of 2025. The short post-intervention record limits the statistical power of pre- vs post-scraping comparisons at these wells and precludes any conclusion about whether the scraping benefit observed at CEH36 over a longer timeframe will be replicated at the secondary sites.

Fourth, the pre-scraping baseline length differs substantially across the focal wells: CEH4 (paired control, installed May 2006) provides 8.9 years; CEH36 (central treatment well, installed January 2011) provides 4.2 years; CEH18 (installed September 2007) provides 7.6 years pre-2015; and CEH21 (installed July 2010) provides 4.8 years pre-2015. CEH36's shorter pre-scraping baseline does not invalidate the analysis: the scraping methodology does not require estimation of a long-baseline parametric covariate structure (in contrast to the clearfell ANCOVA, Section 3.5.4, which estimates a cumulative-water-balance slope from pre-felling data). CUSUM, Welch's t-test on era means, synthetic-control calibration and SSM forward residual all operate effectively with the available pre-event window, and the robustness analysis described in Section 3.5.3 tests the resulting BACI step against three independent methods to confirm convergence.

Fifth, the scraping analysis treats CEH4's progressive ground-surface lowering from coastal erosion as a confound documented in the era-by-era comparisons rather than as a covariate explicitly modelled in the BACI design. CEH4 sits approximately 340 m inland of the southern foreshore, and the characteristic propagation timescale (L²S\_y / K, Section 5.4.3) for coastal-erosion effects to reach inland wells through the aquifer is multi-year to multi-decadal. Most of the coastal-erosion propagation envelope therefore lies outside the analysis window, with the visible deterioration at CEH4 reflecting both the integrated effect of historical coastal retreat and a contribution from the post-2017 felling pulse. The Tier 1 CUSUM analysis at CEH4 captures this combined drift but cannot decompose it into its component drivers; the synthetic control and SSM forward residual analyses below provide the necessary alternative counterfactuals.

1\) Paired-control BACI design and analytical sub-procedures

Four analytical procedures are applied within the paired-control BACI framework. Each addresses a distinct research question; results are cross-referenced to the relevant sub-sections of Section 4.5.

**Paired BACI and net benefit estimation.** Tier 1 CUSUM analysis evaluated each local control well against the regional mean to verify that the controls were tracking system-wide background trends consistently. Tier 2 paired CUSUM analysis differenced each treatment well against its matched local control to isolate the scraping signal from regional climatic drift. Net benefit was computed as the BACI shift in the treatment-minus-control difference between consecutive eras, as defined in Section 3.5.1. Results are presented in Section 4.5.1 (background drift) and Section 4.5.2 (paired BACI shifts).

**Scraping propagation analysis.** A separate analysis tested whether the CEH36 scraping event — which created a topographic low acting as a local drain — produced a detectable drawdown at wells uphill of the scraped site. Groundwater flow toward the new drain would steepen the local hydraulic gradient, lowering the water table at neighbouring wells. The displacement-formulation SSM was fitted independently to pre-scraping (start of record to March 2015) and post-scraping (April 2015 to November 2017) windows for each of 10 wells located north and north-west of CEH36, positioned along the expected groundwater flow path into the forest interior. Seven distant C3 wells (NW1, NW2, NW11, NW13, WMC4, D25, WMC2), located 850–1100 m from CEH36 and well beyond any expected scraping influence, served as BACI controls. Per-well coefficient shifts (Δβ₁, Δβ₂, Δβ₃) were corrected by subtracting the control centroid shift, isolating any scraping-specific perturbation from background climate drift. A distance-decay regression tested whether the BACI-corrected Δβ₃ diminished with distance from CEH36, as would be expected if the scraping drain were pulling water from the uphill aquifer. Results are presented in Section 4.5.5.

**Summer minima analysis.** The annual summer minimum depth (June–September) was computed for each of the five focal wells. For each well, the gap between the well's summer minimum and both the climate control centroid and the paired control summer minimum was computed annually, and the pre- vs post-scraping shift tested by Welch's t-test. This mirrors the methodology applied to the clearfell intervention (Section 3.5.4) and addresses the ecological question directly: does scraping improve the critical summer low that determines dune slack habitat viability? Results are presented in Section 4.5.4.

**Scenario comparison.** To contextualize the observed scraping benefit at CEH36, five alternative scenarios were computed at the same well using its own SSM coefficients and its own per-well WTF-derived specific yield. Three forestry scenarios — clearfell, 50% thinning, and broadleaf conversion — are hypothetical, asking what the response would be if CEH36 had pine canopy and was then managed. These apply interception and atmospheric draw perturbations from the forestry analysis (Section 3.5.4) to CEH36's SSM. Two climate scenarios (dry and wet) apply UKCP18 RCP8.5 2050s central estimates. The equilibrium volumetric water table change (mm water equivalent per month) was computed from the SSM equilibrium response under two forcings: annual-mean rainfall and PET, and summer (July–September) rainfall and PET. Both figures report the same volumetric quantity, so all scenarios are directly comparable; the summer figure is the equilibrium response to summer forcing and not a summer minimum, which the equilibrium framework cannot resolve. For scraping, the on-site bar is the observed paired-BACI response converted to a volumetric equivalent, and a second bar gives the modelled off-site neighbour drawdown at 100 m (with a 250 m reference line) from the drain-cone model of Section 4.9.3. Results are presented in Section 4.5.6.

*These four sub-procedures are implemented in 09a\_paired\_baci.py, 09b\_scraping\_propagation.py, 09c\_summer\_minima.py and 09d\_scenario\_comparison.py respectively.*

2\) Era-specific β₃ analysis on the drainage residual

Beyond the per-era CUSUM and BACI shift tests, the scraping intervention may have altered the storage decay coefficient β₃ at the treatment wells. To diagnose this, a two-step procedure is applied separately at each treatment well and its paired control. The procedure isolates per-era variation in β₃ from the confounding influence of inter-era differences in climate forcing.

First, β₁ and β₂ are estimated by fitting the full displacement-formulation SSM (Section 3.4) to the complete well record. The climate forcing contribution is then removed from each monthly observation to yield a drainage residual series, representing the component of water table change attributable to head-dependent drainage after removing the estimated rainfall and evapotranspiration contributions:

*D*<sub>*r**e**s**i**d**u**a**l*</sub> = *Δ**h*<sub>*t*</sub> − *β*<sub>1</sub> ⋅ *P*<sub>*t*</sub> − *β*<sub>2</sub> ⋅ (−*P**E**T*<sub>*t*</sub>)

Second, for each management era separately, the era-specific drainage coefficient is estimated by regressing this drainage residual against minus the displacement at the end of the previous month, with a constant term:

*D*<sub>*r**e**s**i**d**u**a**l*</sub> = −*β*<sub>3</sub> ⋅ (−*h*<sub>*d**i**s**p*, *t* − 1</sub>) + *c* + *ϵ*

with 95% confidence intervals computed by OLS on each era's data subset. Era-specific β₃ values are compared across the Baseline, Pure Scraping (or Felling Pulse) and where applicable After Scraping eras, with non-overlapping confidence intervals interpreted as evidence of an era-specific shift in drainage behaviour.

This procedure reduces the influence of differential climate forcing between eras, but does not eliminate all sources of artefact: where the water table undergoes a sustained directional shift between eras — as occurs following both clearfell and scraping — the h\_disp,prev term itself tracks a non-stationary trajectory, and the model may interpret systematic head changes as an elevated drainage signal. Era-specific β₃ estimates should therefore be interpreted cautiously, and spatial extent is used as the primary diagnostic: changes confined to the intervention footprint are consistent with a genuine drainage response, whilst changes extending uniformly to unmanipulated control wells point to a network-wide artefact rather than an intervention effect. Results are presented in Section 4.5.3.

*This procedure is implemented in 09a\_paired\_baci.py as part of the paired-control BACI workflow.*

3\) Robustness triangulation of the CEH36 step

The CEH36 Pure Scraping era step change is the central scraping result. Because the paired control well CEH4 is subject to two independent confounds — progressive ground-surface lowering from coastal erosion and post-2017 felling-pulse deterioration — the magnitude of the CEH36 step estimated by paired BACI alone may carry a CEH4-specific bias. Three independent estimation methods are applied at CEH36 to test whether the step is robust under alternative counterfactuals.

The first method is the **paired BACI** estimate itself, reported in Section 3.5.3 and presented for comparison.

The second method is a **synthetic control** estimate. A weighted composite of donor wells, drawn from the C3 climate control set and from other reference-network wells with strong pre-scraping correlation to CEH36, is constructed to match CEH36's pre-scraping trajectory. The donor weights are estimated on the pre-scraping period only, ensuring that the synthetic control is calibrated independently of the post-intervention data it is then used to evaluate. The scraping step is computed as the difference between observed CEH36 and the synthetic-control prediction over the Pure Scraping era.

The third method is an **SSM forward residual** estimate. The displacement-formulation SSM is fitted to the CEH36 pre-scraping record alone, and the fitted model is then forward-simulated through the Pure Scraping era using observed climate forcing as input. The simulated water table represents the predicted CEH36 trajectory in the counterfactual where no scraping had occurred. The scraping step is computed as the difference between observed and predicted CEH36 over the Pure Scraping era.

Convergence between the three methods — paired BACI, synthetic control, and SSM forward residual — provides evidence that the estimated step is a real intervention effect rather than an artefact of CEH4's own deterioration. The three estimates and their convergence are reported in Section 4.5.2. *This triangulation is implemented in 09e\_robustness.py.*

### <span id="anchor-20"></span>Clearfell BACI experiment 

A five-tier hierarchical BACI experiment was conducted across 17 wells to assess the hydrological consequences of the December 2017 clearfell. Wells were assigned to tiers defined a priori from spatial proximity to the felled compartment and cluster membership, with all wells required to have a minimum of eight years' pre-felling baseline to ensure stable coefficient estimation:

-   **Impact:** WMC3 (45 m from felling compartment centroid E=241210, N=363607, inside the cleared area; 8.4 years pre-felling record).

-   **Edge:** CEH31, CEH20, CEH30, CEH16 (152–229 m, adjacent to the felling boundary; 7.4–10.3 years pre-felling).

-   **Forest control:** CEH32, CEH34, CEH33, NW10, CEH2 (C4 Main Forest interior, unaffected by felling, same canopy cover; 306–428 m; 7.3–11.6 years pre-felling).

-   **Coastal control:** CEH19, CEH17 (C5 Coastal Forest; separated from C4 on the basis of a distinct β₂ regime and non-overlapping elevation range — Section 4.2; 9.7–10.3 years pre-felling).

-   **Climate control:** CEH9, NW7, NW6, NW5, WMC2 (C3 Western Block, no forest canopy; 571–1056 m; 8.8–12.6 years pre-felling).

Wells with insufficient pre-felling baseline were excluded from the primary analysis. The candidate-well criterion adopted here was monitoring continuity from the 2010 network expansion onwards, providing a minimum of seven years of pre-felling record and at least four years of pre-scraping record before the April 2015 scraping. This baseline length is required for two reasons. First, the April 2015 scraping at CEH36 (Section 3.5.3) introduced a level shift in the BACI gap at the same date, requiring a scraping covariate in the ANCOVA (Section 3.5.4) to avoid biasing the clearfell estimate; reliable estimation of this covariate requires that included wells have monitoring data spanning both pre- and post-scraping periods so that the April 2015 step is identifiable separately from the December 2017 felling step. Second, multiple full annual cycles are needed to constrain the seasonal cycle and the cumulative water balance slope independently of the felling step. FE1, FE2 (both installed August 2015) and NW8B (July 2015) were installed after the 2010 expansion and within four months of the scraping; their entire pre-felling baseline sits within the post-scraping era. FE3 (April 2017), FE4 (November 2017) and LIS1 (November 2017) provide eight months or fewer of pre-felling record. Following general BACI guidance that pre-intervention monitoring should span sufficient years to characterize inter-annual variability and resolve overlapping perturbations (Smokorowski and Randall, 2017), these wells were excluded. CEH36 was excluded as the scraping treatment site itself: its post-scraping water table record does not represent undisturbed pre-intervention conditions.

The CEH34 well in the Forest control tier was installed in August 2010, providing 7.3 years of pre-felling record. To extend its participation in the Forest control centroid back to the start of the broader pre-scraping baseline window, a donor-regression hindcast was applied. CEH34's pre-2010 record was synthesized from a calibrated relationship with CEH9 (Climate control), fitted by OLS on their overlapping pre-clearfell record (August 2010 to November 2017). CEH9 was selected as the donor in preference to the empirically more strongly correlated CEH2 because CEH9 belongs to the Climate control set and is therefore independent of the Forest control centroid; using a Forest control donor would have partially double-counted that donor's contribution to the pre-fell baseline. The synthetic CEH34 hindcast was spliced with the observed CEH34 record from August 2010 onwards, providing a continuous Forest control centroid input across the full pre-scraping to post-felling window. *The CEH34 hindcast is implemented in 10i\_ceh34\_hindcast.py and runs as a prerequisite for all downstream BACI analyses.*

The separation of controls into three counterfactual pools — Forest, Climate and Combined — allows the clearfell question to be asked three ways: (a) did the felled wells diverge from unfelled C4 wells sharing the same canopy, substrate and elevation range, the most position-matched comparison; (b) did they diverge from the climate trajectory defined by unforested C3 wells in the Western Block; and (c) against a blended counterfactual pooling all 12 control wells (C4 Forest, C5 Coastal Forest and C3 Climate). The Coastal Control tier (C5: CEH19, CEH17) is not used as a primary counterfactual in the three-pool ANCOVA — its distinct β₂ regime and non-overlapping elevation range from C4 mean it is not position-matched to the felled wells — but it appears in the Combined pool and is analysed separately as a coastal-erosion diagnostic in the summer minima and coefficient decomposition analyses (Sections 4.6.4 and 4.6.6).

The clearfell effect is quantified through three complementary analyses, presented in turn below: (i) the network structure and ANCOVA model, which estimates the felling step from the BACI gap against each counterfactual pool, with covariates for the April 2015 scraping and the east–west coastal-retreat gradient; (ii) a summer-window analysis with a climate-collinearity sensitivity variant, isolating the response in the ecologically critical low-water season; and (iii) a per-era SSM coefficient decomposition of the clearfell effect, attributing the change to the recharge, atmospheric-draw and drainage terms.

1\) Network structure and ANCOVA model

The BACI gap for each target tier (Impact, Edge) was calculated as the target centroid mean water level minus the control centroid mean, producing a monthly time series of (target − control) values for each of the three control definitions. This monthly BACI gap is distinct from the SSM displacement (Section 3.4), which is the head above the drainage datum used internally as a predictor in the SSM β₃ term; the BACI gap is the dependent variable in the clearfell ANCOVA.

To separate the intervention signal from background climate variability and from the prior scraping event, an ANCOVA-BACI model (Underwood, 1992; Gotelli & Ellison, 2004; Osenberg et al., 1994) was fitted to each monthly BACI gap series:

*B**A**C**I*<sub>*g**a**p*</sub> = *α* + *β*<sub>1</sub> ⋅ *C**W**B* + *β*<sub>2</sub> ⋅ *D*<sub>*s**c**r**a**p**e*</sub>(*t*) + *β*<sub>3</sub> ⋅ *D*<sub>*f**e**l**l*</sub>(*t*) + *β*<sub>4</sub> ⋅ (*C**W**B*×*D*<sub>*f**e**l**l*</sub>) + *β*<sub>5</sub> ⋅ *E**a**s**t**i**n**g*(*t*) + *ϵ*

where CWB is the centred cumulative water balance anomaly (cumulated P − PET departure from the long-run mean); D\_scrape(t) is a distance-weighted scraping covariate applying an exponential decay from CEH36 with length scale λ = 300 m, activated at the April 2015 scraping date; D\_fell(t) is a step function for the December 2017 clearfell; CWB × D\_fell is an interaction term testing whether climate sensitivity changed after felling; and Easting(t) is an easting × time interaction term that absorbs the progressive coastal erosion gradient across the well network.

The BACI displacement series (target centroid minus control centroid) contains several overlapping signals: the scraping step, the clearfell step, climate-driven fluctuations (absorbed by CWB), and a slow linear drift caused by coastal erosion progressively deepening the more coastal target wells relative to inland controls. If this drift is not explicitly modelled, the regression distributes the unmodelled trend across whichever coefficients best reduce the residual, biasing all estimates including the clearfell step. The easting × time term is therefore included for any control run in which the combined target-and-control well set spans an easting range exceeding 200 m; this criterion is met for all three control definitions (Forest control easting range 499 m, Climate 1,056 m, Combined 1,056 m). This linear gradient captures average differential deterioration across the observation window but does not represent time-lagged inland propagation of the coastal-erosion signal: at the felling-affected wells, which sit several hundred metres inland of the coast, the characteristic propagation timescale (L²S\_y / K, Section 5.4.3) for coastal-erosion effects to reach the wells is multi-year to multi-decadal, with most of the propagation envelope lying outside the 2018–2026 analysis window. The linear gradient should therefore be interpreted as an empirical correction for differential drift across the network during the observation period rather than as a physical model of the coastal-erosion signal itself; a process-based representation would require a longer record than is available to characterize the time-lagged component.

An additional step term for the October 2023 re-scraping was tested in each model and retained where it improved fit (ΔAIC &lt; −2). The scraping distance-decay length scale was fixed at λ = 300 m on physical grounds (Section 3.5.3); sensitivity to λ = 200 m and λ = 500 m was tested and reported. The inclusion of the scraping covariate is empirically motivated: a significant level shift in the BACI gap at the April 2015 scraping date was detected independently in the scraping analysis (Section 3.5.3). The propagation analysis (Section 4.5.5) found no detectable perturbation in the drainage coefficient at uphill wells; the level shift at WMC3 is nonetheless a genuine measured drawdown rather than a coincident climate signal, being reproduced at near-identical magnitude at both the April 2015 and October 2023 scrapings (Section 4.6.3). It is consistent with a head-level drawdown propagating toward the lowered scrape surface, which need not perturb a well's intrinsic drainage coefficient; the covariate is retained on this basis.

A non-linear extension of this model was also fitted as a sensitivity test of the climate-buffering hypothesis. The linear CWB × D\_fell interaction tests whether climate sensitivity changed after felling under the assumption that the BACI gap responds linearly to cumulative water balance; a buffering effect concentrated at climate extremes would, however, be largely averaged out by a single linear slope. The extension adds a centred CWB² term and a CWB² × D\_fell interaction to the Forest-control Impact and Edge specifications, allowing the post-felling climate response to take a curved form. It is fitted as a reported sensitivity variant only — the headline clearfell step and the linear model above are unaffected — and its results are given in Section 4.6.2.

This three-counterfactual design produces six primary ANCOVA results (three controls × two target tiers), each with its own clearfell step estimate, confidence interval, and model diagnostics. *The primary three-counterfactual ANCOVA-BACI analysis is implemented in 10a\_ancova\_baci.py. *

**Four-zone pooled-panel cross-check**

The three-counterfactual ANCOVA above fits the model six times — once for each pairing of a control definition with a target tier — and each fit has its own dependent variable, because the BACI gap is measured against a different control centroid each time. The six step estimates are consequently not directly comparable with one another: a contrast such as Impact relative to Edge cannot be read off by differencing two of them. As a cross-check in which every zone-to-zone contrast comes from a single consistent fit, a pooled-panel model was fitted alongside the principal ANCOVA.

The pooled-panel model is a single regression, fitted once to one combined dataset. Rather than analysing each zone in a separate model, the monthly water-table records of all wells in four zones are stacked into one long table — one row per well per month — and zone membership is carried as a categorical variable within that table. One of the four zones, the Forest control, is designated the reference level; the model then estimates how each of the other three zones behaves relative to that reference. A single fit therefore yields three felling steps at once — C3/Warren, Edge and Impact, each expressed as a difference from the Forest control — and because all three rest on the same baseline and the same fitted error structure, any further contrast between two zones (Impact relative to Edge, for example) follows by exact subtraction. This is the structural advantage over the six separate ANCOVAs: there, each fit has its own baseline and the steps cannot be differenced; here, one fit supplies a single internally consistent set of contrasts.

The four zones are the Forest control (the five unfelled C4 interior wells, the reference level); the Edge and Impact tiers as defined above; and a fourth zone of four open western-dune wells (CEH1, NW1, NW2, NW11), here termed C3/Warren. The C3/Warren zone is included as a second control: it lies more than 500 m from the felled compartment, on the western side away from the direction in which forest-management effects propagate, and is therefore expected to show no felling response — a zero result there confirms the design is sound rather than constituting a finding. The Coastal control wells are excluded from this model because they carry the coastal-erosion confound.

2\) Summer-window analysis and climate-collinearity sensitivity

The annual BACI step estimated from the ANCOVA above is computed over all monthly observations from the start of record to the analysis cutoff. Because the ecological consequences of any water table change concentrate in the summer months when slack inundation determines habitat viability, a parallel summer-window ANCOVA is fitted directly to June–September observations only, using the same model specification, control definitions, and covariates as the annual analysis. This directly-fitted summer ANCOVA provides a monthly-resolution estimate of the clearfell step over the ecologically critical period, without the seasonal scaling assumption implicit in extrapolating an annual estimate.

A sensitivity variant of the summer-window analysis is additionally fitted with the CWB covariate and the CWB × D\_fell interaction dropped from the design matrix. Within the restricted June–September window, the CWB covariate covaries more closely with the clearfell step indicator than it does in the annual window, because the summer-only data captures the part of the seasonal cycle in which the climate-balance trajectory and the post-2017 felling step share the most temporal overlap. This collinearity inflates the standard error on the clearfell step coefficient in the full-specification fit and may absorb a portion of the felling signal into the CWB term. The sensitivity variant tests how much the summer step estimate depends on the CWB specification; the interpretation of the two estimates and the mechanistic argument for the collinearity behaviour are presented in Section 4.6.3.

A separate annual summer-minimum analysis is also computed. For each well in the 17-well network, the annual summer minimum depth (June–September) was computed and the gap between the well's summer minimum and the control centroid summer minimum was tracked annually. The pre- versus post-felling shift in this gap was tested by Welch's t-test, and tier-mean shifts were computed for both forest and climate control centroids. A mixed-effects model with random intercept per well and fixed effects for post-felling and post-scraping indicators was fitted to each tier to provide a pooled clearfell step estimate with proper uncertainty:

*g**a**p* = *β*<sub>0</sub> + *β*<sub>1</sub> ⋅ *D*<sub>*f**e**l**l*</sub> + *β*<sub>2</sub> ⋅ *D*<sub>*s**c**r**a**p**e*</sub> + *u*<sub>*w**e**l**l*</sub> + *ϵ*

3\) Per-era SSM coefficient decomposition of the clearfell effect.

The clearfell intervention may have altered the recharge, atmospheric draw and drainage responses of the affected aquifer (Komatsu et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2015). To diagnose these changes mechanistically, the SSM coefficients β₁, β₂ and β₃ are estimated separately for the pre-intervention (Before) and post-intervention (After) eras at each well in the clearfell BACI network, and the differences Δβ₁ = β₁,After − β₁,Before, Δβ₂ = β₂,After − β₂,Before and Δβ₃ = β₃,After − β₃,Before are interpreted as the recharge, atmospheric draw and drainage shifts attributable to the intervention.

Both era-specific SSMs are fitted on the displacement formulation defined in Section 3.4, so β₃ is the displacement-storage coefficient (positive, Darcy-consistent) in both eras. The Before era is the record-length-balanced pre-intervention window, with an additional binary dummy in the design matrix at wells affected by the April 2015 scraping pulse to absorb that earlier intervention. The After era extends from the intervention date to the end of record. Both fits retain an intercept term, on the grounds that the era-mean residual carries information about non-mechanistic baseline shifts (background drift, instrument offsets, large-scale climate departures from the long-term mean) that the β coefficients themselves should not be forced to absorb.

The mechanistic prediction of the intervention effect, projected onto the post-intervention climate, is then computed at each well as:

$$\Delta{h\_{\mathit{predicted}} = \Delta}\beta\_{1}\cdot\overline{P\_{\mathit{after}}}-\Delta\beta\_{2}\cdot\overline{\mathit{PET}\_{\mathit{after}}}-\Delta\beta\_{3}\cdot\overline{h\_{\mathit{disp},\mathit{after}}}$$

where bar P\_after, bar PET\_after and bar h\_disp,after are the post-intervention era means of rainfall, PET and displacement above the drainage datum. This expression isolates the change in the climate-driven and drainage-driven response of the aquifer from the change in baseline level, by holding the climate forcing and head position at their post-intervention values and varying only the coefficients.

Δh\_predicted is compared to the observed BACI step Δh\_observed estimated from the ANCOVA model in Section 3.5.4. The Δh\_predicted formula captures only the β·X component of the era shift; the intercept difference Δα = α\_After − α\_Before absorbs the era-mean residual, which at most wells is the dominant component of the observed step. The predicted-versus-observed comparison is therefore informative about the mechanistic decomposition (Δβ₁ recharge, Δβ₂ atmospheric draw, Δβ₃ drainage) but should not be read as a complete reconstruction of the BACI step. The interpretation of the Δα residual — whether it represents genuinely non-mechanistic baseline drift, mis-specification of one of the β terms, or a structural state shift not captured by the time-invariant SSM — is taken up in Section 4.6.6.

Two additional supplementary analyses extend the mechanistic decomposition. The SSM residual normalization calibrates the SSM on the pre-felling record for each well, runs it forward iteratively using observed climate forcing, and normalizes the post-felling residuals (observed minus predicted) by subtracting the forest control mean residual; this provides a climate-independent test of clearfell-specific displacement and is presented in Section 4.6.6. A synthetic counterfactual for the Impact and Edge tiers is constructed from an optimally weighted combination of six donor wells calibrated on the pre-intervention period, with the gap between observed and synthetic post-felling providing an estimate of the intervention effect that does not rely on the ANCOVA parametric assumptions; this is also presented in Section 4.6.6. A clearfell transect analysis tests whether the post-felling response shows a spatial gradient consistent with a localized clearfell effect, comparing era-mean depths across a transect from the plantation interior to the felling core, and testing whether the broadleaf-canopy well diverges from the pine composite over time, rolling-window SSM coefficient tracking, and drainage diagnostic plots, with limitations of the NW10 broadleaf comparison discussed in Section 6.3.

A separate synthetic-extension analysis tests whether the FE1 and FE2 wells, sited inside or at the immediate edge of the clearfell compartment but excluded from the primary ANCOVA on the grounds of insufficient pre-scraping baseline, confirm the WMC3 Impact result. Their records were extended backwards using OLS donor regression onto the three Forest Control wells CEH34, CEH2, and CEH33, selected on the basis of proximity, shared canopy conditions, and pre-clearfell correlation (r &gt; 0.99 for both FE wells). The regression was calibrated on the pre-clearfell overlap window (July 2015 to November 2017, n = 29 months) and applied to hindcast FE well levels back to August 2010, providing a synthetic pre-scraping baseline. The synthetic hindcast was spliced with the actual FE observations to create a stable-composition impact centroid spanning all three eras (pre-scraping, post-scraping, post-felling). Three impact centroid variants were tested: (A) WMC3 + FE1 + FE2; (B) WMC3 + FE2; and (C) WMC3 alone. Each was run through the same ANCOVA framework as the primary analysis, against the Forest, Climate, and Combined control definitions. FE1 is caveated as sitting approximately 20 m outside the clearfell boundary in standing forest. Results are presented in Section 4.6.6.

## <span id="anchor-21"></span>Threshold Forecasting

To translate the observed spatial dynamics into predictive management tools, operational forecasting equations were derived using a multi-tiered mathematical framework. This approach combines continuous mass-balance modelling with seasonal prediction equations to quantify the aquifer's climatic vulnerability.

### <span id="anchor-22"></span>**Cluster-Level Mechanistic State-Space Equations** 

The cluster-level SSM coefficients derived in Section 3.4.1 were used to construct operational P\_flood threshold equations for each of the five principal clusters. The model was fitted with the intercept strictly forced to zero, ensuring that the coefficients represent a strict physical mass balance and providing the basis for algebraic inversion in Section 3.6.3.

### <span id="anchor-23"></span>Seasonal Prediction Equations

While the SSM operates on a continuous monthly basis, conservation management typically requires inter-seasonal risk forecasting — specifically, predicting winter flood peaks and summer drought minima from antecedent conditions. To address this, autoregressive prediction equations were fitted at a hydrological-year resolution for the three primary structural domains: Eastern Block, Western Block, and Forest clusters.

Two equations are fitted for each domain. The winter prediction equation predicts the peak winter water table (h\_peak) from cumulative winter precipitation and the antecedent summer minimum:

*h*<sub>*p**e**a**k*</sub> = *a* ⋅ *P*<sub>*w**i**n**t**e**r*</sub> + *b* ⋅ *h*<sub>*min* </sub> + *c*

The summer prediction equation predicts the summer minimum water table (h\_min) from cumulative summer precipitation and the antecedent winter peak:

*h*<sub>*min* </sub> = *a* ⋅ *P*<sub>*s**u**m**m**e**r*</sub> + *b* ⋅ *h*<sub>*max* , *w**i**n**t**e**r*</sub> + *c*

where P\_winter is cumulative precipitation over October–March, P\_summer is cumulative precipitation over April–September, h\_min is the preceding summer minimum water table depth, h\_{max,winter} is the preceding winter maximum water table depth, *a* is the fitted regression coefficient on the seasonal precipitation predictor, *b* is the fitted coefficient on the antecedent-state predictor, and *c* is a fitted intercept. The seasonal equations are AR(1)-style empirical regressions at hydrological-year resolution, providing operational forecasting at coarser temporal resolution than the monthly SSM described in Section 3.4. Data are organized by hydrological year commencing 1 October.

Head-dependent drainage (the −β₃·h(t−1) term in the SSM) is not fitted as a separate parameter at the seasonal scale: with one observation per hydrological year, drainage and the antecedent-state term are aliased, so drainage is absorbed implicitly into *b* and the fitted intercept *c* rather than appearing as a separate coefficient.

The antecedent-state terms (h\_min in the winter equation, h\_max in the summer equation) explicitly formalize the memory of the dune aquifer system — the degree to which a winter flood peak is conditioned by the depth of the preceding summer drought, and vice versa. This parameterization allows managers to construct simple conditional forecasts: given a known or projected winter rainfall total and the current summer water table position, the expected winter flood maximum can be estimated directly from the fitted equation without running the full SSM.

### <span id="anchor-24"></span>**Algebraic Derivation of the Critical Rainfall Threshold (P\_flood​)**

The most operationally significant metric for dune slack management is the critical rainfall threshold (P\_flood) — the cumulative winter rainfall depth required to raise the water table from its summer minimum to the elevation of the slack floor (h\_target, where depth to water table equals zero).

This threshold was derived by iterating the cluster-level state-space equation (Section 3.4.1) forward from the September summer minimum (h₀) over the months from October to each cluster's historical peak-of-record month. The horizon is therefore cluster-specific and reflects the observed timing of winter peak water table: five months (October–February) for C1 and C2, and six months (October–March) for C3, C4 and C5.

The monthly recurrence equation — applied once per step from October to the peak month — is:

*h*<sub>*t*</sub> = (1−*β*<sub>3</sub>) ⋅ *h*<sub>*t* − 1</sub> + *β*<sub>1</sub> ⋅ *P*<sub>*t*</sub> − *β*<sub>2</sub> ⋅ *E*<sub>*t*</sub> − *β*<sub>3</sub> ⋅ *D*

*(1) h\_t is the water level at the end of month t. The term (1 − β₃)·h\_{t−1} is how much of last month's displacement survives into this month — close to 1 for sluggish aquifers such as C4, lower for fast-draining ones such as C1. β₁·P\_t is the recharge contribution from this month's rainfall; β₂·E\_t is the atmospheric draw-down from PET; β₃·D is the drainage loss proportional to the fixed datum displacement. Monthly precipitation is expressed as P\_t = λ·P̄\_t, where P̄\_t is the long-term monthly mean rainfall from the RAF Valley reference climatology and λ is a multiplier representing the departure of a given winter from the long-term mean. Monthly PET is taken directly from the climatology (E\_t = Ē\_t) without scaling, reflecting the fact that winter PET varies considerably less year-to-year than winter rainfall at this latitude.*

Letting α = 1 − β₃, iterating the recurrence forward n months from h₀ yields the closed form:

$${h\_{n} = h\_{0}}\cdot{\alpha^{n} + \beta\_{1}}\cdot{\sum\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\alpha^{n-i}}\cdot\lambda\cdot{\overline{P}}\_{i}-\beta\_{2}\cdot{\sum\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\alpha^{n-i}}\cdot{\overline{E}}\_{i}-D\cdot\left( {1-\alpha^{n}} \right)$$

*(2) h\_n is the water level after n months of winter recharge. Rather than stepping through the equation n times, this single expression accumulates the effect of all n monthly steps at once. The term h₀·αⁿ is how much of the starting summer deficit survives n months of drainage — for a C2 well with α = 0.93, roughly 71% of the September deficit persists to February. The two summation terms accumulate the rainfall benefit and PET cost month by month, with the weighting α^{n−i} giving greater influence to earlier months because they have more remaining winter months to amplify their contribution. The final term D·(1−αⁿ) is the cumulative drainage loss over the horizon.*

Setting h\_n = h\_target and solving for λ:

$$\lambda = \frac{h\_{\mathit{target}}-h\_{0}\cdot{\alpha^{n} + \beta\_{2}}\cdot{S\_{E} + D}\cdot\left( {1-\alpha^{n}} \right)}{\beta\_{1}\cdot S\_{P}}$$

*(3) λ is the rainfall multiplier needed to raise the water table to the slack floor from a given summer minimum h₀. The numerator is everything the aquifer must overcome: the starting deficit (−h₀·αⁿ), the cumulative PET demand (β₂·S\_E), and the drainage losses (D·(1−αⁿ)). The denominator is the drainage-weighted rainfall the winter can supply at climatological rates (β₁·S\_P). Here S\_P = Σα^{n−i}·P̄\_i and S\_E = Σα^{n−i}·Ē\_i are the drainage-weighted sums of climatological rainfall and PET over the horizon. A λ of 1.0 means an exactly average winter suffices; λ = 1.5 means a winter 50% wetter than average is needed.*

The critical rainfall threshold then follows directly:

$${P\_{\mathit{flood}} = \lambda}\cdot{\sum\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\overline{P\_{i}}}$$

*(4) P\_flood is λ converted from a dimensionless multiplier into a depth in millimetres — the number a site manager can compare directly against a seasonal rainfall forecast or a historical percentile. For example, a C2 well with a September minimum of 0.5 m below ground requires λ = 1.05, giving P\_flood ≈ 448 mm — a near-average October–February total. A well requiring λ = 2.5 has never received sufficient winter rainfall in the 95-year RAF Valley record.*

Because the weighting α^{n−i} places greater emphasis on early-winter rainfall than on late-winter rainfall, the formulation encodes the physical fact that October recharge has more months to contribute to the peak than March recharge does, modulated by the cluster-specific drainage coefficient β₃. Three natural classes of well emerge from this framework: those whose required λ is less than unity (P\_flood reachable under sub-average winters), those requiring 1.0 ≤ λ &lt; 2.5 (reachable only in wetter-than-average winters of varying rarity), and those for which λ ≥ 2.5 (effectively unreachable under the present climate). This classification is applied spatially across the dipwell network in Section 4.

This algebraic inversion provides site managers with a dynamic, site-specific forecasting tool. By entering a measured summer minimum dipwell reading (h₀) into the cluster's equation, a manager obtains both the cumulative winter rainfall (mm) required to raise that well to the slack-floor threshold, and its expression as a multiple of climatological winter rainfall (λ) — a direct measure of ecological flooding feasibility in the subsequent recharge season.

### <span id="anchor-25"></span>Long-Term Climate Trajectory and Threshold Exceedance Analysis

To assess the long-term trajectory of slack hydrological viability, annual summer minima and winter maxima were extracted for all five clusters from the cluster-centroid time series generated during the clustering analysis. C4 and C5, both forested clusters whose water tables sit below the slack viability thresholds, are included for completeness to characterize the full network trajectory and to support future analysis of forest management effects on seasonal extremes. The hydrological year was defined as 1 October to 30 September, consistent with Section 3.6.2, and a minimum of three valid monthly observations per season was required for a year to be included.

For summer minima, ordinary least squares linear regression was fitted to the annual series for each cluster over the 2004–2026 observation window and extrapolated to 2040, with 95% confidence intervals derived analytically. Summer trajectories were plotted against the eco-hydrological viability thresholds established by Curreli et al. (2013): −0.61 m for wet slack communities (SD15b) and −0.98 m for dry slack communities (SD16).

The uncertainty in the projected year at which each cluster's summer-minimum trend crosses the Curreli et al. (2013) SD15b and SD16 thresholds was quantified by a year-resampling bootstrap. The annual summer-minimum series for each cluster was resampled with replacement (n = 1000 replicates); the ordinary least squares trend was refitted to each replicate; and the crossing year for each threshold was extracted from the refitted trend. The central estimate is the median of the resulting distribution of crossing years, and the 90% confidence interval is given by its 5th and 95th percentiles. Because the interval is generated from the resampled trend rather than from an analytical slope variance, it reflects both slope uncertainty and the year-to-year variability of the summer-minimum series, and is consequently wider than a parametric trend-confidence projection. The bootstrap captures sampling uncertainty in the trend slope and intercept but not model-form uncertainty — in particular the assumption that the linear trend extrapolates cleanly into a regime where the summer minimum may approach a drainage-controlled basement — so the resulting horizon should be read as an upper bound on viability rather than a calibrated forecast. *The procedure is implemented in 14b\_year\_of\_crossing.py.*

For winter maxima, no reliable projection method was identified — direct linear regression on annual winter maxima produced R² values below 0.03 across all clusters, and transfer function projection via the seasonal models of Section 3.6.2 produced physically implausible trajectories for the eastern clusters due to extrapolation beyond the fitted range. The winter analysis therefore presents the observed record only. Threshold exceedance frequency was calculated for each cluster as the proportion of hydrological years in which the winter maximum exceeded the wet slack flooding threshold of −0.10 m (SD15b) and the dry slack threshold of −0.25 m (SD16), providing a direct measure of current ecological flooding adequacy for each structural domain.

*Critical rainfall threshold equations, peak flood prediction equations, and summer drought prediction equations were implemented in 11\_forecasting\_thresholds.py. Climate trajectory projections and threshold exceedance analysis are implemented in 14\_climate\_projections.py.*

### **Spatial Distribution of Threshold Metrics**

The cluster-level threshold framework of Sections 3.6.3 and 3.6.4 is complemented by a set of per-well spatial fields that render the eco-hydrological status of every dipwell across the classified network. Four metrics are mapped. The mean summer-minimum depth is computed at each well from its annual minimum above-Ordnance-Datum water level over the August–September window, requiring at least one valid observation per year, and converted to depth below ground by subtracting the level from the LiDAR ground elevation. The mean winter-maximum depth is computed analogously from the annual maximum over the October-to-March window, requiring at least three valid observations in that window for a year to be admitted, and converted to depth below ground on the same convention. The critical rainfall threshold P\_flood is evaluated at each well by applying the iterated closed-form solution of Section 3.6.3 to that well's observed mean summer minimum, using per-well state-space coefficients at the reference wells and cluster-centroid coefficients at the extended wells assigned through the Pearson affinity audit (Section 3.3). The winter flooding frequency is computed at each well having at least five years of winter record as the percentage of hydrological years in which the winter-maximum water table reached or exceeded the local ground surface, taking the LiDAR ground elevation as the surface datum.

Aggregating on above-Ordnance-Datum elevations renders the summer-minimum and winter-maximum metrics invariant to the ground-surface lowering produced by scraping at treatment wells; the surface change is carried instead by the depth-below-ground conversion, which applies the post-intervention ground elevation. Each per-well field is interpolated to the standard site grid by inverse-distance weighting and masked across the dune ridges where the elevation model exceeds the interpolated water surface, on the convention described in Section 3.8. The summer-minimum, winter-maximum and flooding-frequency surfaces are referenced to the eco-hydrological thresholds of Curreli et al. (2013). The spatial fields are presented in Section 4.7.

*The per-well threshold metrics and their spatial fields are implemented in 11b\_spatial\_thresholds.py.*

## <span id="anchor-26"></span>Characterizing Water Table Losses and Long-Term Trajectories

### <span id="anchor-27"></span>**Depth-Dependent Evapotranspiration Model** 

To test whether the atmospheric draw coefficient varies significantly with water table depth — and whether a depth-dependent formulation is warranted over the standard fixed-β₂ SSM — an extended model was implemented in which β₂ was replaced by the depth-dependent term β₂ · exp(−λ · d), where d is the depth of the water table below ground surface (metres) and λ is a decay parameter (m⁻¹) controlling the rate at which evapotranspiration influence declines as the capillary fringe retreats from the root zone. Depth below ground surface was computed at each timestep as d = max(−h, 0), where h is the water table position (negative convention). No upstand term enters: h is already referenced to the ground surface (Section 3.1.1). The floor at zero keeps d a physical distance below ground when a slack is ponded. Because the modified predictor −exp(−λ · d) · PET remains linear in β₂ for any fixed λ, the three coefficients β₁, β₂ and β₃ were recovered by no-intercept OLS at each value of λ. A systematic grid search over λ ∈ \[0, 6\] m⁻¹ at intervals of 0.05 m⁻¹ was conducted independently for each cluster, with the optimal λ selected by maximizing iterative simulation NSE. Setting λ = 0 recovers the standard SSM exactly, providing a direct baseline for comparison.

Despite the statistical improvement that the grid search produces (Section 4.4), the depth-dependent formulation introduces a nonlinearity that would propagate through the threshold equations (Section 4.7), forestry scenarios (Section 4.9), and forecasting tools. The standard SSM's fixed β₂ provides an effective depth-averaged parameterization that preserves the closed-form analytical framework on which these operational outputs depend. The depth-dependent formulation was therefore not adopted for the primary analysis; the standard fixed-β₂ SSM is retained throughout. The fixed β₂ should be understood as absorbing a real depth-coupling effect whose magnitude varies across clusters; its diagnostic significance is discussed further in Section 5.6.1.

*The depth-dependent PET grid search and benchmark comparison are implemented in 15\_depth\_dependent\_pet.py.*

### <span id="anchor-28"></span>**Mean Monthly Water Balance Decomposition**

Water balance decomposition of the kind applied here — partitioning monthly head change into recharge, evaporative draw, drainage and residual — has a long history in the coastal dune hydrology literature, particularly in the Dutch and North Sea coastal systems where managed water tables and dune slack conservation drive analogous restoration questions (Stuyfzand, 1993; Grootjans et al., 2002, 2017). The decomposition applied in this study derives the component fluxes directly from the fitted SSM coefficients, allowing direct comparison of fluxes across clusters subject to identical climate forcing using only the dipwell and climate records already available for the site.

To quantify and compare the hydrological inputs and losses operating on each hydrogeological cluster, a mean monthly water balance decomposition was applied to the five principal clusters (C1–C5) using cluster-level SSM coefficients derived in Section 3.4.1. All terms are expressed in metres of water table change per month (m/month), the natural unit of the state-space model and directly comparable across clusters receiving the same climate forcing.

The decomposition isolates three components from the canonical state-space equation (Section 3.4):

*Δ**h* = *β*<sub>1</sub> ⋅ *P* − *β*<sub>2</sub> ⋅ *P**E**T* − *β*<sub>3</sub> ⋅ *h*<sub>*d**i**s**p*</sub>

Mean monthly recharge was computed as β₁ × P̄ , where P̄ is the mean monthly precipitation over the monitoring period from the RAF Valley record. Atmospheric draw was computed as β₂ × PET̄ , where PET̄ is the mean monthly Thornthwaite PET. Gravity drainage was computed as β₃ × h̄ \_disp, where h̄ \_disp is the mean displacement above the drainage datum (h̄ \_disp = D + h̄ , with D = 3.7 m; see Section 3.4) for each cluster derived from the cluster-average hydrograph. Canopy interception is not included as a separate loss term — it is implicitly captured in the forested clusters' (C4 and C5) lower β₁ and lower β₂, and adding it explicitly would double-count losses already embedded in the fitted coefficients. Total losses are therefore the sum of atmospheric draw and drainage only. The net monthly balance — recharge minus total losses — quantifies the water balance residual — the flux that would be required from some unmodelled source to maintain long-run water table equilibrium.

An indicative volumetric conversion is presented alongside the primary head-space decomposition (Table 4b; Figure 11a), using assumed specific yield values: C1 = 0.08 (shallow sand over glacial till) and C2–C5 = 0.12 (deeper clean dune sand), consistent with the literature range for unconfined sandy aquifer materials (Fetter, 2001). Empirical upper bounds on Sy derived from the WTF method are presented in Section 3.7.3 and Table 4c, and used to bracket the volumetric estimates from above; the partition uncertainty range between the two methods is shown as a hatched band in Figure 11b. For the forested clusters (C4 and C5), canopy interception is shown as an explicit segment on the inputs bar of Figure 11a, computed as 0.24 × P̄ following Freeman (2008), reducing the net input rather than being additive.

*The mean monthly water balance decomposition and volumetric conversion were produced by 16\_water\_bal.py (outputs: 16\_water\_bal\_table.csv, 16\_water\_bal\_vol\_table.csv).*

### <span id="anchor-29"></span>**Specific Yield Estimation: Water Table Fluctuation Method**

To provide an empirical upper bound on the specific yield (Sy) values used in the volumetric water balance conversion (Section 3.7.2; Table 4b), an independent estimation was performed using the water table fluctuation (WTF) method of Healy and Cook (2002):

$${S\_{y} = \frac{R}{\Delta}}h$$

where R is the net recharge reaching the water table (m) and Δh is the corresponding water table rise (m).

Three complementary derivations of Sy are reported. Approach A (through-origin OLS) fits a linear regression of corrected water-table rise against net recharge for each cluster over the winter months (November–March, PET &lt; 25 mm/month), with the regression forced through the origin so that the slope is the cluster-mean Sy. The water-table rise is corrected for storage decay by adding the drainage component β₃·|h\_prev| derived from the SSM, isolating the recharge-driven rise from the contemporaneous drainage loss. Approach B (event median) computes a per-month Sy = R / Δh for each rising-limb event in the record (months where Δh &gt; 5 mm and net recharge P − PET &gt; 10 mm), with event-level estimates further constrained to the physically plausible range 0.01 &lt; Sy &lt; 0.50 to exclude months where the suppressed or negative Δh signal produced implausibly high individual Sy values; the cluster-mean Sy is reported as the median of the event distribution with interquartile range as the uncertainty bound. Approach C (rapid recharge events, after Crosbie et al., 2005) is a third, methodologically independent estimator: rather than correcting for drainage (as Approach A does through β₃) or ignoring it (as Approach B does), it selects short, sharply-rising episodes in which drainage during the rise is negligible by construction. A candidate episode requires at least two consecutive prior months of falling or static water table (Δh ≤ 0), begins on the first rising month, extends for at most two further months while the rise continues, and must accumulate a cumulative rise of at least 50 mm; the per-episode Sy is the accumulated net recharge over the episode divided by the cumulative rise, filtered to the same 0.01–0.50 plausibility range as Approach B, with the cluster estimate taken as the median of qualifying episodes and a 1000-sample bootstrap 95% confidence interval. Forest clusters carry the same Freeman (2008) interception correction as Approach B. Approach B is the canonical figure used by downstream water-balance scripts; Approaches A and C are reported alongside as independent methods cross-checks and neither propagates to any downstream calculation. Agreement among the three approaches on the cluster ordering is taken as evidence that the specific-yield gradient is robust to the choice of estimation strategy, while divergences at individual clusters are interpreted as diagnostic of the estimators' differing sensitivities rather than as a ranking between them.

Both approaches are applied to the cluster-mean hydrographs derived in Section 3.4.1. Monthly data cannot isolate individual storm events, and the WTF method at monthly resolution is known to overestimate effective Sy because it cannot separate true gravity drainage from capillary fringe release (Healy and Cook, 2002; Scanlon et al., 2002). The resulting estimates should therefore be interpreted as upper bounds on Sy rather than point estimates of the long-term effective value.

For the Forest clusters (C4 and C5), a correction was applied for canopy interception prior to computing net recharge:

*R*<sub>*e**f**f*</sub> = (1−0.24) ⋅ *P* − *P**E**T*

following the site-specific interception fraction of Freeman (2008). Only the precipitation term is reduced. The PET term is retained unchanged because Thornthwaite PET is an energy-based atmospheric demand derived from temperature and day length, independent of land cover; it represents the capacity of the atmosphere to receive water vapour rather than the realized evaporative flux from any particular surface. Reducing PET to account for canopy-evaporated intercepted water would conflate atmospheric demand with actual evapotranspiration and remove the same quantity from both sides of the water balance. Without the interception correction, months in which canopy suppression of Δh is most pronounced produce individual Sy estimates above the plausibility threshold and are excluded from the event pool; applying the correction recovers these months, increases the event count, and lowers the event-median Sy at both forest clusters by repopulating the event pool with below-median values (see Section 4.2.4 and Table 4c). Approach A interception-corrected variants were tested but found to be unstable in this dataset, and are not reported; the Approach B forest values cited in Section 4.2.4 use the corrected event pool.

Results of the cluster-level and well-level WTF analyses are presented in Section 4.2.4. *The cluster-level analysis was implemented in 17\_wtf\_specific\_yield.py and the well-level spatial analysis in 18\_wtf\_spatial.py.*

### Network-Scale Coastal-Retreat Gradient Regression

To partition each cluster's observed summer-minimum decline into a climate-driven component and a coastal-retreat-driven component, and to provide an independent check on the BACI ANCOVA's easting × time covariate (Section 3.5.4), a non-linear panel regression was fitted to the full 21-year monthly record. Each well's perpendicular distance to the eroding Caernarfon Bay High Water Mark was computed within the pipeline from a committed OpenStreetMap mean-high-water coastline (EPSG:27700) restricted to the 15 km west-facing Newborough shoreline; the Menai Strait and Llanddwyn Island were excluded as non-eroding boundaries.

Two functional forms were fitted: a Dupuit–Forchheimer steady-state strip-aquifer form,

*δ*(*d*) = *max* (*δ*<sub>0</sub>⋅(1−*d*/*L*),0) + *c*

and an exponential decay form,

*δ*(*d*) = *δ*<sub>0</sub> ⋅ exp (−*d*/*L*) + *c*

where d is distance from the coast, δ\_0 is the coast-edge water-table deepening rate above climate, L is the inland reach, and c is the far-field climate background. Both were fitted by profile non-linear least squares, with well and month fixed effects and a cumulative-water-balance covariate absorbed by within-well demeaning. Model selection between the two functional forms used the Akaike Information Criterion. Three nested specifications were fitted to test robustness to forest-cover inclusion: the full network (72 wells, all clusters minus the BACI clearfell-zone wells), a forest-free specification dropping all C4 and C5 wells, and a C3-only specification using the 17 non-forested C3 wells alone. The forest-free linear-with-cutoff fit is taken as the headline.

The headline fit is then applied to two downstream questions: per-cluster decomposition of the long-term summer-minimum slope into gradient and climate components (Section 4.8.1), and corroboration of the BACI ANCOVA's easting × time coefficient against the model's predicted differential coastal-retreat signal between each impact zone and each control tier (Section 4.6.1).

*The network-scale coastal-retreat gradient regression is implemented in 25\_coastal\_gradient.py.*

### **Five-Year Mean Spring Water Level (MSL5) and Associated Forecasting Tools**

The seasonal prediction equations of Section 3.6.2 and the critical rainfall threshold of Section 3.6.3 operate in summer-minimum and winter-peak space, against the eco-hydrological thresholds of Curreli et al. (2013). Van Willegen et al. (2025) revisited the eco-hydrological calibration for the Newborough dune slacks using a larger vegetation dataset and a wider set of candidate hydrological metrics, and identified the unweighted five-year mean spring water level as the best-performing predictor of community-mean vegetation moisture response. This metric, here denoted MSL5, is computed across the monitoring network as a complement to the summer-minimum framework. Three analytical components are described in turn: the MSL5 metric itself, an empirical transfer function that forecasts the next spring's mean level, and a climate-scenario projection of the metric.

**The MSL5 metric.** For each well, the annual mean spring water level for hydrological year *y* is the unweighted mean of the March, April and May readings of that year. To place the entire antecedent forcing window within a single hydrological year, the van Willegen hydrological year is defined as 1 June (*y*−1) to 31 May (*y*) — distinct from the October-commencing hydrological year used for the seasonal equations of Section 3.6.2, and adopted here so that the spring response window and its winter and spring antecedents share one annual unit. The five-year mean spring water level MSL5, indexed by its closing year, is the unweighted mean of five consecutive annual spring means. Two completeness rules are applied: all three spring months must be present for an annual spring mean to be admitted, and all five constituent annual means must be valid for a five-year mean to be admitted; windows containing any excluded annual mean are themselves excluded rather than bridged. The metric is computed at every well with sufficient record and aggregated to cluster level as the unweighted mean of member-well values. Levels are reported below ground surface, the convention of the source study, which the ground-referenced records of Section 3.1.1 already provide without further correction.

**The spring MSL transfer function.** To allow the coming spring's mean level to be forecast before the season closes, an empirical transfer function was fitted independently for each of the five clusters, predicting the annual spring mean from antecedent winter and spring conditions:

*M**S**L*<sub>*y*</sub> = *α* ⋅ *h*<sub>*max* , *w**i**n**t**e**r*</sub> + *β* ⋅ *P*<sub>*w**i**n* − *s**p**r*</sub> + *γ* ⋅ *P**E**T*<sub>*w**i**n* − *s**p**r*</sub> + *c*

where
*M**S**L*<sub>*y*</sub>
is the cluster-centroid mean of the March–May water levels in hydrological year *y*;
*h*<sub>*max* , *w**i**n**t**e**r*</sub>
is the cluster-centroid maximum head over October (*y*−1) to February (*y*);
*P*<sub>*w**i**n* − *s**p**r*</sub>
and
*P**E**T*<sub>*w**i**n* − *s**p**r*</sub>
are cumulative precipitation and Thornthwaite PET over October (*y*−1) to May (*y*); and
*c*
is a fitted intercept. The coefficients are fitted by ordinary least squares with an intercept term, using the cluster-centroid monthly series — the same baseline against which the cluster state-space coefficients of Section 3.4.1 are fitted, ensuring internal consistency between the empirical transfer function and the mechanistic model. The winter peak enters as the antecedent-state term and carries the aquifer memory between seasons, in the same role as the antecedent terms in the Section 3.6.2 seasonal equations. The transfer function gives managers a forecast of the coming spring mean from readings already in hand by late winter, which can be combined with the rolling record of recent observed annual means to update the MSL5 statistic ahead of the end-of-spring readings.

**Climate-scenario projection.** To place the MSL5 metric in the context of projected climate change, a perturbation overlay was computed under UKCP18 RCP8.5 central-estimate scenarios for the 2050s and 2080s. For each cluster and scenario, a monthly water-table perturbation is computed from the cluster's state-space coefficients and the scenario climate departure:

*Δ**h*(*m*) = *β*<sub>1</sub> ⋅ (*P*<sub>*s**c**e**n*</sub>(*m*)−*P*<sub>*b**a**s**e*</sub>(*m*)) − *β*<sub>2</sub> ⋅ (*P**E**T*<sub>*s**c**e**n*</sub>(*m*)−*P**E**T*<sub>*b**a**s**e*</sub>(*m*))

where
*β*<sub>1</sub>
and
*β*<sub>2</sub>
are the cluster recharge and atmospheric-draw coefficients of Section 3.4.1;
*P*<sub>*b**a**s**e*</sub>(*m*)
and
*P**E**T*<sub>*b**a**s**e*</sub>(*m*)
are the long-term monthly climatology over the monitoring period; and
*P*<sub>*s**c**e**n*</sub>(*m*)
and
*P**E**T*<sub>*s**c**e**n*</sub>(*m*)
apply seasonal UKCP18 multipliers to that climatology. This is the seasonal-perturbation formulation also used in the forestry scenario analysis (Section 3.5.4 and the scenario evaluation of the pipeline), expressing a scenario as a departure from baseline climate rather than as an equilibrium displacement. Because the perturbation is linear in precipitation and PET and the scenario multipliers are a stationary shift in climatology rather than an interannual sequence, the projected change in MSL5 is a single constant per cluster and scenario — the mean of the perturbations over the three spring months — and the projected MSL5 trajectory is the observed trajectory shifted vertically by that constant. The projection is therefore a climatological response under stationary observed variability: it characterizes the sensitivity of MSL5 to a shift in mean climate, and is not a forward-in-time forecast of the level that will be observed in any particular future year.

*The MSL5 metric and its forecasting and projection companions sit alongside, and do not replace, the summer-minimum framework of Sections 3.6 and 4.8; the two measure the same multi-year hydrological state through different seasonal windows. The MSL5 aggregation is implemented in 26\_van\_willegen\_msl.py; the spring transfer function in Section 5 of 11\_forecasting\_thresholds.py; and the UKCP18 projection in 26b\_van\_willegen\_msl\_projections.py.*

###  Equilibrium Wetness Index and Vegetation Cross-Validation

The MSL5 metric of Section 3.7.5 measures spring wetness directly from the water-level record; the state-space model of Section 3.4.1 offers a structural counterpart computed from the fitted coefficients. Section 3.7.5 sets aside the equilibrium-displacement form of the model in favour of a seasonal perturbation for climate projection; here that same equilibrium form is used not for projection but to define a static wetness index, calibrated against MSL5 and cross-validated against independent vegetation data.

**The equilibrium wetness index. **Setting the mean monthly change to zero in the state-space model (Section 3.4.1) and solving the head-dependent drainage term for the steady-state displacement gives h\_disp,eq = (β₁·P̄ − β₂·PET̄)/β₃, whence the equilibrium wetness index EWI = h\_disp,eq − d, reported below ground surface to match the MSL5 convention; the records being ground-referenced no upstand term is applied, the records being ground-referenced (Section 3.1.1). Here β₁, β₂, β₃ are the per-well recharge, atmospheric-draw and drainage coefficients; d is the drainage datum (3.7 m); and P̄, PET̄ are the full-record monthly-mean precipitation and Thornthwaite PET — the same long-term climatology used as the projection baseline in Section 3.7.5. Because the forcing is the long-term normal rather than a specific observation window, the index is climate-window-independent, estimating a well's intrinsic wetness from its response coefficients alone and computable from a shorter record than MSL5 requires. Two network tiers are distinguished: reference wells take the quality-controlled coefficients of Section 3.4.1, while extended wells are fitted with the same shared state-space routine as a single pass without that control, and flagged accordingly. Wells whose drainage coefficient is near zero or negative (CEH13, CEH14) are excluded, that coefficient being the denominator of the equilibrium expression.

**Uncertainty in the index.** The equilibrium expression is a ratio, so uncertainty in the fitted coefficients propagates into the index. Because the drainage datum d is a constant, subtracting it shifts the equilibrium displacement without changing its uncertainty; the propagation is therefore anchored on h\_disp,eq itself rather than on the datum-referenced index value, which would understate the error at wells whose equilibrium level lies close to the datum. Two variants are computed. The first propagates the drainage coefficient alone: since h\_disp,eq is inversely proportional to β₃, the relative error in β₃ passes directly into the index, and this dominant term is the variant quoted below. The second is a first-order propagation over all three coefficients treated as independent — a close approximation rather than an exact joint interval, given the mild collinearity between the recharge and atmospheric-draw terms. Standard errors for reference wells are recovered from the committed coefficient, its two-sided p-value and the sample size, the t statistic inverting exactly for the no-intercept three-predictor model; extended-tier fits return standard errors directly from the shared fitting routine.

**Calibration onto the spring-level scale. **The index sits on a systematically deeper scale than the observed spring level and is calibrated onto the MSL5 scale by ordinary least squares, MSL5 = a + b·EWI, across the wells carrying both. Consistent with MSL5's open-dune character (Section 4.8.4) and with the forest coefficients being the least constrained on the site (Sections 3.4.4, 4.9.2), the calibration is scoped to the open-dune network (clusters C1–C3); forest wells (C4, C5) are predicted but reported out of scope. Cluster membership for reference wells follows Section 3.4.1, and for extended wells the Pearson affinity analysis of Section 3.3.

**Vegetation cross-validation. **To test both metrics against the ecological target directly, the co-located Ellenberg-F moisture indicator values (the wetness indicator, scale 1–12; Hill et al., 1999) of van Willegen et al. (2025) were obtained from the associated open dataset (van Willegen et al., 2024) and aggregated to a mean per piezometer. Observed MSL5 and the equilibrium index were each regressed on mean Ellenberg-F between wells, and the difference between the two correlations — dependent, since they share the response variable — was tested by Williams' test (Williams, 1959). This cross-validation draws on an external dataset and is computed once rather than as part of the recurring pipeline.

The equilibrium wetness index and its MSL5 comparison are implemented in 26\_van\_willegen\_msl.py; the vegetation cross-validation uses the van Willegen open dataset.

## <span id="anchor-30"></span>Computational Methods and Software Implementation

### <span id="anchor-31"></span>Data Pre-processing and Time-Series Management 

All data pre-processing, statistical modelling, and spatial mapping were executed within a Python programming environment. Time-series data manipulation, interpolation of missing temporal records, and standardization were performed using the pandas and NumPy libraries.

*Data preparation including quality control, gap-filling and the canonical geometry derivation is implemented in 01\_data\_prep.py; baseline climate figures and network summary statistics are produced by 00\_climate\_summary.py.*

### Statistical and Predictive Modelling 

The core parameterization of the aquifer mechanics was executed using the statsmodels module. For the continuous state-space modelling (Section 3.4), the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) algorithm was constrained to fit through the origin (omitting the intercept constant) to enforce physical mass balance. Conversely, seasonal prediction equations predicting peak flood and summer drought minima (Section 3.6.2) used unconstrained OLS regressions. The statsmodels architecture was further used to extract 95% confidence intervals and p-values for all SSM and ANCOVA coefficients, including the era-specific β₃ regressions of Section 3.5.3 and the ANCOVA-BACI β coefficients of Section 3.5.4. Iterative forecasting metrics, including the Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), were computed via NumPy array operations to benchmark the state-space model against traditional regression approaches (Section 3.4.2).

*State-space model fitting and the shared SSM utility functions used by all downstream scripts are implemented in utils/model\_utils.py. Shared pipeline constants — including DRAINAGE\_DATUM, HEADLINE\_LAG, FOREST\_INTERCEPTION, and the canonical cluster labels and colours — are defined in utils/config.py.*

### Geospatial Processing and Cartographic Visualization 

The integration of the topographical data with the monitoring network was managed through a dedicated spatial stack. GeoPandas and Fiona were used to parse external KML feature boundaries (e.g. the clearfell perimeter and hydrological boundaries) and standardize all spatial geometries to the British National Grid coordinate reference system (EPSG:27700).

The high-resolution Digital Elevation Model (DEM) was ingested and masked using the rasterio library. To visualize the localized dune topography effectively, the DEM arrays were rendered using a dual-slope normalization technique (TwoSlopeNorm) provided by matplotlib, anchoring the visual transition at specific elevation thresholds to highlight critical hydrological features. Contextual basemaps were fetched via contextily when necessary, and the label placement and occlusion avoidance was handled using the adjustText library.

*Spatial coefficient mapping and the SSM spatial fits (Section 3.4.3) are implemented in 07\_spatial\_coefficients.py. Shared geospatial utilities used across the spatial analyses are in utils/paths.py (output path conventions) and utils/map\_utils.py (map rendering helpers).*

### Spatial Groundwater Analysis

A well-level scenario framework was implemented to translate the per-well SSM parameterization into site-wide spatial summaries of head change (Δh) under prescribed climate and forest management perturbations. For each well, the scenario head change relative to baseline was computed directly from the per-well SSM equation as:

*Δ**h* = (*β*<sub>1</sub>⋅*P*<sub>*e**f**f*, *s**c*</sub>−*β*<sub>2, *s**c*</sub>⋅*P**E**T*<sub>*s**c*</sub>−*β*<sub>3</sub>⋅*h*<sub>*d**i**s**p*</sub>) − (*β*<sub>1</sub>⋅*P*<sub>*e**f**f*, 0</sub>−*β*<sub>2</sub>⋅*P**E**T*<sub>0</sub>−*β*<sub>3</sub>⋅*h*<sub>*d**i**s**p*</sub>)

where P\_eff applies the relevant canopy interception factor at forest wells (C4 and C5; 24% under the Corsican pine baseline following Freeman (2008); 15% annual mean under broadleaf conversion following Komatsu et al. (2011); 12% under 50% thinning; 0% under full clearfell), and β₂,sc applies a scenario-specific multiplier at forest wells. For full clearfell, the multiplier is derived dynamically from the BACI-corrected Edge-tier post-felling β₂ ratio reported in Section 4.6.6 (Edge mean ratio minus Climate Control mean drift, plus 1.0). The Edge tier is used as a conservative upper bound: although Edge wells were not directly felled, they recorded the largest tier-mean β₂ increase of any near-felling tier, while the directly-felled Impact well showed a smaller ratio not statistically distinguishable from the Climate Control drift. Adopting the Edge ratio therefore yields a precautionary upper bound on canopy-removal-attributable β₂ change. The thinning multiplier is set as half the clearfell perturbation.

Under broadleaf conversion, β₂ is scaled seasonally with phenological windows: a leaf-on (May–October) multiplier applied above the Corsican pine baseline, and a leaf-off (November–April) multiplier applied below it, reflecting the seasonally asymmetric transpiration signature of deciduous canopy compared to the year-round pine. The two phenological multipliers are estimated from the BACI Edge-tier monthly β₂ profile under the assumption that broadleaf canopy approaches the leaf-off canopy state in winter and a moderate-transpiration state in summer. Canonical values and their derivation are presented in Section 4.9.7. The same clearfell multiplier is applied at both C4 and C5 forest wells; no felled C5 wells were available in the BACI design, so the C5 application represents extrapolation from the C4 near-felling signal rather than direct measurement.

The β₃·h\_disp term is identical in the scenario and baseline expressions and cancels in the difference. Specific yield Sy does not appear in the equation because the fitted β coefficients already embed the aquifer storage response through the SSM fit to observed head data; the per-well WTF-derived specific yield values (Section 3.7.3) are retained as supplementary display information in the interactive scenario viewer, not as a divisor on Δh.

Six scenarios were evaluated: baseline; UKCP18 2050s (winter P +10%, summer P −15%, winter PET +5%, summer PET +20%, RCP8.5 central estimate for Wales, informed by the UKCP18 Regional 12 km ensemble of Met Office (2018) and the CHESS-SCAPE bias-corrected projections of Robinson et al., 2023); UKCP18 2080s (winter P +20%, summer P −30%, winter PET +10%, summer PET +35%, same sources); full clearfell; 50% thinning; and broadleaf conversion. Climate scenarios were evaluated per season, with the annual Δh reported as the 0.5-weighted mean of the winter and summer equilibrium responses.

The per-well Δh field was interpolated to a site-wide 50 m grid on the British National Grid (EPSG:27700) using Delaunay triangulation with linear barycentric weighting (scipy.interpolate.griddata, method="linear"), masked to the rectangular sea-boundary extent; the resulting field is rendered in the interactive scenario viewer described below rather than as a static map. Two wells were excluded from the interpolation: CEH12 (bedrock-anchored, geologically distinct from the dune aquifer) and CEH15 (forest slack edge, transitional between Forest and Western Residual clusters). The hydraulic conductivity reported for the site (K = 6.0 m/day; Betson et al., 2002) is retained as directional context for the Darcy flow vectors shown in Figure 57 and does not enter the Δh calculation.

The accompanying interactive scenario viewer (Hollingham, 2026b; scenario\_viewer.html) renders the same per-well Δh field using inverse-distance-weighted interpolation (power 1, eight nearest neighbours) for responsiveness under slider-driven re-interpolation, and offers a complementary depth-below-surface view obtained by subtracting the IDW-interpolated head field from the high-resolution DEM on a per-cell basis and masking inter-dune ridge cells (where the DEM exceeds the well-plane IDW elevation by more than 1.0 m) to confine the visualization to hydrologically meaningful slack positions. The viewer and the static figures therefore report matching per-well Δh values but use distinct interpolation methods and present complementary quantities at the grid-rendering stage.

A two-dimensional finite-difference PDE model was initially implemented during development as an alternative spatial framework, using the Helmholtz form of the steady-state groundwater flow equation in level form (Bear, 1972; Freeze and Cherry, 1979):

∇ ⋅ (*T*⋅∇*h*) − *β*<sub>3</sub> ⋅ *h* = −(*β*<sub>1</sub>⋅*P*<sub>*e**f**f*</sub>−*β*<sub>2</sub>⋅*P**E**T*)

The PDE works in level form rather than the displacement formulation of the SSM (Section 3.4) because it solves directly for the equilibrium head field h(x, y) under Dirichlet sea-boundary conditions, where the constant datum offset would be absorbed into the boundary specification. With transmissivity T = K·b (K = 6.0 m/day after Betson et al., 2002; b IDW-interpolated from four borehole constraints after Bristow, 2003), Dirichlet head conditions at sea boundaries, and implicit Neumann no-flow at the ridge, the sparse linear system of approximately 4,200 equations was solved using scipy.sparse.linalg.spsolve (Virtanen et al., 2020). The solver produced physically plausible baseline head surfaces and Darcy flux fields, but forest management scenarios evaluated through the PDE produced near-zero site-wide differences (mean Δh = −0.003 to −0.008 m for full clearfell and thinning). This reflects three structural limitations: the β coefficient changes affect only the forest wells (C4 and C5), which when IDW-interpolated to the grid are diluted by the surrounding clusters; the weak drainage feedback at C4 means a moderate source term change produces only a small equilibrium head change; and the elevated boundary condition at the ridge margin, where the C4 wells occupy the highest topographic positions in the dune system, is not parameterized in the source terms. Combined with the sparse constraints on K (a single tracer test) and b (four borehole logs across 700 ha), the PDE approach was judged to overclaim relative to the data available, echoing the calibration difficulties that constrained Betson et al.'s (2002) MODFLOW model at this site. The well-level IDW framework described above was therefore adopted as the primary spatial analysis, on the basis that it retains the physical content that the SSM parameterization supports without claiming spatial flow dynamics the monitoring data cannot constrain.

All scenario outputs are provided through a self-contained interactive viewer allowing site-wide and cluster-level Δh responses to be explored under any combination of the six defined scenarios and their underlying parameter sliders (scenario\_viewer.html; available at [*https://newbroman.github.io/Newborough\_Hydrology/scenario\_viewer.html*](https://newbroman.github.io/Newborough_Hydrology/scenario_viewer.html)).

Beyond the scenario Δh fields, two further baseline spatial products are derived from the same per-well parameterization and rendered on the standard site grid. The mean annual water-table surface is constructed by inverse-distance-weighted interpolation of each well's mean above-Ordnance-Datum head over the monitoring period to the 50 m grid, masked to the sea-boundary extent. No elevation-model mask is applied to this surface, because the water table is a continuous field independent of the overlying dune topography. From the interpolated head surface, groundwater flow-direction vectors are obtained as the negative head gradient,

*Q* = −*K* ⋅ *b* ⋅ ∇*h*

(Bear, 1972; Freeze and Cherry, 1979), evaluated by finite differences across the grid, then normalized to unit length and reported as direction only. The hydraulic conductivity K = 6.0 m day⁻¹ (Betson et al., 2002) and the interpolated aquifer-thickness surface b therefore set the orientation of the field rather than a calibrated flux magnitude, and vectors falling on ridge interpolation artefacts are suppressed. The vectors are reported as directional context for the interpolated head field, not as the output of a calibrated continuous-flow model.

The state-space water-balance residual field expresses, at each reference well, the degree to which the three-term model closes the long-term balance. Setting the monthly change to zero at long-run equilibrium, the residual is the excess of modelled losses over modelled recharge,

$${\alpha = \left( {\beta\_{2}\cdot{\overline{\mathit{PET}} + \beta\_{3}}\cdot\overline{h\_{\mathit{disp}}}} \right)}-\beta\_{1}\cdot\overline{P}$$

where the overbars denote long-term monthly means, overline h\_disp is the mean displacement above the drainage datum (Section 3.4), overline PET is Thornthwaite potential evaporation — above-canopy at the forested clusters and open-dune elsewhere — and overline P is gross rainfall; both are taken uncorrected at every cluster, including the forested ones (Section 3.8). A positive residual marks a location where the rainfall-driven recharge captured by the model does not account for the observed head — an inadequacy in the three-term model at that location, or in principle an unmodelled input; a negative residual marks slight over-prediction. On the present evidence the diagnostic reading is the supported one (Sections 4.9.7, 5.2.1). The residual is computed from the fitted β coefficients alone — specific yield does not enter — and interpolated to the site grid with zero-valued anchors along the sea and estuary boundaries and the dune ridges masked. The head surface, flow field and residual field are presented in Section 4.9.

*The spatial groundwater analysis and scenario mapping are implemented in 19\_spatial\_groundwater.py. Publication-quality spatial figures are produced by 20\_spatial\_figures.py. The forestry scenario equilibrium framework and the broadleaf seasonal β₂ derivation are implemented in 21\_forestry\_scenarios.py.*

### Combined Driver and Network-Change Spatial Analysis

This section describes the spatial figures that extend the groundwater analysis beyond the scenario framework of Section 3.8.4. A first group combines or differences the active drivers: a measured clearfell step-change map, a parametric net state map combining all five active drivers, a 2005→2025 driver-change map, and an empirical MSL5 change map. A second group characterizes observed network change directly, independently of the driver model: a secular differential-movement map (Figure 63), an absolute climate-removed trend map (Figure 64), a climate-response envelope (Figure 65 and 66), a per-well climate-sensitivity coefficient, and a two-window sensitivity demonstration. All use the same 50 m British National Grid (EPSG:27700) site grid and site-polygon clip described in Section 3.8.4.

The clearfell step-change map (Figure 59) is derived from the climate-corrected difference in mean water-table level between the pre-felling scraping era and the post-felling era at each well (fell\_step\_cc, Script 10b; Section 3.5.4), converted to millimetres. Values are interpolated to the site grid using IDW (power 1.5) with a 100 m Gaussian blur applied within the site polygon to suppress single-well artefacts. Well markers use the same discrete banded diverging colourmap as the surface (±150 mm symmetric scale, 14 bands) to ensure self-consistency between data points and the interpolated field. Seventy wells pass the quality filter (minimum six post-felling monthly observations required to estimate a stable post-felling era mean; wells with data-quality flags excluded). This map is distinct from Figure 34, which renders the same per-well values as proportional symbols without interpolation.

**The parametric net state map **(Figure 60) superimposes five annual-mean driver fields on the site grid. Each field uses an exponential decay H₀ × exp(−d/λ) from the relevant source geometry, where λ = √(Kb/Syβ₃) ≈ 230 m (K = 6.0 m day⁻¹, b = 5 m, Sy = 0.306, β₃ = 0.0566 month⁻¹ from the C3 cluster centroid). The reach is a scaling estimate rather than a calibrated length: hydraulic conductivity is a literature value for dune sand, and across the range reported for that material (2–20 m day⁻¹) λ spans roughly 130–420 m, while the assumed 5 m aquifer thickness (3–8 m) spans 180–290 m. The drainage datum contributes less than either, β₃ varying by about a quarter over a plausible datum range and λ with its square root. The results below are therefore stated to the nearest tens of metres, and the conclusions they support depend only on the reach being of order 200 m rather than of order 20 m or 2 km. The five components are: (i) forest canopy drawdown — loss, H₀ = 150 mm, decaying from the forest boundary, zeroed over the felling polygon; (ii) clearfell gain — positive, same H₀ and λ, decaying from the felling polygon boundary; (iii) scrape drain — loss, H₀ = 129 mm anchored to the measured CEH36 response, same λ; (iv) coastal erosion drawdown — loss, h₀ = 21 mm at the dune toe from the 6 m Storm Brendan exemplar retreat and the live Script 25 fit; (v) sea-level-rise head gain — positive, +20 mm over five years at the shore. Net = SLR + clearfell − forest − erosion − scrape. A first-order superposition of single-mechanism illustrative annual-mean fields; not a closed water balance. Implemented in 20\_spatial\_figures.py (plot\_msl5\_change; CURR\_YR = 2023).

**The 2005→2025 driver-change map **(Figure 61) re-bases the net-state construction to the monitoring record, differencing each modelled driver field between its 2005 and 2025 states so that the map shows what the drivers imply should have changed over the study window rather than the accumulated state since the forest was planted. Because the standing pine canopy is at equilibrium at both endpoints, its drawdown cancels in the difference and the mature forest interior returns to near zero, confirming the map is an epoch difference rather than a re-drawing of Figure 60. Five fields change over the window and are summed: the clearfell gain, as in the net-state map; the dune-scrape response, with all cuts within the window contributing; a broadleaf-restock field new to this map, computed on the same exponential-decay construction but scaled by the broadleaf interception fraction (0.15, against 0.24 for pine; Komatsu et al., 2011) and by the 2005→2025 canopy-establishment increment, giving of order 56 mm of added drawdown at source and appearing as a loss patch of the opposite sign to the clearfell gain; the five-year chronic coastal drawdown, five years of the observed coast-edge decline rate (Script 25 forest-free fit, δ₀ = 29.0 mm yr⁻¹) giving h₀ = 5 × δ₀ ≈ 145 mm at the dune toe on the same five-year horizon as the sea-level-rise field; and the sea-level-rise head gain over the same span. As a first-order superposition the map is illustrative and not a closed water balance; the broadleaf and coastal fields in particular are modelled quantities the dipwell record cannot fully resolve, and are presented as indicative. It is the modelled companion to the absolute climate-removed trend map (Figure 64) over the same window. *Implemented in 20\_spatial\_figures.py (plot\_driver\_change\_2005\_2025).*

****The differential-movement map ****(Figure 63) isolates the secular component of spring water-table change that the two-window MSL5 comparison cannot resolve. For each well a **spring value is formed for every year as the mean of the available March–May readings; the well's anomaly is its spring level minus the site-mean spring level in the same year, the subtraction removing the common climate signal so that what remains is movement relative to the network as a whole. The site-mean reference panel is restricted to the wells present in a fixed majority fraction of the period's springs, so the reference does not drift with coverage. The per-well metric is the ordinary-least-squares slope of this anomaly against year, in millimetres per year, fitted over two periods — 2011–2025 (primary) and 2005–2025 (robustness). Significance is assessed by a lag-1 autoregression-corrected t-test on the effective sample size and cross-checked against a moving-block bootstrap confidence interval; significant wells are drawn solid and non-significant wells hollow. Only the Llyn Rhos-Ddu lake gauge is excluded — CEH13 and CEH14, withheld from the SSM analyses for state-space reasons, are retained here because the differential anomaly trend is purely observational and does not use the model. The per-well slopes are interpolated to the 50 m grid by inverse-distance weighting (power 2), masked beyond 450 m of the nearest well. Because the anomaly is referenced to the network mean, this map shows relative movement — which well gains or loses ground against the site as a whole — rather than the absolute rate of change at each well, which is mapped separately (Figure 64). *Implemented in 32\_differential\_movement.py.*

**The absolute climate-removed trend map **(Figure 64) complements the differential map by giving each well's secular rate of change on its own terms, with the shared climate signal removed but without re-referencing to the network mean. Where the differential map subtracts the site-mean spring level — which over a wet-spring-lifted window can invert the reading, so that a well holding its position against a rising mean appears to fall — the absolute map removes only the variance attributable to an external climate index, leaving the real site-wide recharge decline and coastal drying in the result. Spring values are formed as for the differential map. The external index is the spring climatic water balance, the sum of precipitation minus Thornthwaite PET over March–May, taken from the RAF Valley record contemporaneously with the spring level and without lag. For each well a single joint regression is fitted, h(t) = a + b·CWB(t) + c·t, and the secular trend is the fitted time coefficient c in millimetres per year; fitting the climate index and the linear trend together orthogonalizes the two, so the climate term cannot absorb the secular signal. A two-step alternative — regressing level on the climate index and then fitting a trend to the residual — was rejected because over a climatically non-stationary window the climate index itself trends and absorbs the real drying before the slope is measured. A coverage filter admits a well to a window only where it carries at least one spring observation before 2011 and its observed record spans at least 80% of the window, excluding short-record wells whose trend would otherwise be fitted to a truncated drought-recovery trajectory. Two periods are fitted — 2005–2025 (primary) and 2011–2025 (robustness); the longer window is primary here, in contrast to the differential map, because the shorter window is dominated by post-2011 short-record wells. Significance, exclusions and marker convention follow the differential map. The per-well slopes are interpolated to the 50 m grid by linear interpolation within the convex hull of the wells, extended 100 m beyond the hull to reach the coastal margin, and clipped to the site polygon. Over 2005–2025 the coastal-forest cluster carries the most negative climate-removed trend (C5 ≈ −11.6 mm yr⁻¹), consistent with the coastal-retreat gradient of Section 3.7.4; the strongest individual driers are the coastal and western wells. I*mplemented in 36\_absolute\_climate\_trend.py.*

**The climate-response envelope** (Figure 65) characterizes the range the spring water table moves between its dry and wet extremes and yields two window-independent products. Spring values are formed as for the differential map. Dry and wet extreme states are taken as the per-well mean spring level over fixed sets of extreme years, selected by site-mean spring extremity and antecedent-rainfall consistency — dry {2011, 2012, 2019} and wet {2014, 2021, 2024}; the year 2006 is excluded as a wet extreme because its 2004–2005 antecedent was the driest in the record and the slow-draining forest wells had not refilled by that spring. Each well's swing is its wet state minus its dry state, and its amplification coefficient is that swing divided by the network-mean swing, with the common-mode swing removed, so that values above one magnify the shared climate forcing and below one damp it. The drought-floor surface (Figure 66) is the dry-state depth to water, retained in absolute depth below ground and contoured against the Curreli et al. (2013) SD15b (0.61 m) and SD16 (0.98 m) thresholds, which are absolute distances to surface rather than relative quantities. As in the differential map, only the lake gauge is excluded; CEH13 and CEH14 are included because the coefficient is observational. The amplification field is interpolated to the 50 m grid by linear interpolation, masked beyond 450 m of the nearest well; the drought-floor surface is likewise linearly interpolated with a ridge mask applied to suppress extrapolation onto the bedrock ridge, and the single raised inter-slack well CEH10 is shown as a distinct slack-edge marker but excluded from the interpolated slack-floor surface, which would otherwise smear its raised-ground depth across the neighbouring slacks.* Implemented in 33\_envelope\_amplification.py.*

**A discrete per-well climate-sensitivity coefficient **(Script 35) accompanies the interpolated amplification field, extending the metric to wells with short or inconsistent records that the matched surface and the state-space model cannot reach. It uses the same dry-to-wet spring swing but normalizes each well against a reference core — the wells with full dry-extreme coverage and adequate wet coverage — with the core swing recomputed over that well's own extreme years, so that wells measured on different subsets of extreme springs remain comparable. This co-temporal normalization cancels the common climate signal window by window, reproducing the matched-window amplification (r ≈ 0.98) while removing coverage artefacts. Each coefficient carries a confidence tier (A: at least two dry and two wet years; B: at least one of each; C: a single year on one side) and a delete-one-year jackknife 90% interval. The coefficient is validated against the independently fitted SSM response — it correlates with the per-well β₂ and β₃ coefficients — and is reported as consistent with, rather than confirming, the fitted drainage and atmospheric-draw response, since at the short-record wells that are its main use case the β coefficients are not available for direct cross-check. *Implemented in 35\_per\_well\_amplification.py.*

**The two-window sensitivity demonstration** (Script 34, presented in Section 5.7.5) places the single 2017→2023 MSL5 comparison inside the envelope of every admissible window pair, to show directly that the two-window method cannot resolve absolute site-wide change. Per-well MSL5 for a window ending in year Y is the mean of the well's annual spring MSL over years Y−4 to Y (taken from the committed van Willegen aggregation, 26\_msl\_annual\_per\_well.csv), and a well qualifies for a window only if all five spring-years are present. For each ordered pair of windows the site-mean change is the mean, over the common panel of wells qualifying in both windows, of the per-well change; holding the panel fixed across the pair ensures that composition change cannot inflate the result. A pair is admissible when its common panel reaches at least forty wells, which excludes only the thin seven-well 2005–2009 baseline, so that the spread arises from window choice alone. All admissible pairs are retained, including those whose current window contains the anomalously wet 2024 spring, since admitting them is the point of the demonstration. The 2017→2023 pair reproduces the −96.8 mm headline. *Implemented in 34\_window\_sensitivity.py.*

****Driver validation (Script 37).**** The 2005→2025 driver-change map described above presents what the modelled fields imply should have changed; Script 37 tests that implication against the observed change directly. Per-well climate-corrected endpoint differences (the absolute climate-removed trend of Figure 64) are regressed on the same unit driver fields at their modelled amplitudes, per window, with a free spatially-uniform intercept, using ordinary least squares with HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. Three windows are fitted: a pre-intervention window (2006–2012, coastal field only), a post-clearfell window (2018–2025, coastal and clearfell fields), and the full record (2005–2025). The intercept absorbs the site-wide recharge decline, so a uniform background drying cannot be attributed to the coastal gradient; the resulting scale factors are dimensionless multipliers on the modelled amplitude, unity meaning the aquifer feels exactly the modelled field. C2 Dune, carrying no driver field, serves as a driver-free negative control, and the sluice-controlled C1 Lake Edge wells are excluded from the fit as management-influenced. This is a validation, not a fitting step: it sets no amplitude used elsewhere. Its outcome is a bounded null — every fitted scale factor has a confidence interval spanning both zero and unity, so the network can neither confirm the modelled amplitude nor exclude it. The modelled coastal field in particular cannot be independently recovered from the monitoring record (Section 5.7); the coastal shape it tests is moreover fitted from the same network (Section 3.7.4), making the validation partly self-confirming, with only an expanding-window trajectory of the implied coastal rate serving as an independent check. *Implemented in 37\_driver\_validation.py.*

****Comparative driver footing (Script 37b).**** The same modelled fields are placed on a common footing over the 2005→2025 window, with each driver entered as its gain and loss components (forest as clearfell and broadleaf restock, scraping as on-site rise and off-site drain cone, coast as sea-level rise and erosion drawdown). In this report the drivers are compared on two directly interpretable measures — peak, near-source head change (mm) and inland reach (m), the depth-against-distance profiles shown in Figure 72 — with amplitudes taken from the observed BACI anchors and the modelled Script 20 fields, not the Script 37 scale factors. The area-integrated and volumetric comparison that underlies the net scraping-versus-coast ratio reported in Section 4.9.8 is derived in Supplement §S.20.6, where the full three-currency construction and its specific-yield conversion are set out; fields are superposed linearly, a first-order construction that is an upper bound in overlap zones. *Implemented in 37b\_driver\_footing.py.*

## <span id="anchor-32"></span>**Data requirements and Replicability**

****A deliberate objective of this study was to demonstrate the hydrological insight achievable from minimal, low-cost monitoring infrastructure. All primary data inputs consist of manual dipwell measurements, recorded with a standard electrical contact meter or tape measure, and publicly available monthly climate summaries from the Met Office historic station dataset. No specialist survey equipment, telemetry, or laboratory analysis was required. Potential evapotranspiration was estimated using the Thornthwaite and Mather (1957) method specifically because it requires only mean monthly air temperature — a variable recorded at every Met Office station and at the majority of automatic weather stations worldwide. Methods requiring wind speed, humidity, or radiation data were deliberately avoided as these would limit replicability to sites with more comprehensive climate instrumentation.****

The analytical framework presented here could be deployed at any comparable lowland wetland, dune slack, or shallow unconfined aquifer system with a dipwell network and access to a nearby climate station. The principal constraint on analysis quality is record length rather than measurement sophistication; the state-space modelling and BACI analyses both benefit substantially from the long baseline available here, but meaningful cluster separation and mechanistic coefficient estimation are likely achievable with records of eight to ten years or more.
