# <span id="anchor"></span>Results

## <span id="anchor-1"></span>**Climate and Well Network Characterization**

### <span id="anchor-2"></span>**Climate Record**

The RAF Valley climate station provides a continuous monthly record from December 1930 to February 2026 (Figure 4a) (Section 3.1.2). Over the well-record period (April 2005 to February 2026), mean annual precipitation was 890 mm and mean annual Thornthwaite PET was 652 mm, giving a mean P/PET ratio of 1.36 (Table 1). The wettest year on record within this period was 2008 (1,040 mm), followed by 2012 (1,022 mm); the driest complete year was 2010 (736 mm). Four years recorded a P/PET ratio below 1.2 — indicating near-balance between precipitation and evaporative demand — namely 2007, 2010, 2011 and 2022, with 2022 recording the lowest ratio of the monitoring period at 1.12. There is no meaningful difference in mean annual precipitation between the pre-felling (2006–2017: 887 mm) and post-felling (2018–2025: 893 mm) periods, confirming that the post-2018 hydrological signal detected in the BACI analysis (Section 4.6) is not driven by a concurrent change in rainfall amount.

PET shows strong seasonality but negligible inter-annual trend across the record, with the 12-month rolling mean remaining stable at approximately 50–55 mm/month throughout (Figure 4b). Consequently, inter-annual variability in the water balance is driven almost entirely by precipitation rather than evaporative demand.

The levelled cumulative water balance series (Figure 4c), expressed relative to the study-period mean of 19.46 mm/month, oscillates with a clear seasonal signal — rising sharply each winter as precipitation exceeds PET and falling each summer — while tracking the multi-year rhythm of wetter and drier phases. No sustained multi-year drift away from the baseline is evident, confirming that the site maintains a consistent long-run surplus throughout the monitoring period. The water table clearly follows this movement (Figure 4d). Linear regression of the cumulative balance against the unsmoothed network mean water level at zero lag yielded R² = 0.514, confirming that approximately half the variance in network mean water table depth is explained by antecedent climate forcing alone. The residual variance reflects the spatial heterogeneity of the network, local boundary subsidies, and land cover effects explored in subsequent sections. This close correspondence between cumulative climate forcing and water table behaviour provided the empirical motivation for the state-space modelling framework described in Section 3.4.

The seasonal structure of the climate record has direct implications for aquifer behaviour. Mean monthly rainfall peaks in October–December (around 101–109 mm/month) and falls to its minimum in April (around 46 mm/month; Figure 4a), whilst Thornthwaite (1957) PET peaks in July (around 107 mm/month) and is negligible from November to February (Figure 4b). The seasonal opposition between peak PET and minimum rainfall concentrates atmospheric evaporative demand on the aquifer during the period of lowest recharge input, driving summer water table drawdown that must be overcome by the following winter's recharge before any slack flooding can occur. This seasonal asymmetry — not the annual rainfall total — is the primary control on dune slack hydrology and is reflected throughout the subsequent analyses. Over the monitoring period, mean annual winter rainfall (October–March) was 516 mm and mean annual summer rainfall (April–September) was 373 mm, together summing to the 890 mm annual mean.

### <span id="anchor-3"></span>**4.1.2 Well Network**

The 66-well reference network (Section 3.1.1) spans a median record length of 191 months (15.9 years), with individual cleaned series ranging from 139 to 250 months (Figure 6, upper left); all wells satisfy the 100-month minimum-observation threshold for admission to the reference network. The network mean water level across all wells and months was 0.74 m below pipe top, with individual well means ranging from 0.26 m above pipe top to 2.07 m below, reflecting the considerable topographic and hydrogeological diversity of the site (Figure 6, upper right). The median seasonal amplitude — defined as the difference between mean August and mean February water levels — was 0.69 m, with the water table deepest in late summer and shallowest in late winter, consistent with the expected seasonal recharge cycle for a maritime dune system (Figure 6, lower right). The network mean time series (Figure 6, lower left) shows a clear seasonal cycle throughout the record, with the inter-well standard deviation envelope widening visibly after the December 2017 clearfell intervention, reflecting the divergence of the impact zone wells from the broader network that is explored in detail in Section 4.6.

Mapped against the eco-hydrological thresholds of Curreli et al. (2013), the network-wide seasonal extremes reveal a system already under considerable stress. The mean annual summer minimum across the reference network was −1.18 m below pipe top, ranging from −0.71 m to −2.32 m across individual wells. No well maintains a mean summer minimum shallower than the wet slack viability threshold of −0.61 m; 28 wells fall within the dry slack zone (−0.61 to −0.98 m); and 38 wells record mean summer minima deeper than the dry slack threshold of −0.98 m, of which 20 lie below the SD16 recovery limit of −1.20 m — placing them beyond the reach of a single scraping event under average conditions. For winter maxima, 25 wells exceed the SD15b wet slack flooding threshold of −0.10 m in an average year and 38 exceed the SD16 dry slack threshold of −0.25 m, though only 11 record mean winter maxima above pipe top. These network-level baselines provide the reference against which cluster-specific dynamics, intervention effects, and climate trajectory are assessed in subsequent sections.

## <span id="anchor-4"></span>Network Clustering and Spatial Architecture

The clustering analysis (Section 3.2) identified k = 5 as the preferred cluster solution (silhouette coefficient = 0.40; bootstrap stability analysis confirming four of five clusters with median per-well co-assignment ≥ 0.93). The five clusters are spatially coherent and hydrogeologically interpretable (Table 3; Figures 7–9); their mechanistic characterization including LCSC values is presented in Section 4.2.2.

The dendrogram confirms a primary binary division separating eastern wells (C1, C2) from western and forest wells (C3, C4, C5) at a Ward linkage distance of approximately 1.0. This top-level split reflects the division between the till-and-estuary-influenced eastern dune aquifer and the deeper, more uniform western sand block. The forest clusters (C4 Main Forest, C5 Coastal Forest) separate from adjacent C3 wells despite geographical proximity, confirming that plantation land cover modifies groundwater behaviour independently of the broader dune aquifer geology. The separation also reflects the position of the C4 wells in the northern part of the study area, closest to the rock ridge, where the water table receives lateral recharge descending from the ridge. C5 (Coastal Forest) occupies the coastal edge of the plantation where canopy and coastal influences combine.

### <span id="anchor-5"></span>  Cluster Hydrographs and Seasonal Amplitudes

The five cluster average hydrographs (Figure 9b) reveal a clear stratification of groundwater behaviour that reflects the hydrogeological architecture identified by the clustering analysis. C1 (Lake Edge) shows the shallowest mean depth (−0.45 m), consistent with its low storage capacity and hydraulic connectivity to Llyn Rhos-Ddu. C2 (Dune) tracks C1 closely but sits systematically deeper (mean −0.60 m), reflecting greater distance from the lake boundary. The fundamental geological contrast between the Eastern and Western Blocks is visible as a persistent vertical offset between C1/C2 and C3 (Western Residual), with C3 running approximately 0.3 m deeper on average (mean −0.89 m) despite occupying a broadly similar topographic position — a contrast that reflects the deeper sand accumulation and higher storage capacity of the Western Residual aquifer. C4 (Main Forest) is the most distinctive cluster, sitting 0.6 m deeper than adjacent C3 wells throughout the record (mean −1.47 m). This persistent depression relative to the surrounding dune aquifer is consistent with the canopy interception interpretation: the Corsican pine plantation intercepts a substantial fraction of gross precipitation before it reaches the water table, suppressing recharge and depressing the water table below that of the adjacent unforested dune. The C4 cluster mean hydrograph should be interpreted cautiously prior to 2010, when only three of the nine cluster wells were operational (CEH2, CEH13 and NW10); these wells are disproportionately located at higher elevations on the ridge margin and carry above-average water balance residuals which could be due to boundary subsidies, so the apparent high mean heads in 2006–2008 reflect an unrepresentative spatial sample rather than a genuine network-wide signal. Full nine-well coverage was not achieved until mid-2010. C5 (Coastal Forest) sits at comparable depth to C4 (mean −1.25 m) but with a damped seasonal signal, consistent with its position at the coastal edge of the plantation where both canopy interception and proximity to the coastal discharge boundary attenuate the seasonal response. Per-well seasonal amplitudes and their temporal evolution are analysed below.

The cumulative water balance panel (Figure 9a) confirms that the cluster separation is not an artefact of differential climate forcing — the 12-month rolling mean of the corrected net balance remains close to zero throughout the record, indicating that all clusters are responding to the same climatic regime. The structural differences between clusters therefore reflect hydrogeological and land cover controls rather than spatially variable precipitation or evapotranspiration.

The network mean water table elevation was 7.60 m AOD across the 66-well reference network, ranging from 3.12 m AOD (CEH11, C1 Lake Edge) to 13.30 m AOD at the ridge flank (CEH14, C4 Main Forest). CEH11's assignment to C1 is discussed in Section 3.2.3: its low elevation and coastal position place it near the Menai Strait tidal boundary rather than Llyn Rhos-Ddu, and it represents the low-elevation extreme of the reference network rather than a typical lake-edge well. Excluding CEH11, the C1 cluster-mean elevation rises from 8.57 m AOD to 9.44 m AOD, and the network minimum shifts to CEH42 (C2, 4.47 m AOD). The Main Forest cluster (C4) occupies the highest mean water table elevation in the network at 9.52 m AOD, compared with 8.57 m AOD in the Lake Edge cluster (C1), 7.57 m AOD in the Dune cluster (C2), and 7.18 m AOD in the Western Residual (C3) — a contrast that reflects the topographic position of the plantation on the northern ridge flank rather than any difference in recharge behaviour. The Coastal Forest cluster (C5) sits at 4.60 m AOD, the lowest cluster-mean elevation in the network, consistent with its coastal-margin position.

Three patterns emerge. First, the open dune clusters (C2, C3) show stable raw amplitude but modest climate-normalized damping (≈7%), consistent with the post-2018 drought summers temporarily inflating apparent variability while the underlying seasonal range has slightly contracted. Second, **C5 Coastal Forest shows a pronounced amplitude collapse** of 18% (raw) to 21% (climate-normalized), the largest reduction in the network. Combined with C5's anomalously fast summer minimum decline (Section 4.8), this indicates that the C5 water table is becoming both lower in absolute terms and less seasonally responsive — recharge peaks no longer reach their pre-2018 heights even after correcting for drought summers, suggesting either progressive interception increase or a structural change in the recharge pathway at the coastal margin. The C3 cluster shows the highest within-cluster spread (post-2018 ratio max:min ≈ 2.0), approaching the amplitude-heterogeneity flagging threshold (Section 3.2.4) and consistent with the geological transition between deep aeolian sand and the western dune-till margin.

The amplitude analysis confirms that the cluster-mean coefficients reported in Section 4.2.2 represent stable averages across wells whose individual amplitude responses differ within most clusters by less than a factor of two, but with sufficient within-cluster variation in C3 and C4 to motivate the per-well coefficient mapping presented in Section 4.9.

### <span id="anchor-6"></span>Cluster Mechanistic Characterization

The five clusters are characterized mechanistically in Table 3 using a state-space model fitted to the average hydrograph for each cluster (Section 3.4.1). β₁ is the recharge sensitivity — the water table rise (mm) per mm of rainfall; β₂ is the atmospheric draw — the water table decline (mm) per mm of PET; and −β₃ is the proportional drainage decay rate — the fraction of antecedent displacement above the drainage datum (Section 3.4) dissipated by lateral drainage each month. All five cluster-centroid coefficient sets reported in Table 3 are statistically significant (p &lt; 0.01); within-cluster, individual per-well coefficients vary in significance, most notably the near-zero C4 drainage coefficient and the small C1 atmospheric-draw term, as discussed below and in Section 4.9.

The recharge sensitivity coefficient β₁ is highest in the Lake Edge cluster (C1: 4.58), indicating that each millimetre of rainfall drives a proportionally larger water table rise in the shallow-till aquifer than in the Dune cluster (C2: 3.98) or Western Residual (C3: 3.57). The two forested clusters return near-identical and substantially lower recharge sensitivities (C4: 2.52, C5: 2.41), consistent with canopy interception suppressing the rainfall signal reaching the water table. The atmospheric draw coefficient β₂ is highest at C4 (2.55), followed by C3 (1.85) and C2 (1.77), with C5 (1.32) and C1 (0.96) lowest. The C4 value of β₂ is near-identical to its β₁, meaning that the Main Forest cluster loses as much water table per mm of PET as it gains per mm of rainfall — the plantation suppresses recharge (low β₁) while simultaneously amplifying the atmospheric draw signal relative to unforested clusters, producing a symmetrically sensitive water balance. Because β coefficients fold together vegetation effects, specific yield and soil moisture dynamics, they cannot be decomposed into crop coefficients without independent Sy estimates; this decomposition is developed in Section 5.6.1. That C5 (Coastal Forest) has low β₂ despite also carrying Corsican pine canopy suggests that coastal proximity moderates the atmospheric draw signal, possibly through higher ambient humidity or higher specific yield in the sandier coastal substrate. C1's low β₂ (0.96, the lowest in the network) reflects its shallow water table and rapid lateral drainage to the lake — the head-dependent drainage term captures the dominant loss mechanism, with the depth-dependent PET diagnostic (Section 3.7.1) confirming that C1 has the strongest residual depth-coupling (λ = 1.90 m⁻¹). The drainage decay coefficient −β₃ is highest at C1 (0.090) where rapid lateral exchange with Llyn Rhos-Ddu drives fast recession, similar at C2 (0.066) and C3 (0.058), lower at C5 (0.045), and lowest at C4 (0.020). The C4 value is weakly identified, however — the forest-interior drainage coefficient is degeneracy-affected (Section 5.2.4), and a triangulation-constrained estimate places it nearer 0.058 month⁻¹. The LCSC (= 100/β₁) expresses these contrasts as a directly interpretable management metric: 22–25 mm of rainfall is required to raise Lake Edge and Dune water tables by 10 cm, compared with 28 mm in the Western Residual and 40–41 mm beneath the plantation.

The C1 cluster-mean coefficients in Table 3 include CEH11, which is geographically separated from the other six C1 members (Section 3.2.3). CEH11 sits at 3.6 m AOD on the south-eastern coast, approximately 1.6 km from the nearest lake-edge well, and its damped hydrograph likely reflects proximity to the Menai Strait tidal boundary rather than to Llyn Rhos-Ddu. Its β₂ value of 0.77 is consistent with the C1 cluster mean (0.96) but may reflect a coastal constant-head mechanism rather than the lake-buffering process that governs the remaining members. CEH11's inclusion extends the C1 elevation range from 8.6–10.9 m down to 3.6 m; if excluded, the six remaining wells (five excluding CEH6, whose negative β₂ is discussed below) occupy a tight 9.1–10.9 m elevation band. The cluster-mean coefficients are not materially affected by CEH11's inclusion — its β₁ (4.46) and β₃ (0.092) are close to the C1 mean — but per-well spatial analyses within C1 should be interpreted with awareness that CEH11 responds to a different boundary condition.

An extended form of the state-space model incorporating a depth-dependent evapotranspiration coefficient (Section 3.7.1) was evaluated at cluster level; as the results of this analysis bear primarily on the mechanistic interpretation of the Forest cluster β₂ coefficient, they are discussed in Section 5.6.1 rather than reported here. The within-cluster coefficient variation is substantial, particularly in the forest zone where per-well β₂ ranges from 0.878 to 3.723 across the 14 C4 and C5 wells. ### <span id="anchor-8"></span>**Specific Yield Estimation**

WTF event-based median Sy estimates for all five clusters, derived from 34–64 qualifying monthly rising-limb events per cluster, are presented in Table 4c. Uncorrected median values range from 0.210 (C1 Lake Edge) to 0.356 (C5 Coastal Forest), with broad interquartile ranges reflecting the inherent noise in monthly cluster-mean data. The two forested clusters return uncorrected medians (C4: 0.315, C5: 0.356) that sit at or above the open dune range (0.210–0.325), because gross rainfall overstates the recharge flux reaching the water table beneath the canopy — the WTF method attributes the full rainfall to recharge when in reality 24% is intercepted, inflating the apparent Sy. After applying the interception correction of 24% of P (Freeman, 2008), the forested cluster medians fall to 0.248 (C4) and 0.321 (C5): C4 falls cleanly within the open dune range (0.210–0.325), while C5 sits at its upper edge. The C5 estimate is the least reliable in the network — a majority of its rising-limb events return event-level Sy at or above the 0.50 plausibility ceiling (Section 3.7.3), so its median is constrained by that limit rather than freely estimated, and its modest apparent response to the interception correction is partly an artefact of the clip; it is therefore reported as a weak corroborator. The C4 result is consistent with the attenuated water table response beneath the plantation reflecting the surface boundary condition (canopy interception) rather than a fundamentally different substrate. Within this corrected dataset, a secondary pattern is visible: the two clusters with the lowest corrected Sy — C1 (Lake Edge; 0.210) and C4 (Main Forest; 0.248) — return lower values than C3 (Western Residual; 0.325) and C5 (Coastal Forest; 0.321), with C2 (Dune; 0.281) intermediate. C1's low Sy is consistent with the shallow clay/till base and short drainage path to the lake seen in the borehole logs; C4 overlies the bedrock ridge, which outcrops within the forest, so an irregular, low-storage rock base is the natural reading of its low Sy and its within-cluster heterogeneity in β₃ — though the extent and depth of that base beneath C4, and hence the precise buried-ridge geometry, are unconstrained without coring. The pattern recurs in the per-well β₂ regression (Section 4.9.5) where elevation — reflecting both vadose zone thickness and proximity to buried bedrock — explains 96% of within-forest β₂ variance.

An independent cross-check on these estimates is available from a second WTF method: an ordinary-least-squares regression of winter water-table rise on net recharge, which returns a per-cluster Sy from the regression slope (Table 4c, OLS-winter column). The OLS estimates run higher than the event-medians at four of the five clusters (mean ratio 1.18), consistent with the documented tendency of WTF methods to bias high on absolute magnitude; the OLS values, like the event-medians, should be read as upper bounds rather than point estimates. The two methods agree on the principal spatial pattern — both place the deep-sand Western Residual (C3) and Coastal Forest (C5) clusters at the high-storage end of the network — which corroborates the substrate-driven interpretation above. They diverge at two clusters, and the divergence is itself informative. At C1 (Lake Edge), the OLS regression returns the weakest fit of the five clusters (R² = 0.62, with C2 next-weakest at 0.67 and C3–C5 at 0.84–0.88), so the OLS estimate is least reliable precisely where it disagrees; the event-median's placement of C1 as the low-storage end-member, consistent with its constrained till-and-clay substrate, is retained. At C4 (Main Forest), the uncorrected OLS estimate ranks the cluster lowest in the network — but C4 is the cluster where canopy interception most distorts the raw WTF signal, which is exactly why the forest interpretation in this section rests on the interception-corrected event-median rather than any uncorrected estimate. Across both methods, the event-based median is retained as the primary specific-yield estimate for each cluster; the OLS regression serves as a corroborating check on the spatial pattern.

Individual well Sy values underlying the spatial mapping of Figure 12 are considered separately below. All five WTF medians exceed the assumed Sy values used in the water balance (C1: 0.08; C2–C5: 0.12) by a factor of two to three, consistent with the known tendency of monthly WTF to conflate gravity drainage with capillary fringe release (Healy and Cook, 2002; Scanlon et al., 2002). The WTF estimates should therefore be interpreted as upper bounds on effective storage. Direct field measurement by slug test or pumping test at representative wells per cluster is identified as a priority for future work.

## <span id="anchor-9"></span>Checking the Clusters Pearson Affinity Analysis

Of the 66 wells in the reference network, 63 confirmed their primary cluster membership through the Pearson Affinity Analysis (Section 3.3). Membership is characterized by three distinct tiers based on the correlation delta (Δr) between assigned and neighbouring cluster centroids: Core members (17 wells) with an unambiguous dominant affinity to their assigned cluster (Δr &gt; 0.05), representing stable hydrogeological anchors within their designated geomorphological zone; Fuzzy members (46 wells) exhibiting closely competing affinities between their primary and secondary clusters (Δr &lt; 0.05), identifying areas where the groundwater regime transitions smoothly between adjacent structural blocks; and three Spy classifications where the well's highest temporal affinity belongs to a different cluster than Ward's assignment. CEH21 and CEH4 (both assigned C3 Western Residual) show strongest affinity with C5 Coastal Forest, consistent with their coastal-margin position where the Western Residual and Coastal Forest signatures overlap; NW13 (assigned C3) shows strongest affinity with C2 Dune. All three Spy deltas are small (|Δr| &lt; 0.012), confirming that these are borderline assignments at gradational boundaries rather than gross misclassifications.

Classification confidence was further characterized by the Multi-Cluster Affinity flag, raised where three or more cluster centroids achieved a correlation of r &gt; 0.90. This criterion was met at 46 of 66 wells, reflecting the high mutual correlation among the five cluster centroids in a contiguous, highly transmissive sand aquifer. Of these, 21 are concentrated within the gradational C1/C2/C3 transitional zone of the mid-eastern dune sector, 14 lie along the C2/C3/C5 boundary where the Dune, Western Residual and Coastal Forest signatures converge, and 9 lie along the C3/C4/C5 forest–dune boundary, and 2 lie along the C2/C3/C4 boundary. The prevalence of MCA flags confirms the continuous nature of the sand matrix, where boundary wells respond to system-wide climatic forcing rather than localized topographic drivers, and identifies the precise locations of greatest structural ambiguity within the network (Figure 14a).

The extended network analysis (Section 3.3.3) classified all 22 extended wells against the reference centroids, yielding 3 Ext\_Core and 19 Ext\_Fuzzy assignments (Figure 14b). The high proportion of Fuzzy classifications reflects the shorter and earlier records of these wells rather than genuine hydrogeological ambiguity — with less temporal overlap against the centroid time series, affinity margins are naturally compressed.

Within the extended network, the clearfell treatment wells showed mixed affinities across the C3/C4/C5 boundary, with all wells carrying Multi-Cluster Affinity flags (r &gt; 0.90 with three or more centroids). FE1 and LIS1 returned strongest affinity with C4 Main Forest (r = 0.989 and 0.973 respectively), while FE2 and FE4 showed marginally strongest affinity with C5 Coastal Forest (r = 0.975 and 0.972) with C4 within 0.006. FE3 showed strongest affinity with C5 (r = 0.985) followed by C3 (r = 0.974). WMC3, in the reference network, showed strongest affinity with C3 (r = 0.965) with C5 a close second (r = 0.961). The affinity profiles for these and the other selected wells are plotted in Figure 13. The small deltas (Δr = 0.003–0.037) confirm that the clearfell zone sits at the gradational boundary between the Western Residual, Main Forest and Coastal Forest clusters rather than within any single hydrogeological unit. The persistence of forest-range affinities (C4 or C5) at these wells despite the December 2017 clearfell is consistent with either residual canopy influence from the surrounding intact plantation, incomplete hydrological recovery, or the high elevation and its inferred deep sandy substrate generating the forest signature independently of the canopy.

## <span id="anchor-10"></span>Model Benchmarking: State-Space Model Comparison with Traditional Linear Models

The SSM was compared to a TLM as outlined in Section 3.4.2. In one-step diagnostic mode — where each prediction is corrected with the observed water level from the previous month — both models track the observed record almost identically (median R² 0.91 and 0.92 respectively; Table 5). The difference emerges under autonomous simulation. Over 100 months without observational correction, the TLM diverges rapidly at wells with strong head-dependent drainage (e.g. CEH6, C2 Dune: TLM NSE = −1.12, SSM NSE = +0.66; Figure 15), while the SSM maintains physical consistency through its storage decay term. Across the full 66-well reference network, the SSM achieves a median iterative NSE of 0.75 compared with 0.18 for the TLM — a median gain of +0.53 NSE units — and returns a positive iterative NSE at 65 of 66 wells compared with 44 of 66 for the TLM (Table 5). The improvement varies systematically across the five clusters (Figure 16a, 16b): C1 Lake Edge shows the largest median gain (+1.286), where strong head-dependent drainage to Llyn Rhos-Ddu makes the storage decay term essential; C5 (+0.726), C3 (+0.527) and C2 (+0.526) show substantial gains; and C4 Main Forest shows the smallest (+0.229), reflecting its characteristically low β₃ (0.020) which produces a near-linear climate-to-water-table relationship that the TLM handles more adequately. The sole well where the SSM fails is CEH14 (C4, iterative NSE = −3.21), a ridge-flank well where the displacement formulation does not adequately capture the boundary condition. Spatial visualization (Figure 16) confirms this pattern geographically, with the largest gains concentrated along the forest–Warren boundary and the C1 lake-buffer sector.

The depth-dependent PET grid search (Section 3.7.1) provides an independent diagnostic of how completely the standard fixed-β₂ formulation captures the atmospheric draw signal. The grid search produced statistically significant NSE improvement at all five clusters, where λ (m⁻¹) is the exponential decay rate controlling how rapidly the PET signal attenuates with water table depth: C1 (ΔNSE = +0.183, λ = 1.90 m⁻¹), C4 (+0.104, λ = 0.15), C2 (+0.069, λ = 0.80), C5 (+0.039, λ = 0.50) and C3 (+0.028, λ = 0.40). AIC improvement is decisive at every cluster. The fitted λ values are physically interpretable as inverse 1/e decay depths: C1's strong decay (1/e = 0.53 m) is consistent with direct evaporation from a frequently shallow watertable near the lake margin, while C4's weak decay (1/e = 6.7 m) confirms that C4's high β₂ reflects low specific yield rather than a depth-dependent ET pathway. The mechanistic interpretation is developed in Section 5.6.1.

## <span id="anchor-11"></span>Dune Scraping Intervention Analysis

### <span id="anchor-12"></span>**Background Environmental Drift**

Three dipwells within the dune slack system — CEH36, CEH18 and CEH21 — were subject to ground surface lowering by mechanical scraping, with CEH36 scraped in April 2015 and CEH18 and CEH21 scraped in October 2023. The analysis uses a two-tier CUSUM-BACI framework (Section 3.5) to isolate the scraping signal from background environmental drift, with CEH4 and CEH22 serving as paired controls. Tier 1 analysis at CEH4 and CEH22 both show accelerating negative CUSUM trajectories across the monitoring period, reaching −10.55 m and −19.02 m respectively by early 2026(Section 3.5.4; Figure 17). The convergent deterioration of two spatially separated control wells — CEH22 and CEH4 near the coast — independently of any management action confirms that progressive coastal erosion of the dune edge is driving a structural decline in water table depth across this part of the network. CEH22's trajectory is the more severe, dominated by a coastal drawdown signal that accelerated markedly from 2021 onwards.

Against this background of coastal erosion, CEH4 shows an additional secondary deterioration signal from December 2017 onwards consistent with the clearfell felling pulse, though this is superimposed on and inseparable from the longer-run erosion trend. These Tier 1 trajectories establish that both local controls are experiencing substantial background decline that must be accounted for before any scraping benefit can be assessed.

### <span id="anchor-13"></span>Paired BACI and Robustness

In this context, Tier 2 paired analysis shows a modest positive signal at CEH36 during the Pure Scraping era (April 2015 to December 2017), the only well and era combination to show a net benefit after Tier 1 correction. Three independent methods all return a positive scraping benefit: the two control-differenced methods agree closely — raw BACI vs CEH4 (+0.129 m) and synthetic control from 11 donor wells (+0.137 m) — while the SSM forward residual, an autonomous-forecast estimator and the most conservative of the three, is lower at +0.081 m — reflecting permanent ground surface lowering rather than a hydrodynamic deviation from the climate-driven trajectory (Figure 18). The cumulative CUSUM for CEH36 rises strongly from 2018 onwards, though this reflects the accelerating deterioration of CEH4 as much as any genuine improvement at CEH36, which is why the synthetic control method is critical to isolating the true scraping signal from CEH4's own trajectory (Figure 19). CEH18 sits consistently negative relative to CEH4 throughout the full record including the pre-scraping baseline, indicating that this well has always sat deeper than its control rather than having been driven down by the clearfell; only a modest recovery is visible after the October 2023 scraping. CEH21's rising CUSUM from 2022 onwards should be interpreted cautiously as its paired control CEH22 is itself deteriorating through coastal erosion, which inflates the apparent relative improvement at CEH21 independently of any scraping benefit; these results are noted for completeness only.

After Tier 1 correction for background drift, the net benefit at CEH36 reversed to −0.049 m during the Felling Pulse era, as the coastal erosion signal in CEH4 — compounded from 2018 by the secondary clearfell pulse — progressively eroded the scraping gain. The raw Tier 2 CUSUM tells a different story, with CEH36 rising strongly relative to CEH4 through this period — but as established above, this reflects CEH4's own deterioration rather than genuine improvement at CEH36. At CEH18 and CEH21, scraped in October 2023, no scraping-specific response survived Tier 1 correction for background drift. CEH18's corrected net benefit was −0.073 m after scraping; CEH21's paired control (CEH22) is itself on a coastal erosion trajectory, so a net benefit cannot be reliably estimated. The monitoring window for both wells is short (two post-scraping summers) and the analysis is likely underpowered to detect benefits of the magnitude observed at CEH36.

The annual summer minimum records for all three treatment wells, partitioned by management era and plotted against the Curreli et al. (2013) ecological thresholds, are presented in Figure 21. The progressive deepening of the CEH4 control well across all eras — from 1.16 m at baseline to 1.18 m during the Pure Scraping era and 1.40 m during the Felling Pulse — is consistent with the system-wide climate drying trend documented in Section 4.8.1 and confirms that management benefit must be assessed against this background trajectory rather than in absolute terms.

### <span id="anchor-16"></span>**Scraping Propagation into the Surrounding Aquifer**

**A supplementary analysis tested whether the CEH36 scraping signal propagated uphill into the surrounding aquifer, comparing split-window SSM fits at ten wells between 250 m and 780 m from CEH36, with BACI correction against seven distant C3 control wells. Under a full three-coefficient refit, all eight wells within 575 m returned a positive BACI-corrected change in the drainage coefficient (mean Δβ₃ = +5.0 × 10⁻³; one-sample t = 4.98, p = 0.002). Taken at face value this might suggest an uphill propagation of the scraping perturbation, but two independent lines of evidence indicate it does not represent a physical drainage response.**

First, the uphill wells traverse documented pre-existing spatial gradients in the drainage coefficient that are unrelated to the scraping event. The wells nearer CEH36 lie within the C3 Western Residual cluster, which carries a significant internal β₃ gradient along the south-west to north-east axis (Section 4.9.2; β₃ increasing inland, r = +0.58, p = 0.006); the higher uphill wells lie within the C4 and C5 forest zone, where β₃ shows a significant elevation dependence (Section 4.9.5; r = −0.77, p = 0.001). A split-window fit comparing CEH36 against wells arranged along these gradients cannot separate a genuine propagating signal from the static spatial structure already present in the aquifer. Consistent with a spatial-structure origin, the BACI-corrected Δβ₃ shows no decay with distance from CEH36 — the signature of a co-located gradient rather than a perturbation radiating from a point source.

Second, the aquifer diffusivity timescale places a physical bound on how far a genuine drainage perturbation could propagate within the observation window. With L²Sy/K exceeding seven years at a separation of 570 m (Fetter, 2001), a real drainage front could not traverse the uphill well array within the 31-month post-scraping record. The scraping benefit at CEH36 is therefore best understood as a direct geometric perturbation confined to the scraped well itself (Figure 25): the inferred ground surface lowering of approximately 0.42 m (back-calculated as D = H₀/Sy from the measured BACI step H₀ ≈ 0.13 m and open-dune Sy ≈ 0.31) produces an immediate water-table shallowing that partially re-equilibrates over approximately two years, retaining +129 mm as the BACI-corrected benefit during the pure scraping era (Section 4.5.2). The SSM forward residual analysis (Figure 19b) confirms near-zero deviation during this period, indicating a permanent surface-lowering effect rather than a change in the aquifer's dynamic response properties.

### <span id="anchor-17"></span>**Scenario Contextualization**

> To contextualize the observed scraping benefit at CEH36, five alternative interventions were compared at the same well under two climate forcings (09d). Under annual-mean forcing (Figure 26), the observed scraping benefit (+45.2 mm w.e./month) substantially exceeds any alternative: clearfell +15.4, thinning +7.7, broadleaf +4.5, and climate wet +7.7 mm/month, against −13.8 mm/month under climate dry. Evaluated under summer forcing (Figure 27) the ranking is unchanged and the forestry gains are marginally smaller (clearfell +13.5, thinning +6.7, broadleaf +2.9), while the climate-dry penalty is marginally larger (−14.4); both figures report the same equilibrium volumetric quantity and are directly comparable. Both figures also show the modelled off-site neighbour drawdown the scrape imposes on the surrounding water table (−30.3 mm w.e./month at 100 m, decaying to −15.6 at 250 m): scraping raises the slack locally but draws its neighbours down, a dipole absent from canopy management. Even at a single well, scraping delivers an order-of-magnitude larger on-site response than canopy management, confirming that it operates through a fundamentally different mechanism — creating a topographic drain rather than modifying the surface energy balance. (For reference, the observed paired-BACI summer-minimum shift at CEH36 was +165 mm; the equilibrium framework does not resolve summer minima, so it is not plotted.)

## <span id="anchor-18"></span>The December 2017 Experimental Clearfell: Monitoring and Retrospective Analysis

### <span id="anchor-19"></span>**Interpretive Context**

Three features of the monitoring record bear on interpretation of the clearfell results. First, wells within the clearfell footprint (FE1–FE4, LIS1) and the scraping treatment well CEH36 were excluded from the BACI design on the grounds of insufficient or confounded pre-felling baselines (Section 3.5.4). FE1 and FE2, which have approximately 28 months of pre-felling record but no pre-scraping baseline, were excluded from the primary BACI design because their late start dates create an unstable impact centroid when pooled with WMC3 — the changing well composition introduces spurious level shifts that the ANCOVA conflates with the intervention steps. These wells were instead included as a robustness check via donor-regression synthetic extension, which provides a stable-composition centroid across all three eras (Section 4.6.7). Second, all clearfell analyses use the period from 2010 onwards as the effective baseline, because only three of nine C4 wells were operational before mid-2010 and these are unrepresentative of the cluster (Section 4.2.1). Third, the April 2015 scraping event at CEH36 produced a significant level shift 32 months before the clearfell; the ANCOVA's distance-weighted scraping covariate absorbs this, but the short pre-clearfell window encompasses only three summer minima, limiting baseline power.

### <span id="anchor-20"></span>**Climate Sensitivity**

The CWB × clearfell interaction was non-significant in the Forest control models (Impact p = 0.40, Edge p = 0.16), indicating that the rate at which the BACI gap responds to cumulative water balance deficit did not detectably change after felling when that response is modelled as a single linear slope. The relationship is shown in Figure 28: the fitted pre-felling and post-felling climate-response lines are effectively parallel, consistent with canopy removal having raised the mean water table level (Section 4.6.3) without altering the linear sensitivity of the system to climate forcing.

A linear slope is, however, a blunt instrument for the buffering hypothesis as it would most plausibly operate. If the canopy moderated the water table specifically against climate *extremes*, the effect would be concentrated at the wet or dry tails of the cumulative water balance range and could average close to zero across the full range, leaving a linear interaction undetectably small. To test this directly, a non-linear extension of the headline model was fitted, adding a centred CWB² term and a CWB² × clearfell interaction to the full ANCOVA specification. The CWB² × clearfell interaction is significant at both zones (Impact −27.5 mm per (100 mm CWB)², p = 0.016; Edge −22.2 mm per (100 mm CWB)², p = 0.008), the curvature model is preferred over the linear model by ΔAIC of −4.2 at Impact and −10.7 at Edge, and the joint F-test of the two added curvature terms is significant at both zones. The CWB² main effect is non-significant — the pre-felling climate response is linear; it is specifically the post-felling response that is curved. The negative interaction sign means the post-felling response is concave in cumulative water balance: the felling-attributable rise in the water table is larger in dry, low-water-balance conditions than in wet ones.

This is reported as a preliminary finding rather than a confirmed buffering effect. It rests on a single interacted quadratic term and one felling event at one site, and a climate-state-dependent felling response has alternative explanations — post-felling non-stationary drift, or the coastal-retreat gradient — that this analysis does not fully exclude. It is also a sensitivity variant only: the headline clearfell step and the linear ANCOVA model of Section 4.6.3 are unchanged. With those caveats, the result indicates that the clearfell's effect on the water table was not uniform across climate conditions but concentrated in dry periods, which is the pattern a dry-season canopy-buffering mechanism would produce. The interpretation is developed in Section 5.

The Climate-control models — which contrast the felled wells against the unforested Western Block — return non-significant CWB × clearfell interactions (Impact p = 0.55, Edge p = 0.28). The Forest-control comparison is the position-matched test and its results, linear and non-linear, are the headline findings; the Climate-control interactions are not interpreted as a clearfell-specific change in canopy water-balance physics.

Against the Forest control, the clearfell step was +120 mm for Impact (p &lt; 0.001) and +33 mm for Edge (p = 0.19) (Figure 30, Figure 31). The scraping covariate was significant in both Forest-control models: +344 mm at Impact (p = 0.002) and +590 mm at Edge (p &lt; 0.001). Sensitivity analysis across three decay length scales (λ = 200, 300, 500 m) produced consistent clearfell coefficients, confirming that the scraping and clearfell terms are temporally well-separated. These covariate coefficients index the temporal separation of the two interventions in the model; they are not a physical estimate of the scraping effect on the impact well. In the raw, uncorrected BACI gap the two scraping events each *lower* the WMC3-minus-forest-control level — consistent with drainage of the neighbouring aquifer toward the lowered scrape surface — whereas the climate-corrected covariate carries the opposite sign because it is estimated jointly with the cumulative-water-balance and easting × time terms. Figure 29 shows the raw gap and its per-era steps directly.

The raw before-and-after evidence for all three interventions is shown in Figure 29. In the uncorrected WMC3-minus-forest-control gap, the December 2017 clearfell registers a +41 mm difference-in-differences step — the raw counterpart to the +120 mm climate-corrected ANCOVA step above, the two differing because the raw step retains the drier-than-average pre-felling climate the ANCOVA removes. The gap also steps downward at each scraping event, by a near-identical amount (−55 mm in 2015, −54 mm in 2023). The direction and the consistency across two independent events are what the drainage-drawdown mechanism would produce: the lowered scrape surface acts as a local drainage focus and draws down heads in the surrounding aquifer, including at WMC3 262 m from CEH36. This is a head-level effect, distinct from the drainage-coefficient (Δβ₃) propagation tested in Section 4.5.5 — where WMC3 itself returned an essentially zero BACI-corrected Δβ₃ — so the two observations are consistent: the well is drawn down in level without its intrinsic drainage response changing. The drainage response in the CEH36 area is rapid relative to the record — the fitted drainage half-life (ln 2 / β₃) is 13–21 months at the wells within ~400 m of the scrape — so the local aquifer turns over roughly twice within the 31-month post-scraping window, fast enough for a scraping-induced drawdown to register at WMC3 within the record. The 2015 drawdown is the same scraping signal that confounds the pre-felling baseline of the surrounding within-footprint wells; WMC3 alone has a pre-2015 record that lets the scraping step be separated, which is why it is retained as the sole impact well while CEH36 and the FE wells are excluded (Section 4.6.1). The steps remain uncorrected for differential climate sensitivity between WMC3 and the forest control, so they are presented as consistent with scraping drawdown rather than as a confirmed magnitude.

****Four-zone pooled-panel cross-check.**** The three-counterfactual ANCOVA above fits the felling step six times — once per (control, zone) pair — and each fit has its own dependent variable, because the BACI gap is the target centroid minus a control centroid and the control centroid differs between the Forest, Climate and Combined configurations. The six steps therefore do not share a covariance matrix and are not subtractable. As a cross-check, a four-zone pooled-panel model (Script 10k) was fitted: the Forest control, a second western-dune control (C3/Warren), the Edge tier and the Impact well are stacked into one long-form panel, and every zone contrast is read off a single internally-consistent joint fit. This ****supplements, and does not replace, the three-counterfactual ANCOVA, which remains the principal clearfell analysis.****

The joint fit returns three primary zone-versus-Forest monthly contrasts: Impact−Forest +0.032 m (95% CI \[+0.010, +0.053\], p = 0.004), Edge−Forest −0.030 m (p = 0.13, ns), and C3/Warren−Forest +0.005 m (p = 0.65, ns); joint model R² = 0.856, N = 2475 well-months. The four-zone result is consistent with the principal analysis. The joint-fit Impact−Forest step (+0.032 m) is smaller in magnitude than the three-counterfactual Forest × Impact step (+0.120 m); the two estimators partition climate variance differently — the joint panel estimates climate sensitivity from the full cross-zone monthly record, so a portion of the monthly variance the separately-fitted ANCOVA attributes to the felling step is attributed to climate in the joint fit. The felling response is positive and statistically significant under both estimators, and only the magnitude is sensitive to the partitioning choice. Dropping the easting × time covariate moves the joint-fit Impact step only from +0.032 m to +0.033 m, significance unchanged.

The Impact−Edge difference of +0.061 m is an exact arithmetic combination of two primary contrasts; its standard error is covariance-dependent and not comparable to a primary contrast, so it is not cited with a p-value here. It is instead corroborated independently by the two-zone Impact-versus-Edge estimator (Script 10j), which returns a monthly Impact−Edge step of +0.063 m (p = 0.0002) — the close agreement between the two estimators is the warrant for the contrast.

**Distributional comparison.** Figure 33 shows the distribution of summer minima across the five BACI tiers across three phases: pre-scraping baseline (2007 to April 2015), the 2015–17 era, and post-felling 2018 onwards. The Forest Control group shows stable summer minima throughout. The Impact and Edge tiers track the Forest Control trajectory across the three phases. The Coastal Control tier (CEH19, CEH17) shows progressive deepening across all three phases.

**Four-zone pooled-panel cross-check (summer minimum).** Whereas the directly-fitted Jun–Sep ANCOVA above estimates the felling step from monthly observations in the Jun–Sep window as a differenced centroid, the four-zone pooled-panel model (Section 3.5.4) estimates every zone contrast from a single joint fit on the annual summer-minimum series (Script 10l) — the annual deepest point rather than the summer-window mean. The three primary zone-versus-Forest contrasts were Impact−Forest −7 mm (95% CI \[−23, +8\], p = 0.36), Edge−Forest −29 mm (p = 0.16) and C3/Warren−Forest +12 mm (p = 0.26); joint model R² = 0.724, N = 181 well-years. No zone contrast resolves a significant differential felling response at the summer minimum.

The four summer-window analyses converge: the per-well Welch tests, the pooled mixed-effects model, the directly-fitted Jun–Sep ANCOVA and the four-zone pooled-panel cross-check all return non-significant clearfell steps at the Impact and Edge tiers. The felling produced a measurable rise in the felled compartment's monthly-mean water table (Section 4.6.3) but no detectable change at the summer minimum — the driest and most ecologically critical point of the year, and the level against which the Curreli SD15b and SD16 slack thresholds are defined.

Five-year mean spring water level. The same absence of a discrete felling response is seen in the five-year mean spring water level (MSL5; Section 4.8.4), the baseline metric most directly tied to dune-slack vegetation (van Willegen et al. 2025). At the Impact well WMC3, MSL5 carried no step at the December 2017 felling: the window-end values deepen through the post-felling years (−1.00 m at window-end 2018, −1.12 m at 2019, holding near −1.05 m through 2023) before recovering to −0.90 m at window-end 2024. The four Edge wells (CEH16, CEH20, CEH30, CEH31) show the same pattern — flat to slightly deepening from 2018 to 2023, then a recovery first appearing at window-end 2024. The 2024 recovery is not clearfell-attributable: it appears synchronously across the felled wells, the unfelled Forest control, and every other cluster in the network (Section 4.8.4, Figure 42), six years after the intervention, and reflects the 2023–24 hydrological year entering the rolling five-year window rather than a delayed canopy-removal response. On the baseline metric, as on the summer minimum, the clearfell produced no detectable step against the background climate signal (Figure 32 shows the summer-minimum result by tier). The mean-monthly recovery established by the ANCOVA (Section 4.6.3) is thus specific to the monthly-mean level; it is not expressed in either of the two ecologically referenced metrics — the summer minimum or the five-year spring baseline — against which dune-slack viability is assessed.

### **Coefficient Decomposition**

The SSM was fitted independently to Before (record-length-balanced pre-felling window) and After (felling to end of record) windows for each well in the 17-well network, yielding per-well coefficient shifts Δβ₁, Δβ₂ and Δβ₃ (Script 10e). This decomposition is a mechanistic diagnostic: it characterizes *which* SSM pathway — recharge, atmospheric draw or drainage — shifted between eras and in *what direction*. It is not an estimate of the clearfell magnitude; the clearfell magnitude is the BACI step reported in Section 4.6.3. The per-era fits use the canonical no-intercept SSM (Section 3.5), consistent with Script 03 and the master coefficient table.

β₁ (recharge) declined at most wells across all five tiers: mean Δβ₁ = −0.10 at Impact, −0.27 at Edge, −0.05 at Forest Control, −0.32 at Coastal Control and −0.13 at Climate Control. The network-mean shift across the seventeen-well BACI network is Δβ₁ = −0.16, a 5.6% reduction against a pre-felling network mean of 2.81. The decline is present at the unfelled Climate Control and Forest Control tiers as well as at the treatment tiers, indicating a site-wide phenomenon rather than a clearfell-specific response — its candidacy as a mechanism for the network-wide summer-minimum decline is taken up in Section 4.8 and Section 5.5.

β₂ (atmospheric draw) increased at the unfelled Forest-Control wells (mean Δβ₂ = +0.15) with no comparable rise at the non-forest tiers — the direction and tier-specificity expected if progressive canopy maturation raises the canopy's share of the PET budget at mature unfelled pine. The shift is not a climate-sampling effect: refitting β₂ on a common PET support across the two eras leaves the Forest-Control increase positive while the Climate-Control tier remains negative, so a change in climate forcing — common to all tiers — cannot account for the forest-specific direction. The Impact-tier β₂ shift (Δβ₂ = −0.28) is a single-well estimate lying within one standard error of zero and is not interpreted; Edge-tier shifts were near zero in the mean (−0.03) and incoherent in sign across wells.

β₃ (drainage) shifts were small and spatially incoherent across all tiers (mean Δβ₃ between −0.01 and 0.00 at every tier), consistent with no systematic change in the drainage pathway.

Individual wells departed from these tier means. Within the Forest Control, NW10 and CEH2 showed β₁ increases (Δβ₁ = +0.28 and +0.12) against a network of general decline. These departures are retained: the per-well era coefficients carry wide confidence intervals (Figure 35) and exclusion would not be justified on physical grounds.

The decomposition therefore identifies a site-wide recharge decline and a forest-maturation signal in atmospheric draw, but does not by itself isolate a clearfell-specific coefficient signature; the clearfell effect is quantified by the BACI analysis of Section 4.6.3.

### <span id="anchor-24"></span>**Robustness**

Three independent methods were applied to test whether a clearfell-specific signal could be detected once the ANCOVA assumptions were relaxed.

**SSM residual normalization.** The SSM was calibrated on the pre-felling record for each well and run forward iteratively into the post-felling era. The Impact well WMC3 showed a +56 mm normalized step — its post-felling water table 56 mm above the pre-felling SSM prediction. The Forest Control wells drifted upward by a larger mean of +82 mm over the same period, so against the Forest-control drift the Impact-specific component is approximately −26 mm: the Impact well's upward residual is slightly smaller than the unfelled forest controls', and this method does not isolate a positive clearfell-specific step.

**Synthetic control.** A synthetic counterfactual for WMC3 constructed from six optimally weighted donor wells showed an Impact step of +99 mm (p = 0.001); the corresponding Edge synthetic-control step was +41 mm (p = 0.22), non-significant. The synthetic-control and SSM-residual methods agree that WMC3's gross post-felling step is of order +50 to +100 mm; they differ on how much survives normalization against the unfelled forest controls, and neither, after that normalization, resolves a clearfell-specific step distinguishable from zero.

**Clearfell transect.** Step changes across a transect from the plantation interior to the felling core showed no significant spatial gradient (−0.4 mm per 100 m, p = 0.19). A distance-decay response would be strongest at WMC3 and diminish outward; the observed pattern is statistically uniform, consistent with a common climate signal (Figure 36). The post-felling spread in the transect hydrographs is nonetheless visibly wider than pre-felling (Figure 36, panel a), with WMC3 consistently above the pack — a distance-dependent response that the seven-well transect regression lacks the power to resolve.

**Synthetic FE-well extension.** FE1 and FE2 — which sit inside or at the immediate edge of the clearfell compartment but lack pre-scraping baselines — were extended backwards using OLS donor regression calibrated on Forest Control wells (calibration R² &gt; 0.99, RMSE 18–25 mm; Section 4.6.1). Against the Forest control, the centroid of WMC3 + FE2 produced a clearfell step of +103 mm (95% CI \[+45, +162\], p &lt; 0.001, R² = 0.34), consistent with the WMC3-only ANCOVA estimate of +120 mm (Section 4.6.3). FE2's post-felling divergence from its own donor counterfactual was +28 mm (p &lt; 0.001), indicating a significant within-compartment response. Extending the centroid to WMC3 + FE1 + FE2 returned a Forest-control clearfell step of +85 mm (95% CI \[+39, +131\], p &lt; 0.001). FE1 sits approximately 20 m outside the felling boundary in standing forest, so including it attenuates the step magnitude — from +120 mm (WMC3 alone) through +103 mm (WMC3 + FE2) to +85 mm — but the within-compartment signal remains highly significant throughout.

### <span id="anchor-26"></span>**Mechanistic State-Space Equations**

The cluster-level state-space equations (Section 3.6.1) fitted without intercept produced statistically significant coefficients at all five clusters (all p &lt; 0.001 except the C4 drainage coefficient β₃, where p = 0.002), confirming that the physical mass-balance formulation is well-supported by the data. Model fit ranged from R² = 0.813 at Western Residual (C3) to R² = 0.680 at Coastal Forest (C5), with the two forest clusters (C4 R² = 0.685, C5 R² = 0.680) the least well fit — consistent with the greater storage buffering of the forest clusters, whose higher LCSC values (Table 3) attenuate the direct rainfall–water-table coupling the SSM captures. The full coefficient values are reported in Section 4.2.2; note that the β coefficients used in the forecasting tool are expressed in m/mm rainfall, one thousand times smaller than the m/m values in Table 3.

### <span id="anchor-27"></span>**Seasonal Prediction Equations**

The prediction equations were fitted at hydrological-year resolution, one equation per cluster under the k = 5 partition (Table 12). The two predictor terms — winter rainfall total (P\_winter) and antecedent summer minimum (h\_min) — show a clear spatial partitioning of dominance across the site.

In the Eastern Block, winter rainfall is the dominant predictor. P\_winter is significant at Lake Edge (p = 0.002) and Dune (p = 0.004), while h\_min is non-significant in both (p = 0.545 and 0.475 respectively). This is consistent with the faster-draining, lower-memory character of the eastern aquifer, where same-season rainfall dominates the winter flooding response.

In the Western Residual and forest clusters, the pattern reverses. The antecedent summer minimum is the dominant predictor: Western Residual (p = 0.017, R² = 0.638), Forest (p &lt; 0.001, R² = 0.865) and Coastal Forest (p &lt; 0.001, R² = 0.882). Winter rainfall is non-significant at all three (p = 0.078, 0.919 and 0.379 respectively). The depth of the preceding summer drought is a stronger predictor of winter flooding potential than the actual winter rainfall amount in these more buffered domains. This reflects the aquifer's multi-month memory — a deep summer minimum requires progressively more winter rainfall simply to restore the water table to its pre-drought position before any flooding can occur. The significant intercepts at Forest (p = 0.035) and Coastal Forest (p = 0.003) are consistent with the strong structural offset imposed by the plantation canopy on water table depth.

Two individual coefficients require comment. The negative h\_min coefficient at Lake Edge (−0.134) is not mechanistically meaningful given its non-significance; it is an artefact of the small sample (n = 19) and the dominance of P\_winter in that cluster. The negative P\_winter coefficient in the Forest cluster (−0.00006) reflects multicollinearity with h\_min and should not be interpreted mechanistically.

### <span id="anchor-29"></span>**Spatial Distribution of P\_flood and Flooding Status Across the Well Network**

Application of the cluster-level P\_flood equations derived in Section 3.6.3 to each well in the 88 well classified network (66 reference wells with per-well SSM coefficients; 22 extended wells assigned cluster-level coefficients via the Pearson affinity audit of Section 3.3), using that well's observed mean summer minimum as h₀, produces a per-well critical rainfall threshold expressed as either millimetres of cumulative winter rainfall or as a dimensionless multiplier λ = P\_flood / Σ P\_clim, where Σ P\_clim is the long-term mean rainfall over the cluster-specific recharge horizon (401 mm for C1/C2 over Oct–Jan; 464 mm for C3/C4/C5 over Oct–Feb). A well with λ = 1.0 would flood under an average winter; λ &gt; 1.0 indicates that above-average rainfall is required. The network-wide picture is summarized in Figures 36–39.

The summer minimum depth map (Figure 37; Section 3.6.5) classifies each well against the five eco-hydrological bands derived from Curreli et al. (2013) and introduced in Section 2: SD15b wet slack viability (&lt;0.61 m), SD15b-recoverable (0.61–0.75 m), SD16 dry slack (0.75–0.98 m), SD16-recoverable (0.98–1.20 m), and beyond-recoverable (&gt;1.20 m). The two forest clusters (C4 and C5) show the deepest mean summer minima across the network, consistently below 0.98 m. By contrast, the majority of C1 Lake Edge wells maintain mean summer minima within or close to the SD15b wet slack zone, reflecting the high-frequency recharge dynamics of the shallow till substrate. Of the 88 wells in the classified network, 41 have mean summer minima deeper than 0.98 m — the SD16 dry slack threshold — of which 24 are below the SD16-recovery threshold of 1.20 m.

### <span id="anchor-30"></span>Critical Rainfall Threshold Equations (P\_flood)

The structural difference between clusters is immediately apparent from the threshold equations (Table 15; Section 3.6.3). The coefficients from Table 3, together with the cluster-specific recharge horizons from October to each cluster's historical peak month (four months for C1 and C2, five months for C3, C4 and C5) and the long-term monthly RAF Valley rainfall and PET climatology, propagate through the iterated closed form of Section 3.6.3 to produce a linear relationship between observed summer-minimum depth and required winter rainfall. The C4 recharge horizon endpoint is marginal — the cluster's historical mean peak falls between February and March depending on the treatment of partial monitoring years — and the shorter February horizon is adopted as the conservative choice; the C1, C2, C3 and C5 horizons are unambiguous.

The iterated derivation reduces to the form P\_flood = A · d + B for each cluster, where d is the observed summer-minimum depth below ground (m, positive) and P\_flood is the cumulative winter rainfall required over the cluster's recharge horizon (mm). The slope A represents the marginal rainfall demand per additional metre of summer drawdown, and the intercept B represents the rainfall demand required even for a well already at the surface — non-zero because accumulated winter PET continues to draw down the water table through the recharge season. Both terms express the cluster gradient directly: A rises from 173 mm per metre at C1 through 213 (C2) and 237 (C3) to 364 (C5) and 375 (C4), and B ranges from 287 mm (C4) to 424 mm (C5).

As a worked example, at a typical C3 well with a mean summer minimum of 1.0 m below ground, P\_flood = 236.56 × 1.0 + 375.26 = 611.8 mm of cumulative winter rainfall over the October–February recharge horizon, corresponding to a rainfall multiplier λ = 612/464 = 1.32 times the long-term climatological total. The same depth at a C2 well produces P\_flood = 213.04 × 1.0 + 296.15 = 509.2 mm (λ = 509/401 = 1.27), a 103 mm difference reflecting the stronger antecedent-depth dependence and longer recharge horizon of C3. These differences translate directly into operational recoverability status: the C2 well lies within a marginally-wet-winter-reachable range, while the C3 well requires slightly wetter conditions over a longer accumulation of months.

### <span id="anchor-32"></span>**Summer and Winter Trajectories**

Summer minimum trends were fitted for all five clusters over the monitoring period (Section 3.6.4; Figure 44, panel a). All clusters show declining summer minima, although the rates and significance levels vary substantially. C1 Lake Edge declined at −0.0096 m yr⁻¹ (R² = 0.196, p = 0.052, n = 20) and C5 Coastal Forest at −0.0377 m yr⁻¹ (R² = 0.396, p = 0.003, n = 20) — the only two clusters reaching statistical significance at the 5% level. C2 Dune was marginal (−0.0122 m yr⁻¹, p = 0.056, n = 21). C3 Western Residual (−0.0066 m yr⁻¹, p = 0.336) and C4 Main Forest (−0.0115 m yr⁻¹, p = 0.398) were non-significant. The moderate R² values across all clusters reflect substantial interannual variability rather than an absence of signal.

These trends translate into progressively worsening threshold compliance. C1 has been below the wet slack summer viability threshold (−0.61 m) in all 20 monitored years, with the shallowest summer minimum recorded in 2016 (−0.704 m). C2 has similarly remained below the threshold throughout the record, with its shallowest minimum in 2005 (−0.682 m). C3 has never approached the wet slack threshold, ranging between −0.955 m and −1.561 m, and falling below the dry slack threshold (−0.98 m) in 20 of 21 years.

**Winter maximum trends **were fitted for all five clusters. C1 showed a positive but non-significant trend (+0.0087 m yr⁻¹, p = 0.119); C2 (+0.0062, p = 0.477), C3 (+0.0034, p = 0.769) and C4 (−0.0034, p = 0.850) were all non-significant. C5 showed a significant declining winter maximum trend (−0.0368 m yr⁻¹, p = 0.035), indicating that this cluster is deteriorating in both seasons.

**The C5 Coastal Forest trajectory anomaly** warrants specific comment. At −0.038 m yr⁻¹ in summer and −0.037 m yr⁻¹ in winter, C5's decline rate is 3.9 times the summer rate of C1 — the only other cluster with a statistically significant summer trend — and C5 is the only cluster with significant trends in both seasons. The magnitude of this divergence argues against a purely climate-driven explanation, since all clusters share the same climate forcing. Three candidate explanations, which are not mutually exclusive, are identified: amplified head response per unit climate forcing due to thin saturated thickness at the coastal margin, where a given recharge deficit produces a proportionally larger head change at lower Sy; progressive coastal boundary retreat, reducing aquifer volume at the seaward edge and producing an ongoing decline with no equilibrium; and progressive canopy maturation, increasing interception and transpiration demand as the Corsican pine stand ages.

The post-2018 collapse in C5 seasonal amplitude (−18% raw, −21% climate-normalized; Section 4.2.1) — the largest amplitude reduction in the network — reinforces the structural interpretation: recharge peaks are no longer reaching their pre-2018 heights even after correcting for drought summers, suggesting that at least one site-specific process is operating beyond the common climate signal. The non-stabilization of the C5 CUSUM trajectory through to the end of the monitoring record favours an ongoing process (coastal erosion or continued canopy maturation) over a one-off step change.

### **Network-scale partition of the summer-minimum decline.** 

The third candidate mechanism for the C5 anomaly — progressive coastal boundary retreat — can be tested quantitatively at network scale. A non-linear panel regression was fitted to the full 21-year monthly record (12,457 observations across 72 wells; FE wells, the scraped well CEH36, and the BACI clearfell-zone wells excluded) to test whether the C5 anomaly is quantitatively consistent with coastal boundary retreat operating site-wide. Each well's perpendicular distance to the eroding Caernarfon Bay High Water Mark was computed from the OS Open Map Local Tidal Boundary (Menai Strait and Llanddwyn Island excluded as non-eroding boundaries). The headline fit is the forest-free panel (C4 and C5 dropped), giving a Dupuit–Forchheimer steady-st ate strip-aquifer form with a coast-edge anomaly δ₀ of −29.0 ± 1.9 mm yr⁻¹ above the climate background, an inland reach L of 894 ± 52 m beyond which the gradient effect vanishes, and a climate background c of −6.4 ± 0.6 mm yr⁻¹ consistent within one standard error with the network-mean per-well summer-minimum slope. The linear-with-cutoff form and an exponential decay are statistically indistinguishable (ΔAIC = 0.5); the linear-with-cutoff form is retained on physical grounds, as the Dupuit–Forchheimer steady-state solution for a strip aquifer with a fixed inland reach. ### <span id="anchor-33"></span>**Observed Winter Maximum Flooding Record**

The observed winter flooding record reveals a stark spatial contrast between structural domains (Figure 44, panel b). The C1 Lake Edge cluster exceeded the wet slack winter flooding threshold (−0.10 m) in 14 of 20 monitored years (70%), confirming that the eastern lake-influenced slacks retain substantial winter flooding capacity. C2 Dune exceeded the threshold in 11 of 21 years (52%), indicating episodic but reliable flooding in the eastern mature dune zone. C3 Western Residual exceeded the wet slack threshold in only 1 of 21 years (5%) — occurring in 2007 — and the dry slack threshold (−0.25 m) in 6 of 21 years, confirming that the Western Residual fails to achieve the minimum winter flooding conditions required to maintain even dry slack communities in all but exceptional years. C4 Main Forest did not exceed either threshold in any monitored year. C5 Coastal Forest exceeded the wet slack threshold in 1 of 20 years (5%).

### Five-Year Mean Spring Water Level (MSL5)

The cluster-level summer-minimum trajectories examined in Sections 4.8.1–4.8.3 describe stress events — single annual values capturing the deepest water-table excursion of each year. A vegetation community's response is not driven by stress events alone; it integrates the long-term baseline water level over which it grows. The 5-year mean spring water level (MSL5), as defined in Section 3.7.5 and implemented per van Willegen et al. (2025), is the metric that captures this baseline. It is the rolling 5-year mean of the cluster-mean March–May water table, taken at the end of each hydrological year. Where the summer-minimum metric is sensitive to single dry years, MSL5 is sensitive to sustained hydrological state — the regime within which dune-slack vegetation establishes and persists.

The cluster-mean MSL5 trajectories for window-ends 2014–2025 are shown in Figure 42, and the per-cluster current-window values and threshold-crossing counts are summarized in Table 17. Two horizontal reference values, drawn from Curreli et al. (2013), separate viable hydrological regimes for SD15b wet-slack vegetation (above −0.61 m) and SD16 dry-slack vegetation (above −0.98 m); the SD16 zone is shaded for visual emphasis of cluster–window combinations sitting inside the dry-slack ecohydrological regime.

The lake-edge and open-dune clusters sit comfortably above the wet-slack threshold throughout the recorded window-end range. C1 Lake Edge and C2 Dune both hold their MSL5 within the SD15b regime for every recorded window-end (Table 17). On the baseline metric, the core open-dune system supports SD15b hydrology robustly: the summer-minimum threshold-approach trajectory documented for C1 in Section 4.8.1 reflects a separate stress-event signal that does not extend to the 5-year baseline. The two metrics describe different things and, at the open-dune clusters, point in different directions.

The Western Residual cluster (C3) sits close to but largely above the wet-slack threshold. C3 is below SD15b in six of twelve recorded window-ends (Table 17) and never below SD16, with current-window MSL5 −0.485 m. The cluster's intermittent excursion below SD15b is consistent with its western-margin character (Section 4.9.2) rather than with sustained sub-wet-slack hydrology. C3 is, on the baseline metric, a marginal SD15b cluster: it qualifies as wet-slack viable in approximately half the recorded windows and falls just below it in the others.

The forest clusters present a substantially different picture. C4 Main Forest sits below the SD15b wet-slack threshold for all twelve recorded window-ends, and below the SD16 dry-slack threshold for eight of those twelve. C5 Coastal Forest sits below the SD16 dry-slack threshold for all twelve recorded window-ends — the only cluster to do so. For C4 and C5, the 5-year baseline metric describes a sustained departure from the SD15b vegetation regime, with C5 sustained inside the dry-slack regime as a long-term hydrological state. MSL5 is, in the van Willegen framework, primarily an open-dune metric; the forest clusters' MSL5 deficits are consistent with canopy interception suppressing winter–spring recharge (Section 3.5). The C5 trajectory rests on nine wells (five reference and four extended-network) located in the south-western Corsican-pine block; the cluster is the smallest in the classified network and the result should be read with that in mind, but the wells are spatially coherent and the trajectory is unambiguous within that reach.

The spatial pattern of the MSL5 surface across the network is shown in Figure 43. The figure is an IDW interpolation across the 85 wells that carry at least one valid MSL5 window; for 75 of these the latest valid window ends in 2025, while for eight, recent gaps in spring measurements mean the latest valid window ends earlier (2015–2024), and these appear at their latest available value. The map is distinct from the cluster-mean trajectories of Figure 42 in that it shows the full spatial reach of the per-well MSL5 surface rather than the cluster-identity-based aggregation. The two figures show complementary things — Figure 42 is cluster-mean MSL5 over time, Figure 43 is the per-well MSL5 surface at the most recent window. The map shows the orange-red dry-slack zone concentrated in the south-western corner of the site — the C5 Coastal Forest block — with the surrounding open-dune network in the blue wet-slack range. The Western Residual cluster's western margin appears in the orange-yellow transition zone visible on the map's western edge, consistent with C3's marginal SD15b position in the trajectory analysis.

The most recent window-ends carry a synchronous upward step. All five clusters jump wetter between window-end 2023 and window-end 2024, with the magnitude varying by cluster (Figure 42). The step reflects the 5-year window incorporating the 2023–24 hydrological year, which carried significantly higher annual rainfall than the 2018–22 range it displaces. The magnitude varies because the displacement-sensitive clusters (C4 +220 mm, C5 +132 mm) respond more strongly than the near-surface ones (C1 +50 mm) to a single wet year entering the rolling window. Because the metric is a 5-year rolling mean, the 2024 step's persistence depends on subsequent years: a return to the recent dry hydrological pattern would erode the 2024 jump as it ages out of the window. The 2025 value already shows slight partial reversion at C5 (from −1.162 m at 2024 to −1.140 m at 2025). The 2024 step is therefore a real but climatically-driven recent recovery, not a sustained shift; trend extrapolation from the late-record portion of the trajectory should be avoided.

The MSL5 metric requires a five-year record of valid spring measurements. The earliest valid window-ends fall in 2009–2011 depending on cluster, but the early portion of the record carries only one to five wells contributing per cluster — too few for cluster-mean representativeness, given within-cluster well-to-well variability of order 0.3 m at C2 and 0.4 m at C3 and C4. The trajectory plot starts at window-end 2014, which is the earliest window-end for which all five clusters carry sufficient contributing wells. Earlier window-ends are computed and stored in the supporting outputs for completeness, but are not plotted or discussed here.

The headline ecological reading of the MSL5 trajectory is therefore threefold. The open-dune SAC vegetation regime (C1, C2) is supported by a 5-year baseline well within the SD15b range, and has been throughout the recorded window-end range; this is the central conservation finding for the open-dune system. The Western Residual cluster (C3) is marginal for SD15b — qualifying in some windows and not in others — with the current-window value above the threshold but the longer record showing intermittent excursion below. The forest clusters (C4, C5) sit in the dry-slack regime as a sustained state, with C5 in particular below SD16 throughout the recorded window-end range; the 2024 recovery does not undo this for C5, and only partially reduces it for C4. Implications for the climate trajectory are taken up in Section 4.10.1; the divergence between this baseline-metric reading and the summer-minimum reading of Section 4.8.1 is returned to in Section 5.1.

### Equilibrium Wetness Index and Vegetation Cross-Validation

MSL5 (Section 4.8.4; defined Section 3.7.5) follows van Willegen et al. (2025), who identified it as the strongest single hydrological correlate of dune-slack vegetation moisture at Newborough. Reconstructing per-well MSL5 for the seventeen van Willegen piezometers reproduces their published mean spring levels to within approximately 11 mm once the fixed dipwell-versus-quadrat datum offset is removed, and between wells MSL5 predicts mean Ellenberg-F — the moisture indicator value (wetness scale 1–12; Hill et al., 1999) — strongly (r = +0.83, n = 18). The validated wells lie in the lake-edge, open-dune and western-residual clusters, van Willegen having placed no quadrats in the forest interior.

The state-space coefficients afford a structurally-derived estimate of the same wetness. Setting the mean monthly change to zero in the fitted model (Section 3.4) and inverting the head-dependent drainage term yields an equilibrium water-table level, h\_eq = (β₁P̄ − β₂PET̄)/β₃ − d (d = 3.7 m), from each well's coefficients and the long-term annual-mean climate. Tested directly against Ellenberg-F this equilibrium index performs as well as observed MSL5: the two are statistically indistinguishable as vegetation predictors (Figure 45; Table 19), their correlation confidence intervals overlapping almost entirely and a Williams test for the difference between the two dependent correlations returning p = 0.81. The equivalence holds not only in aggregate but well by well: binned by prediction accuracy, the two metrics distribute similarly across match bands, the equilibrium index in fact placing marginally more wells in the closest band (Table 20). That coefficients calibrated purely on month-to-month dynamics predict vegetation moisture as well as the direct spring-level metric is the substantive result, subject to the caveat that eighteen wells give limited statistical power, so this is equivalence within the data rather than proven identity. Because the equilibrium index is computed from the coefficients and climate alone, this comparison is independent of MSL5 — each metric is regressed on Ellenberg-F separately, so the equivalence tests two independent predictors rather than restating the spring-level metric. No single state-space coefficient predicts Ellenberg-F nearly as well (best |r| ≈ 0.5, versus 0.81 for the equilibrium index), the vegetation signal residing in the equilibrium level rather than any individual rate or storage term.

### <span id="anchor-36"></span>Spatial Coefficient Structure

The R² surface (Figure 47a), derived from per-well SSM fits at each well's optimal drainage datum, shows the model explains 70–85% of monthly water table variance across the central and northern open dune, declining toward the forest (C4 and C5 mean 0.68) and some peripheral south-eastern wells. Since the R² gain from per-well datum optimization is negligible (Figure 46b), these values also represent the effective fit quality under the standard pipeline datum. The spatial pattern is consistent with the SSM performing best where the climate signal is strongest and least attenuated by canopy or boundary effects.

The three coefficient surfaces (Figure 47b–d) reveal a coherent spatial story about how rainfall enters, is consumed, and drains from the aquifer. Recharge sensitivity (β₁) is highest in the north-east around the C1 Lake Edge wells closest to Llyn Rhos-Ddu (&gt;5 mm/mm), declines across the open dune (3–5 mm/mm), and drops to 2–3 mm/mm west of the forest boundary. Atmospheric draw (β₂) shows a different geography: the highest values occur in two zones — the C4 Main Forest interior, where canopy-driven transpiration amplifies the signal, and a band across the northern open dune adjacent to the ridge — while C1 Lake Edge wells show notably low values (mean 0.62 mm/mm), consistent with a system where head-dependent drainage dominates the loss pathway. C5 Coastal Forest shows moderate β₂ (mean 1.21), lower than C4 despite also carrying pine canopy, possibly reflecting coastal moderation of atmospheric demand. Drainage rate (β₃, logarithmic scale) shows the strongest spatial contrast of any coefficient: C1 and northern C2 wells drain fastest (&gt;8% of displacement per month), the forest interior shows the lowest rates in the network (C4 mean 1.6%, with individual wells below 1%), and C5 falls between the two (mean 4.6%). One C4 well (CEH14) records a negative β₃ (−1.6%), where the displacement formulation does not adequately capture the boundary condition at the ridge flank.

Within the C3 Western Residual cluster, the coefficient surfaces resolve into a coherent internal gradient aligned with the south-west to north-east axis — the same axis as distance inland from the eroding south-west coast. Projecting the 21 C3 well positions onto this axis, recharge sensitivity β₁ increases inland (r = +0.76, p &lt; 0.001), drainage rate β₃ increases inland (r = +0.58, p = 0.009), and WTF-derived specific yield declines inland, from approximately 0.41 at the south-west margin to 0.25 at the north-east (r = −0.83, p &lt; 0.001). Because the SSM's β₁ is recharge divided by specific yield, the β₁ and β₃ gradients are both consistent with a single underlying control — a spatial trend in near-surface specific yield — rather than a spatially varying recharge input. Lower specific yield at the inland margin produces both a larger water-table rise per unit recharge and a faster drainage response per unit head. The mechanistic interpretation of this gradient is developed in Section 5.6.1.

The combined picture is of an aquifer whose eastern margin fills quickly, loses water quickly, and resets each season, while its western and forested interior fills slowly, holds water against evaporative demand, and drains sluggishly — the "shallow pan" versus "deep sponge" contrast identified at cluster level in Section 4.2.

### <span id="anchor-37"></span>**Storage–Drainage Index and Forest Drawdown Propagation**

The drainage decay half-life t½ = ln(2)/β₃ (Figure 48; Section 3.4.6) maps the time for excess groundwater storage above the drainage datum to drain to half its initial value at each reference well. It is derived from the SSM drainage coefficient β₃ alone, without requiring an independent storage estimate. The eastern open dune shows the shortest half-lives in the network: C1 Lake Edge t½ = 5.2-8.0 months (mean 6.9 months), meaning excess storage is largely dissipated within a single season; C2 Dune t½ = 6.2-12.6 months (mean 9.9 months). The western dune shows intermediate values: C3 Western Residual t½ = 6.2-22.3 months (mean 13.8 months), with the wide range reflecting the hydraulic heterogeneity of the western sand body. The forest clusters show markedly longer half-lives: C5 Coastal Forest t½ = 12.1-18.6 months (mean 15.4 months), comparable to C3 despite carrying pine canopy, reinforcing the elevation and substrate control identified in Section 4.9.5; and C4 Main Forest t½ = 16.1-88.1 months (mean 39.8 months), the longest half-lives in the network. At the upper end of the C4 range, excess groundwater storage persists for over seven years before draining to half its initial level. The pronounced C4 hotspot on the elevated plateau, where β₃ is lowest, reflects the progressive thinning of the sandy aquifer over the underlying bedrock ridge (Section 2); its detailed geometry beneath C4 remains unconstrained without coring. The wide within-C4 range (16-88 months) is itself a finding, indicating that the forest block is hydraulically non-uniform. CEH14 (negative β₃) and CEH13 (near-zero β₃) are excluded as ridge-flank wells where the displacement formulation does not apply.

The model benchmarking diagnostic (Section 4.4; Figure 16) complements the half-life map. The iterative NSE improvement from SSM over TLM (ΔNSE) correlates positively with β₃ (r = 0.68, p &lt; 0.001) and negatively with β₂ (r = -0.67, p &lt; 0.001). The TLM fails most severely at C1 Lake Edge (median ΔNSE = +1.29, median TLM NSE = -0.57), where head-dependent drainage to the lake is strongest and a fixed drainage rate cannot capture the large seasonal variation in discharge. Conversely, the TLM performs relatively well at C4 Main Forest (median ΔNSE = +0.23, median TLM NSE = +0.42), where β₃ is so weak that misspecifying the drainage term has little impact on overall model performance. ΔNSE therefore identifies where drainage memory is indispensable - the eastern, lake-proximal aquifer - and where atmospheric draw dominates the water balance regardless of drainage formulation - the forest interior.

**When t½ and ΔNSE are plotted together with point size scaled by Sy (Figure 50), the five cluster centroids occupy distinct regions of the diagnostic space, though the separation is moderate and individual wells overlap appreciably within it. C1 Lake Edge (short t½, high ΔNSE, low Sy) represents a shallow, fast-cycling aquifer over flat clay/till with a short, highly head-dependent drainage path to the lake. C4 Main Forest (long t½, low ΔNSE, intermediate Sy) represents a sluggish system in which β₃ is too weak for its misspecification **to matter - consistent with drainage impeded by the irregular bedrock base the forest is known to overlie. C5 Coastal Forest is displaced from C4 in t½ and storage despite sharing the same pine canopy - occupying intermediate t½ and higher Sy, consistent with deeper, more uniform sand at its lower elevation; its higher ΔNSE marks where the state-space model more clearly outperforms the linear benchmark. C2 and C3 span the middle ground, with C3's longer t½ and higher Sy reflecting the deeper western sand body. The separation of clusters in this independently derived diagnostic space provides a model-based characterization of aquifer architecture that is consistent with, but independent of, the Ward's clustering applied to the raw hydrograph data. This is the per-well quantification of the 'shallow pan' versus 'deep sponge' contrast identified at cluster level in Section 4.2.**

The spatial reach of forest management perturbations is shown directly by the drawdown propagation analysis (Figure 49), which models the steady-state head perturbation from forest canopy interception as an exponential decay with flow-weighted cost-distance from the forest boundary (λ = 225 m). Estimated drawdown exceeds 50 mm only within ~100 m of the forest edge, decays to 24 mm at CEH23 and 8 mm at CEH6, and is negligible (≤1 mm) at all C1 and eastern C2 wells. These are steady-state values; the timescale to reach equilibrium is governed by the drainage decay half-life values mapped in Figure 48, ranging from months in the eastern dune (t½ approximately 7-14 months) to years in the forest interior (C4 mean t½ approximately 40 months)* *(Section 5.4.3). Forest management perturbations are therefore structurally confined to the forest zone and its immediate western margin; they do not propagate to the eastern slacks where the ecological need is greatest.

### Coastal-Margin Processes and the Easting Gradient

The forest drawdown propagation map (Figure 49) raises a natural comparison: how does the spatial reach of canopy-driven drawdown compare with the coastal-margin processes acting on the same aquifer? Two such processes operate at the western boundary, and because they act in opposing directions, neither can be assessed in isolation. Shoreline retreat lowers the ground surface relative to the water table, raising the displacement head and enhancing seaward drainage — the same mechanism, in reverse sign of intent, as the dune scraping of Section 4.5. Sea-level rise acts oppositely, raising the water table at the coastal boundary. Mapped as companion fields on the same hillshade base as the forest drawdown figure, constructed as set out in Section 3.4.5, these processes are shown in Figure 51 (erosion drawdown from a single shoreline-retreat event), Figure 52 (the sea-level-rise head response as a finite-window transient), and their cell-by-cell difference in Figure 53.

The net map (Figure 53) shows that the two processes do not cancel but partition spatially. A narrow erosion-dominated band lies along the dune-toe margin, coincident with the western Coastal-Forest (C5) and western Western-Residual (C3) wells; this is flanked by a sea-level-rise–dominated rise at the immediate shore and near-neutral conditions across the dune interior. The erosion and sea-level-rise contributions are of comparable magnitude over a matched five-year window, so the western-margin signal is the residual of two opposing processes rather than the action of either alone.

This spatial partition provides a physical interpretation of the easting × time covariate carried in the clearfell BACI analysis (Section 4.6.3). That covariate absorbs a west-to-east deepening gradient that the BACI design cannot otherwise separate from the felling signal. The forest-free coastal-retreat gradient fitted in the corroboration analysis predicts a western-margin drawdown of −11.1 mm yr⁻¹, which is statistically consistent with the −16.8 ± 5.0 mm yr⁻¹ absorbed by the easting × time term at the Forest–Impact tier (z = −1.16; Section 4.6, Table 8). A single linear easting term is therefore a defensible first-order proxy for the western-margin coastal signal, even though that signal in fact combines processes of opposing sign and differing inland reach: erosion acting at the dune toe, sea-level rise at the shore. The covariate captures the net west-to-east gradient but does not decompose it into its constituent processes; the interpretive consequences for the clearfell attribution are taken up in Section 5.5.

These three figures are single-mechanism illustrative constructions on the eroding Caernarfon Bay shore only; they combine a single storm-magnitude retreat event with gradual sea-level rise, and depend on assumed retreat-rate, hydraulic-diffusivity, datum, and window parameters. They illustrate the relative spatial reach of the two processes, not a deterministic forecast or a closed water balance.

The dune scraping of Section 4.5 can be placed on the same spatial footing as the forest and coastal fields. Figure 54 renders the April 2015 CEH36 scrape as a steady-state drain, with edge magnitude anchored to the measured CEH36 response (129 mm) and the same leaky-aquifer decay length as the forest field (λ = 225 m). On this anchoring the unsurveyed excavation depth is recovered as an inferred ≈ 0.42 m. Modelled drawdown exceeds 50 mm only within ~100 m of the excavation and decays to a few millimetres by the nearest clearfell-tier wells. As with the forest field these are steady-state values: the equilibration time at these distances is years to decades (Section 5.4.3), which is why the uphill propagation is not resolved as a distance-decay signal within the 31-month BACI window (Section 4.5).

Figure 54 combines the two non-felling drawdown drivers acting on the clearfell compartment — the 2015 scrape and a Storm-Brendan-scale retreat event — as a single head-loss field on the pre-fell baseline. The combined drawdown reaches ≈ 135 mm at the scrape and tapers through the clearfell compartment, with the coastal-erosion term adding a south-western band; the felling step estimated by the BACI ANCOVA (Section 4.6) is the residual once both are accounted for. This is the spatial form of the easting × time covariate of Section 4.6.3: the covariate absorbs the net west-to-east gradient that this figure decomposes into the scrape drain and the coastal retreat.

### <span id="anchor-38"></span> Per-Well Forest Zone Analysis

The spatial coefficient atlas (Figure 47b–d) shows that the C4 and C5 forest wells span nearly the full site-wide range of β₂ values despite sharing the same Corsican pine canopy. To test whether this within-forest variation tracks topographic position (a proxy for the uncored substrate) or canopy differences, per-well SSM coefficients for the 14 forest zone wells (9 in C4, 5 in C5) were regressed against elevation, distance from the ridge and Easting (Section 3.4.4).

Elevation is overwhelmingly the dominant spatial predictor (Table 18). The atmospheric draw coefficient β₂ shows the strongest relationship (r = 0.981, p &lt; 0.001; R² = 0.963), with higher-elevation wells on the ridge returning systematically higher β₂ values. Adding distance from the ridge contributes virtually no additional explanatory power (R² = 0.963). The drainage coefficient β₃ shows a significant but weaker elevation dependence (r = −0.787, p &lt; 0.001; R² = 0.619), with distance from the ridge adding meaningful information (combined R² = 0.739): wells further from the ridge and at lower elevation drain faster. The recharge coefficient β₁ shows no significant relationship with elevation (r = 0.258, p = 0.374); Easting is the best single predictor (r = 0.620, p = 0.018) but explains only 38% of variance. This elevation–drainage relationship is visible directly in the storage–drainage index map (Figure 48), where C4 wells show τ values up to 34 months compared with 5–13 months at C5.

C4 and C5 form two clearly distinct groups rather than a continuum. They separate almost entirely on the β₂ axis: C4 wells cluster at β₂ = 2.2–3.7 while C5 wells sit at β₂ = 0.9–1.5, with zero overlap. Mean elevations differ significantly (C4: 10.7 m, C5: 5.7 m; p &lt; 0.001), with a clean gap between 6.5 m and 8.7 m containing no wells. This corresponds to the transition from the elevated ridge and plateau terrain — where a deep unsaturated zone allows strong atmospheric draw and weak drainage — to the low-lying coastal fringe, where the shallow water table limits atmospheric influence and enhances lateral drainage. Both zones carry Corsican pine canopy, so the separation tracks elevation and water-table depth rather than canopy: C4 sits on the flank of the bedrock ridge (which outcrops within the forest), C5 on the lower coastal sand, with the depth and extent of rock beneath each unconstrained without coring.

Two individual wells merit comment. CEH14, at the highest elevation in the forest zone (14.4 m), has the highest β₂ in the network (****3.723****) — entirely consistent with the elevation regression rather than being an outlier. Its negative β₃ (****−0.016****) and low R² (0.568) suggest that at this ridge-proximal position, lateral recharge from the bedrock ridge at high head overwhelms the drainage signal, reversing the expected relationship between displacement and head change. NW10, the broadleaf block well, has the highest β₁ in the forest zone (****3.481****, ****2.4 SD**** above the C4 mean) and the highest β₃ among C4 wells (****0.043****). Its elevated recharge sensitivity likely reflects the deciduous canopy intercepting less winter rainfall than the surrounding Corsican pine (15% vs 24% interception), though this cannot be confirmed with a single well.

### Mean Water Table Surface and Groundwater Flow Direction

The mean annual water table surface was constructed by IDW interpolation of per-well mean maOD values (2005–2026) to a 50 m grid on the British National Grid, masked to the rectangular sea-boundary extent (Figure 56; Section 3.8.4). No DEM-height mask is applied because the water table is a continuous surface independent of the overlying topography. The highest mean water levels (~14 m AOD) occur in the north-western forest zone adjacent to the bedrock ridge, declining to 7–9 m AOD across the central dune and reaching 2–3 m AOD at the southern and eastern coastal margins. Contours run approximately NE–SW across the site, consistent with the topographic configuration. normalized Darcy flow direction vectors, computed as Q = −K·b·∇h (Bear, 1972; Freeze and Cherry, 1979) from the head gradient field using K = 6.0 m day⁻¹ (Betson et al., 2002), show a radial pattern diverging from the north-western high point: predominantly southward and south-westward in the western half of the site, and south-eastward in the eastern half. This pattern is broadly consistent with the DEM-derived flow network (blue lines in Figure 56; Conrad et al., 2015; Natural Resources Wales LiDAR DTM, 2023). The flow vectors are reported as directional context for the interpolated head field rather than as output of a calibrated continuous-flow model.

The SSM water balance residual field (Figure 57; Section 3.8.4) quantifies the degree to which the three-term SSM closes the long-term water balance at each well. The 2005→2025 driver-change map

The 2005→2025 driver-change map (Figure 60) re-bases the parametric net-state construction to the study record, differencing each modelled driver field between its 2005 and 2025 states so that the map shows what the drivers imply should have changed over the monitoring window rather than the accumulated state since the forest was planted. Because the standing pine canopy is at equilibrium at both endpoints, its drawdown cancels in the difference and the mature forest interior returns to near zero — the check that the map is an epoch difference and not a re-drawing of Figure 59. Five fields change over the window and are summed: the clearfell gain, computed as in the net-state map from the removal of the interception deficit over the felled compartment; the dune-scrape response, with all cuts within the window contributing; the five-year chronic coastal drawdown; sea-level-rise head gain over the same span; and a broadleaf-restock field new to this map. The broadleaf-restock block, felled in 1993 and restocked in 1998, was already near closed canopy at the 2005 baseline and reaches full canopy by 2025; only the increment of interception deficit that develops over 2005→2025 enters the map, computed on the same exponential-decay construction as the forest field but scaled by the broadleaf interception fraction (0.15, against 0.24 for pine; Komatsu et al., 2011) and by the canopy-establishment increment, giving of order 56 mm of added drawdown at source. It appears as a drawdown patch of the opposite sign to the clearfell gain — the atmospheric-draw signature of a canopy returning where the clearfell removed one. The coastal-retreat field is expressed as a five-year chronic drawdown, five years of the observed coast-edge decline rate (from the live Script 25 forest-free fit, δ₀ = 29.0 mm yr⁻¹), giving h₀ = 5 × δ₀ ≈ 145 mm at the dune toe on the same five-year horizon as the sea-level-rise field — the strongest of the five fields at the western coastal margin, and rate-independent by construction. As a first-order superposition of single-mechanism fields the map is illustrative and not a closed water balance; the broadleaf and coastal fields in particular are modelled quantities the dipwell record cannot fully resolve, and are presented as indicative. The map is the modelled companion to the observed climate-removed trend map (Figure 63, Section 3.8) over the same 2005→2025 window: the two are built from different data by different methods and are expected to agree in broad direction rather than in detail, so their agreement where it occurs supports the attribution and their divergence flags a driver the model does not capture.

<span id="anchor-40"></span>**Observed network change **

Figure 61 presents the conventional two-window reading of observed network change — validated on its own terms (Section 5.7.5) but, as set out below, limited in what a two-window difference can establish about absolute change.

The MSL5 change map (Figure 61; method: Section 3.8.5) compares window end 2017 (springs 2013–2017, pre-clearfell, network mean −492 mm below ground) with window end 2023 (springs 2019–2023, network mean −589 mm). The raw 2023-minus-2017 difference exceeds the ±25 mm threshold at 56 of 59 wells, every one of them deepening; no well shallows. The map shows a broadly deeper aquifer in the current window, the deepening intensifying from the eastern dune interior toward the south-western coastal margin. Because the figure differences two five-year windows, every time-invariant driver cancels — in particular the constant drawdown of the 60-year standing forest, which is present in both windows and contributes nothing to the difference — so the surviving pattern reflects only drivers that progressed across the study period, principally the coastal influence developed in Section 4.8.2; it is not, and cannot be, the standing-canopy drawdown shown parametrically in Figure 59.

The eastern C1/C2 dune wells deepen least (CEH25 −13, L7 −17, D7 −25 mm), several falling within the ±25 mm near-neutral band. The most strongly negative zone is the south-western coastal margin: CEH22 (−229 mm), CEH21 (−194 mm), CEH18 (−158 mm), and the C5 coastal members CEH19, CEH17, NW9 (−150 to −153 mm). These wells are most directly exposed to the coastal-retreat gradient documented in Section 4.8.2, and their deepening is consistent with the BACI easting × time absorption of Section 4.6.3.

The C4 Forest Control wells deepen by −130 mm (cluster mean), more than the −97 mm site-wide average. As noted, the constant drawdown of the 60-year standing forest cancels in a window-to-window difference, and these are unfelled controls, so this excess is neither a standing-canopy nor a clearfell effect; it is consistent with the forested clusters’ stronger differential response to climate, whereby their higher β₂ atmospheric draw and 24% canopy interception convert the between-window climate anomaly into a larger water-level change than the open dune. Because the map applies no climate correction, this differential response is expressed in full: the forest-shaped deepening is therefore a climate-response artefact of the uncorrected differencing, not an active forest signal.

These results are robust within the two-window method: the τ-window and spring-gap checks of Section 5.7.5 confirm the 2017→2023 deepening is not an artefact of window length or spring-record gaps. Their limitation is comparative rather than internal. Because the slow-draining aquifer carries several years of memory, the absolute magnitude of a two-window difference — and at some wells its sign — depends on which two windows are compared, even between windows matched for antecedent rainfall; the matched-window sensitivity analysis of Section 5.7.5 yields site-mean changes spanning −0.14 to +0.22 m and changing sign across all admissible window pairs (19 of 66 falling, 47 rising) — the −97 mm comparison being one interior point and the wet-2024 windows the +0.22 m extreme — which is why absolute site-wide change is not rested on this two-window method (Script 34, Figure for §5.7.5). The −97 mm site-wide figure is therefore specific to the 2017/2023 comparison, not a robust estimate of secular change. The two figures that follow remove this dependence: Figure 62 maps the differential movement with the common climate signal removed, and Figure 64 the climate-response envelope — the latter quantifying directly the forested clusters’ larger climate response invoked above.

<span id="anchor-41"></span>Secular differential movement

Figure 62 shows the secular differential movement of the spring water table across Newborough Warren: with the common climate signal removed, the high, slow-draining forest mound holds its position while the fast-draining lake-edge and coastal margins decline relative to the site as a whole — a pattern governed by the aquifer’s recession geometry rather than by management intervention. The quantity mapped is, for each well, the linear trend through its spring anomaly — the well’s mean-spring level minus the site-mean spring level in the same year — over 2011–2025. Subtracting the site mean each year removes the shared climate forcing that dominates the raw record, so what remains is the rate at which each well is gaining or losing ground against the rest of the Warren. Because it is built from a per-well trend rather than a two-window difference, the map is insensitive to the window-selection problem of Figure 61; it correlates above 0.98 with the two-window pattern but does not inherit its fragility.

The differential signal is spatially coherent but statistically uneven. Of the 72 wells mapped over 2011–2025, eight reach individual significance under an AR(1)-corrected trend test cross-checked by a moving-block bootstrap; these are predominantly the lake-edge and coastal wells that are losing ground. The forest-interior rise is coherent across neighbouring wells but individually sub-significant — each forest well is rising slowly against a noisy background — and it firms up over the longer 2005–2025 window (13 of 75 wells significant), consistent with a slow, persistent process rather than a recent step. Critically, the same holding-up behaviour appears in the C5 coastal-forest wells only where they sit on the high ground, not by virtue of their canopy: the inland C4 forest mound holds while C5 wells nearer the eroding coast decline with their open-dune neighbours. The pattern is therefore one of position within the recession geometry, not of forest cover, and it is not attributable to the felling intervention.

*****Absolute movement with the climate signal removed *****

Figure 62 measures each well against the network mean, and over a wet-spring-lifted window that referencing can invert the plain reading: a well that is holding steady while the site mean rises appears to decline, and the slow-draining forest reads as *rising* not because its water table is recovering but because it is falling more slowly than the rest. Figure 63 removes that ambiguity by mapping the absolute secular trend at each well — the shared climate signal taken out by regression against an external climatic-water-balance index, but with no re-referencing to the network mean, so brown marks ground where the water table is genuinely falling and blue where it is genuinely holding or rising. The construction and window are described in Section 3.8.5; the trend is fitted over 2005–2025, the longer window being primary here because the shorter window is dominated by wells whose records begin only after 2011.

The absolute map tells a quieter and more spatially specific story than the differential one. Once the weather is removed, most of the Warren is close to holding its position — the lake-edge, dune and main-forest clusters all sit within a few millimetres per year of zero (C1 +2.1, C2 +4.6, C4 +2.7 mm yr⁻¹) — and the genuine, weather-independent losses are confined to the western and coastal margins. The coastal-forest cluster carries much the most negative trend (C5 ≈ −11.6 mm yr⁻¹), and the western residual cluster is mildly negative (C3 ≈ −3.4 mm yr⁻¹); the strongest individual driers are the western and coastal wells (CEH3 −28, NW9 −23, CEH4 −19 mm yr⁻¹). This is the same coastal-margin decline that Figure 62 identifies as the fastest-losing part of the network, but expressed in absolute terms: the forest interior is not recovering, it is simply stable, and the drying that matters is at the coast. The two maps are complementary — Figure 62 answers "which part of the Warren is gaining or losing ground against the rest," Figure 63 answers "where is the water table genuinely rising or falling" — and their agreement on the coastal margin, reached from different constructions, strengthens the reading that coastal retreat is the dominant secular driver.

<span id="anchor-42"></span>The climate-response envelope

Where Figure 62 measures slow secular change, Figure 64 measures climate sensitivity: how far each part of the network moves between the driest and wettest springs the record contains, and Figure 65 how close the dry-year water table already lies to ecological thresholds. The amplification panel (Figure 64) addresses the first. Between its antecedent-dry and antecedent-wet spring extremes the site-mean water table swings approximately 0.75 m; expressing each well’s swing as a ratio to that network-mean swing, with the common-mode removed, gives a relative amplification field independent of window choice, because it compares true end-members rather than adjacent windows. The forest interior amplifies the shared swing to about 1.7×, while the lake edge damps it to about 0.6×, with the open dune close to unity; the forest is thus the most climate-volatile zone on the site and the lake margin the most stable. This is the same forest behaviour that inflates the C4 deepening in Figure 61, here measured directly rather than as an artefact of differencing. The amplification correlates most strongly with the atmospheric-draw coefficient β₂ (r ≈ +0.8), which survives control for elevation and exclusion of the forest clusters; β₂ is best read as a marker of a deep, slow-recession store rather than a literal evaporation rate, since in C4 it is inflated by the known β₂–β₃ degeneracy.

The second panel (Figure 65) maps the dry-year spring water-table depth — the mean spring level across the dry extreme years (2011, 2012, 2019). Spring is used in preference to the summer minimum because the spring record is near-complete whereas summer-minimum readings are sparse; the choice is deliberately conservative, since spring is the seasonal high and therefore the shallowest the water table reaches in those years. On this panel the Curreli et al. (2013) thresholds are overlaid not as summer-minimum crossings but to mark where even this spring high has already fallen below wet-slack (SD15b, 0.61 m) and dry-slack (SD16, 0.98 m) viability. Because the summer minimum lies deeper still, the contoured zones are a strict lower bound on the area below ecological viability: where the forest interior and western dune sit beyond SD16 even in spring, the water table is below dry-slack viability for the whole of those dry years. Together the two panels separate the two questions that the single two-window map of Figure 61 conflated — how sensitive each area is to climate, and how close it already sits to ecological thresholds in dry years — neither of which depends on an arbitrary choice of comparison window.

<span id="anchor-43"></span>Summary of pattern

Figure 58 predicts a large net loss across the forest interior under the parametric annual-mean drivers, and the 2017/2023 window comparison (Figure 61) shows a broadly deeper aquifer with the largest declines at the south-western coastal margin; but the absolute site-wide deepening is specific to that window pair (Section 5.7.5). Figure 62 confirms — with the common climate signal removed — that the coastal-margin decline is the dominant secular movement, and that the forest interior holds its position relative to the rest of the Warren. The per-cluster rates quantify this split: C4 Main Forest rises at a cluster mean of +14.9 mm yr*⁻¹* relative to the site (range +8.4 to +20.5 mm yr*⁻¹* across the nine reference wells; none individually significant after AR(1) correction); C1 Lake Edge declines at −8.0 mm yr*⁻¹* (CEH11 significant, p = 0.027); C5 Coastal Forest at −6.8 mm yr*⁻¹* (NW9 significant, p = 0.039); and C2 Dune is approximately neutral (−0.1 mm yr*⁻¹*). CEH22, at the south-western coastal margin, is the most rapidly declining well in the network at −26.5 mm yr*⁻¹* (p &lt; 0.001). The site-mean spring trend over 2011–2025 is +19.7 mm yr*⁻¹* (AR-corrected p = 0.275; bootstrap CI −3.1 to +42.8 mm yr*⁻¹*), reflecting the influence of the exceptionally wet springs of 2021 and 2024 on a panel dominated by slowly draining wells; Figure 62 therefore shows the forest rising *relative to the network*, not in absolute recovery.

Figure 64 shows the forest amplifying the dry-to-wet climate swing more strongly than any other zone (C4 mean 1.72×, range 1.38–2.35×; C1 lake edge 0.61×; open dune near unity). The amplification correlates positively with β₂ (r ≈ +0.74) and negatively with β₃ (r = −0.45, p &lt; 0.001), consistent with slower-draining wells retaining wet-year recharge longer and therefore exhibiting larger spring swings — the same forest behaviour that inflates the C4 deepening in Figure 61, here measured directly rather than as an artefact of differencing.

The felled well FE2 (inside the December 2017 clearfell boundary) registers a spring differential trend of +6.7 mm yr*⁻¹* and a co-temporal amplification of 1.23× (Figure 64). Both values lie notably below the unfelled C4 interior mean (+14.9 mm yr*⁻¹*; 1.72×). FE2's wet-year spring depth (−1.06 m) rises less than the surrounding unfelled C4 reference wells (mean wet-year depth −0.56 m), and its swing (640 mm) is substantially smaller than the C4 mean (1,120 mm). This is physically consistent with canopy removal: the interception-dependent recharge suppression that drives the high wet-year amplification of the unfelled interior is absent at FE2. Concurrently, the summer drought floor at FE2 (−1.79 m in dry years) is slightly deeper than the C4 reference mean (−1.68 m), consistent with greater direct evaporation from exposed soil. FE1, approximately 20 m outside the felled boundary, shows an intermediate response (+13.3 mm yr*⁻¹*; 1.53×). These observations are consistent with the clearfell interpretation of Section 4.6: felling attenuates the wet-year amplification that drives the apparent C4 differential rise, while not improving the summer minimum.

The reconciliation of the parametric prediction with the observed change — the window-sensitivity of the absolute deepening, the differential movement of Figure 62, and the climate amplification of Figure 64 — is taken up in Section 5.7.5.

#### Driver validation and comparative footing

The differential pattern above identifies where the network is moving, but not how much of that movement each modelled driver accounts for. Two diagnostics address this, and their shared conclusion is cautious: the network can bound the drivers' relative footing, but cannot independently confirm the coastal-retreat amplitude from the monitoring record alone.

A per-driver scale-factor regression (Section 3.8.5) fits the climate-corrected per-well change against the modelled coastal and clearfell fields as separate regressors, together with a free spatially-uniform intercept that absorbs the site-wide background decline present even at unfelled control wells. Across every window examined, the fitted scale factors are statistically indistinguishable from both zero and unity: the coastal factor is 0.53 (95% CI −0.12 to 1.18) over the full 2005–2025 record and 2.05 (−0.61 to 4.71) over the pre-intervention 2006–2012 window, while the clearfell factor over the post-clearfell 2018–2025 window is 3.62 (−0.77 to 8.00). The uniform intercept, by contrast, is consistently well-determined and large — a decline of 559 mm over 2006–2012 (95% CI −606 to −512) — indicating that the dominant secular movement in the network is common-mode rather than spatially structured by any single modelled driver. The regression explains only a modest fraction of the between-well variance (R² ≤ 0.30 in every window).

This is a bounded null rather than a refutation. The coastal field cannot be independently confirmed here not because coastal influence is spatially narrow — the modelled reach (L ≈ 894 m) extends across much of the western site — but because the signal cannot be statistically isolated: its spatial signature is collinear with easting (r ≈ −0.48) and with the underlying clay-dip substrate gradient, the per-well residual scatter (of order ±150–200 mm) exceeds the driver amplitudes being tested, and only around fifteen of the wells entering the fit carry a modelled coastal amplitude large enough to constrain the regression at all. The coastal shape (δ₀, L) is moreover fitted from this same network via the gradient analysis of Section 4.9.4, so the scale-factor test is in part self-confirming; the one genuinely independent check is an expanding-window trajectory of the implied coastal decline rate, which is itself too contaminated by the 2006–2012 drought signal to be conclusive. The regression therefore neither confirms nor refutes the coastal-retreat interpretation of Section 4.9; it establishes that the present network lacks the spatial leverage to resolve it, and that background common-mode decline must not be attributed to any driver by default.

A complementary analysis (Section 3.8.5) places the drivers on a common footing over the 2005–2025 horizon, using observed BACI anchors where available (clearfell +119.6 mm; on-site scraping +129.4 mm) and modelled fields elsewhere. Expressed as a water-table depth change, the coastal driver is the largest single term: a lowering of up to approximately 145 mm at the dune toe, tapering inland over a reach of roughly 900 m (L ≈ 894 m); its depth-against-distance profile, alongside those of the forest and scraping drivers, is shown in Figure 69. Dune scraping produces a net site-wide loss because its off-site drainage cone draws down the water table across the area surrounding each cut by more than the on-site slack rise recovers, so the two do not balance; integrated across the site, the net scraping effect is of order 28% of the coastal drawdown in magnitude — a real secondary contributor to decline, but well short of the coastal term and not a primary driver in its own right. Sea-level rise, evaluated on the near-term five-year projection rather than the twenty-year horizon of the other terms, is indicative only and small by comparison.

Both diagnostics are first-order and do not close a water budget; the modelled coastal drawdown remains a projection anchored on the observed δ₀ and unconfirmed spatially by the network. Taken together they indicate coastal dominance in the relative footing of the drivers while placing an explicit bound on how far the monitoring record can substantiate the coastal amplitude itself. ## <span id="anchor-44"></span>**Scenario Analysis**

The per-well SSM coefficients derived in Section 4.9 were used to project equilibrium water table responses under prescribed climate and forest management perturbations, following the framework of Section 3.8. Six scenarios were evaluated: baseline; UKCP18 2050s and 2080s climate trajectories; full clearfell; 50% thinning; and broadleaf conversion. Scenario difference maps were computed as Δh = h(scenario) − h(baseline) for each well, season and cluster. The full per-scenario, per-season, per-cluster Δh output is available through the Newborough Warren Hydrogeological Modelling Suite (Hollingham, 2026b), an interactive web-based scenario viewer that renders the per-well Δh field across the site using IDW interpolation, with a complementary depth-below-surface view masked to hydrologically meaningful slack positions and overlaid with the Curreli et al. (2013) SD15b/SD16 ecological thresholds. The cluster-level results are summarized below.

### <span id="anchor-45"></span>Climate Scenario Projections

Climate scenario responses are spatially coherent and physically consistent with the per-cluster β coefficient structure. Under UKCP18 2050s forcing, the site-wide mean annual Δh is −0.018 m/month, with cluster-level annual responses ranging from −0.026 m/month at C4 (Main Forest) to −0.009 m/month at C1 (Lake Edge). Under UKCP18 2080s forcing, the site-wide mean annual response deepens to −0.033 m/month, with a maximum of −0.047 m/month at C4 and a minimum of −0.017 m/month at C1. Both UKCP18 scenarios show the forested clusters carrying the largest annual declines, driven by the combination of high β₂ (atmospheric draw sensitivity) and reduced net recharge under interception. The annual response conceals a pronounced seasonal asymmetry. Under both trajectories, winter Δh is positive at all clusters (site-wide mean +0.028 m/month at 2050s and +0.056 m/month at 2080s) as the increased winter rainfall under RCP8.5 Wales outpaces the concurrent increase in winter PET. Summer Δh is strongly negative and carries the annual signal: site-wide means of −0.064 m/month at 2050s and −0.121 m/month at 2080s, reaching −0.134 m/month at C2 under the 2080s trajectory. This summer-dominated signature reflects the same physical mechanism established in the observed record (Section 4.8.2): at Newborough, hydrological deterioration under climate change operates through intensified summer atmospheric demand rather than declining winter recharge.

The same projection framework, applied to the five-year mean spring water level (MSL5; Section 4.8.4), gives a markedly smaller response than the summer-minimum metric. The projected MSL5 shift is −12 to −21 mm across the clusters at the 2050s horizon and −21 to −39 mm at the 2080s horizon (Figure 66), against summer Δh of −38 to −70 mm and −71 to −134 mm respectively over the same horizons. The MSL5 response is three to five times smaller than the summer response in every cluster and at both horizons. The contrast is mechanistic, not incidental: MSL5 is computed over the March–May spring window, which receives the projected increase in winter–spring rainfall, partly offsetting the concurrent increase in spring evaporative demand; the summer minimum receives the full projected increase in summer atmospheric demand compounded by the projected reduction in summer rainfall. The spring baseline metric is therefore comparatively insulated against the projected trajectory, while the summer-minimum metric is highly exposed. Expressed against the Curreli et al. (2013) thresholds, the projected MSL5 shifts do not move any cluster across a threshold under either horizon — C1 and C2 remain within the SD15b wet-slack regime, C4 remains in the SD16 dry-slack regime, and C5 remains below SD16 — whereas the parallel summer-minimum deterioration substantially worsens threshold exceedance in the open-dune clusters whose summer minima already approach the dry-slack threshold (Section 4.8.1). The implication for the binding ecological constraint is taken up in Section 5.2.5.

### <span id="anchor-46"></span>Forest Management Scenarios

Forest management scenarios produce responses at the two forested clusters (C4 and C5), with no effect at C1–C3. Full clearfell is modelled by setting canopy interception to 0% and adjusting β₂ at C4 and C5 wells. Fifty percent thinning halves interception from 24% to 12% with proportional β₂ adjustment. Broadleaf conversion replaces the Corsican pine canopy interception fraction of 24% (Freeman, 2008) with an annual-mean deciduous interception of 15% (Komatsu et al., 2011), and applies a seasonally varying β₂ adjustment to reflect deciduous phenology: β₂ follows the winter and summer broadleaf coefficients derived in Section 3.8, held near baseline during the leafless dormant season (November–March) and raised during the growing season to reflect the comparable transpiration rates of mature deciduous and Corsican pine canopy in full leaf.

Under full clearfell with the BACI-corrected β₂ multiplier (Section 3.8), the framework predicts a modest annual water table rise at both forest clusters (C4: +0.039 m/month head, equivalent to +8.1 mm w.e./month volumetric; C5: +0.039 m/month head, +10.4 mm w.e./month). The annual response is driven primarily by the winter recharge gain from canopy removal (C4: +0.047 m/month head; C5: +0.045 m/month head). The summer response is positive but smaller at both clusters (C4: +0.031 m/month; C5: +0.032 m/month), reflecting that the winter interception gain exceeds the summer β₂ cost across the year at both substrates. Fifty percent thinning produces a similar patternat reduced magnitude (C4: +0.020 m/month annually, +4.0 mm w.e./month; C5: +0.019 m/month, +5.2 mm w.e./month). These are steady-state predictions from the SSM coefficient structure; the predicted direction is consistent with the BACI monitoring record, which detects a significant mean monthly recovery at the Impact tier (+120 mm against the Forest control; Section 4.6.3) but no corresponding improvement in summer minima (Section 4.6.4; further interpreted in Section 5.5).

Broadleaf conversion produces a near-neutral annual response at both forest clusters (C4: +0.011 m/month head; C5: +0.013 m/month head), masking a pronounced seasonal asymmetry: winter head-space values are among the largest of any forestry scenario (C4: +0.026 m/month; C5: +0.021 m/month) reflecting the near-zero interception under leafless canopy during the recharge season, but the summer response is slightly negative at C4 (−0.003 m/month) and near-zero at C5 (+0.005 m/month) as the growing-season transpiration penalty under mature deciduous canopy consumes the interception gain. The net annual volumetric effect is −0.7 mm w.e./month at C4 and +1.6 mm w.e./month at C5. The additional winter recharge steepens the local hydraulic gradient and drains away through the highly transmissive sand before the summer minimum is reached (further interpreted in Section 5.5.2).

Site-wide means under forest management are small (annual +0.008 m/month clearfell, +0.004 m/month thinning) because only 14 of 66 reference wells are in the two forest clusters and the framework applies no spatial propagation from the forest zone into other clusters. Broadleaf conversion produces no meaningful site-wide signal.
